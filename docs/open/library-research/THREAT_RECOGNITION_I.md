# Threat Recognition I — Concern, Context, Escalation

**Status:** research / course-design recommendation only  
**Issue:** #104  
**Branch:** `research/issue-104-practical-skills-expansion`  
**Implementation:** out of scope  
**Research date:** 2026-09-17

## Recommendation

**Ship as a bounded scenario-based Argus course, but frame it as threat recognition and reporting rather than threat assessment.**

The useful civilian skill is not “spot a dangerous person.” It is:

- notice behaviour rather than demographic traits;
- distinguish historical/static context from current change;
- notice escalation, accumulation and movement from talk toward action without trying to reconstruct attack tactics;
- identify what is known, inferred and missing;
- select an appropriate reporting or emergency response;
- remain willing to conclude **insufficient information**.

Professional behavioural threat assessment and management (BTAM/TAM) is a multidisciplinary risk-management discipline. Canadian professional guidance expects substantial training, practical experience, scientific knowledge and, where appropriate, structured or validated assessment tools. The Ontario Provincial Police likewise describes its Threat and Behaviour Analysis Team as specially trained in empirically supported risk-assessment tools and concepts. Argus should not simulate that professional role.

The course should instead teach a narrower capability: **recognize when observable behaviour and context justify no special action, factual reporting, or immediate emergency escalation.**

---

# 1. Exact competency boundary

## Completion claim

> **Given authored civilian scenarios, the learner can distinguish ordinary, concerning and urgent situations using observable behaviour, context, change from baseline, pattern accumulation and uncertainty; identify important missing information; and choose an appropriate reporting or emergency escalation response without demographic profiling, amateur diagnosis, tactical analysis or claiming to predict violence.**

This is the strongest defensible completion claim.

Completion does **not** mean the learner:

- is qualified to conduct a behavioural threat assessment;
- can predict who will become violent;
- can assign a professional risk level to a person;
- can diagnose mental illness, dangerousness or violent intent;
- can investigate another person;
- can decide whether police, employers, schools or clinicians should impose a particular intervention;
- has received law-enforcement, security, clinical or threat-assessment certification.

Argus should not award labels such as “threat assessor,” “violence-risk assessor,” “trained profiler,” or equivalent.

# 2. Evidence baseline

## 2.1 Behaviour, not demographic profile

The strongest common principle across FBI, U.S. Secret Service/NTAC and Canadian professional material is that targeted-violence prevention should focus on **behaviour in context**, not a demographic or personality profile.

The Secret Service’s current BTAM guidance explicitly describes behavioural threat assessment as non-profiling: it focuses on an individual’s thinking and behaviour rather than characteristics such as race, ethnicity, age or gender. Earlier Secret Service school-violence research reached the same conclusion: no accurate or useful attacker profile exists.

This matters pedagogically. Argus must never reward suspicion because of:

- race, ethnicity, nationality or immigration status;
- religion or political identity;
- sex, gender identity or sexual orientation;
- age alone;
- clothing, accent, disability or homelessness;
- social awkwardness or being a “loner”;
- a mental-health label by itself;
- lawful hobbies, including ordinary firearms, martial-arts or military-history interests, without relevant behavioural context.

A scenario should be marked wrong if the learner treats a demographic category as evidence of threat.

## 2.2 Threat assessment is not prediction

Modern BTAM guidance is best understood as **risk management under uncertainty**, not a mechanism for predicting rare violent events with certainty.

The U.S. Secret Service’s 2024 law-enforcement guide states that behavioural threat assessment is not prediction; its purpose is to identify and address concerning factors that may be driving a person toward violence. The FBI similarly warns that warning behaviours cannot predict targeted violence. For every attacker with a particular behavioural history, many more people may display similar behaviours and never attack.

This is consistent with the base-rate problem: severe targeted violence is rare, so any broad “warning sign” approach will generate many false positives if treated as a predictive test.

Argus should therefore teach:

> **Concern can justify attention or reporting without justifying a conclusion that violence will occur.**

The learner’s task is to decide what response is proportionate to the information available, not to forecast an outcome.

## 2.3 “Made a threat” and “poses a threat” are not the same question

FBI threat-assessment guidance makes an important distinction:

- some people who make threats ultimately pose a threat;
- some people who pose a threat never make a direct threat;
- many people who make threats do not pose a threat.

The content, context and circumstances surrounding a communication matter. A direct threat should never be ignored, but the existence or absence of an explicit threat is not by itself a complete assessment.

Argus should convert this into a simple lesson rule:

> **Do not use a single statement as a yes/no violence detector. Examine the surrounding behaviour, context, change and available corroboration.**

## 2.4 Static risk factors, dynamic risk factors and warning behaviours are different

The literature uses related terms that Argus should keep separate.

### Static risk factors

Static factors are historical or otherwise fixed facts. In general violence-risk research, prior violent behaviour is a classic example. Static factors may provide background context, but they do not by themselves show what is happening now.

Douglas and Skeem distinguish **risk status**, based largely on relatively static factors, from **risk state**, which can change as dynamic factors change.

Argus should teach static factors only as **context**, not as a profile. It should avoid turning demographic correlates into memorized suspicion rules.

### Dynamic risk factors

Dynamic factors can change over time and can therefore affect current risk state and sometimes provide targets for intervention. Examples in professional practice can include changing life circumstances, acute destabilization, worsening substance misuse, access changes, deteriorating coping or escalating conflict.

For Argus, the key idea is simpler:

> **A current change can matter more for immediate concern than an old fact that has not changed.**

The learner should not diagnose the cause of that change.

### Warning behaviours

Meloy and colleagues use **warning behaviours** for dynamic patterns that may indicate increasing or accelerating risk of intended violence. The original typology includes pathway, fixation, identification, novel aggression, energy burst, leakage, directly communicated threat and last resort behaviour.

These are not a checklist in which one item equals danger. They are professional concepts intended to structure judgment and management. Later work has found empirical support for several warning behaviours, while also showing important limitations in sample size, comparison groups, external validity and prospective prediction.

Argus should teach them as **recognition vocabulary**, not a scoring instrument.

## 2.5 Context and pattern accumulation matter

The FBI’s *Making Prevention a Reality* emphasizes content, context and circumstances. Secret Service research similarly stresses that pre-attack themes are useful only as part of a broader assessment.

The practical implication is that isolated behaviours should usually carry limited weight.

Examples:

- Anger after a bad day is not equivalent to escalating grievance plus repeated violent communications.
- A lawful interest in weapons is not equivalent to a marked new preoccupation tied to a named grievance and violent intent.
- A disturbing creative-writing assignment is not equivalent to repeated target-specific communications plus escalating preparation behaviour.
- Looking around a train station is not suspicious merely because the observer does not understand why the person is doing it.

Argus should reward learners who integrate several facts while resisting arithmetic such as “three signs = high threat.”

## 2.6 Change from baseline is important, but baseline is not certainty

Canadian VTRA material and FBI/NTAC work both place value on behavioural change. A significant deviation from a known baseline can be informative because it tells the observer something has changed.

However:

- strangers often have no reliable baseline;
- a baseline can be incomplete or wrong;
- change may reflect distress, illness, conflict, bereavement, substance use or other non-violent causes;
- unchanged behaviour does not prove safety;
- absence of an observed change may simply mean nobody saw it.

Argus should therefore teach:

> **Baseline change raises a question; it does not answer it.**

## 2.7 Some important behaviour may never be visible

A 2026 FBI quick-reference study of 59 active-shooter cases found that not every pathway element was observable before an attack. In some cases, violent intent or overt behavioural movement was not observed even though the offender later acted.

This is another reason the course must not imply that a trained learner can reliably “spot” all dangerous situations.

The correct lesson is:

> **Observable behaviour can create opportunities for intervention, but lack of an observed warning behaviour cannot prove absence of risk.**

# 3. The Argus model: Concern, Context, Escalation

The course title should be literal. The learner should repeatedly answer three questions.

## 3.1 Concern — what behaviour actually occurred?

Start with observable behaviour or communication.

Ask:

- What did the person actually say or do?
- Is the report first-hand, second-hand or speculative?
- Is the behaviour related to violence, coercion, stalking, threats, severe distress or movement toward a harmful act?
- Is there a plausible ordinary explanation?

The learner must separate observation from inference.

Bad reasoning:

> “He looked unstable.”

Better:

> “He sent five unwanted messages after being told to stop and two referred to harming the recipient.”

## 3.2 Context — what changes the meaning?

Ask:

- What happened before and after the behaviour?
- Is there a known grievance, conflict or target?
- Is this new or escalating relative to known baseline?
- Is there repetition or accumulation across time?
- Are multiple independent sources describing the same pattern?
- Are important facts missing?
- Is the behaviour lawful or commonplace in this setting?

Context prevents both under-reaction and over-reaction.

## 3.3 Escalation — is the pattern becoming more immediate or concrete?

Escalation should be understood as **movement in concern**, not a predicted countdown.

Potentially meaningful escalation includes:

- violent communications becoming more specific or frequent;
- increasing fixation on a person, group or grievance;
- leakage of violent intent to others;
- a cluster of new threat-related behaviours over a short period;
- behaviour suggesting movement from grievance or fantasy toward action;
- increasingly desperate or “no alternative” framing;
- new aggression combined with other threat-related indicators;
- repeated unwanted approach behaviour toward a person of concern.

Argus must not teach the operational mechanics of planning, target selection, security testing, weapon use or attack execution. “Movement toward action” is sufficient for Level I.

# 4. Warning-behaviour vocabulary for Level I

The full professional typology can be introduced, but the learner does not need to perform a professional coding exercise.

| Concept | Safe Level-I meaning | What Argus must emphasize |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pathway** | Behaviour suggesting movement from grievance/violent idea toward carrying out intended violence. | Teach the existence of progression, not attack methods or operational steps. |
| **Fixation** | Increasingly intense or perseverative preoccupation with a person, cause or grievance, especially when it worsens or dominates behaviour. | Strong interest alone is not threat evidence. Change, intensity and violent context matter. |
| **Identification** | Concerning self-identification with violent actors or a violent role. | Do not treat ordinary fandom, military interest or identity as sufficient. |
| **Leakage** | Communication to another person suggesting violent intent, fantasy or planned harm. | Report the communication accurately; do not assume every disturbing statement predicts action. |
| **Energy burst** | Marked increase in threat-related activity over a relatively short period. | Acceleration matters more than raw activity alone. |
| **Last resort** | Increasing desperation, urgency or framing that violence is the only remaining option. | Distress is not violence; violent context and other indicators matter. |
| **Novel aggression** | New aggression that is unusual for the person and relevant to the broader pattern. | One unrelated conflict is not automatically targeted-violence evidence. |
| **Direct threat** | Explicit communication of intent to harm. | Important, but neither necessary nor sufficient for targeted violence. |

The assessment should test understanding of these distinctions, not memorization of sensational examples.

# 5. Three Argus response categories

Argus should avoid professional labels such as “low / medium / high threat.” Those labels imply a level of assessment the course does not provide.

Use three civilian action categories instead.

## 5.1 Ordinary

**Meaning:** The available behaviour is ordinary, benign, lawful, weakly related to violence, or too ambiguous to justify special threat concern.

Typical features:

- no violent or coercive behaviour;
- no meaningful pattern or escalation;
- behaviour is common for the setting;
- the concern is based mainly on appearance, identity or stereotype;
- the evidence consists of one ambiguous observation with several plausible benign explanations.

Correct learner response may be:

- no special threat action;
- do not label the person;
- remain ordinarily aware of the environment;
- choose **insufficient information** if the scenario does not support a stronger conclusion.

“Ordinary” does not mean guaranteed safe forever.

## 5.2 Concerning

**Meaning:** Observable behaviour and context justify sharing factual concern with an appropriate responsible person or system, but there is no clear immediate emergency in the scenario.

Typical features:

- repeated or escalating violent communications;
- leakage;
- threatening or stalking behaviour;
- significant change from known baseline combined with violence-relevant indicators;
- concerning fixation or identification in context;
- movement from grievance toward action;
- multiple indicators accumulating across time;
- a direct threat whose immediacy is unclear.

Correct learner response:

- report facts promptly through an appropriate channel;
- preserve exact wording and chronology when possible;
- distinguish direct observations from hearsay;
- state uncertainty and missing information;
- do not confront, interrogate, diagnose or independently investigate the person.

Possible channels depend on context: a workplace safety/security lead, school threat-assessment process, supervisor, campus safety service, local police non-emergency service or another designated authority.

## 5.3 Urgent

**Meaning:** The scenario presents an immediate threat to safety or active violence. The task is emergency response, not further amateur assessment.

Typical features include:

- violence is occurring;
- an explicit immediate threat is paired with behaviour showing the person is acting on it;
- a person is actively attempting to reach or harm a target while making violent threats;
- another concrete circumstance makes immediate injury reasonably possible now.

Correct learner response:

- prioritize immediate personal safety;
- disengage or move away when safely possible;
- contact emergency services;
- in Ontario, call **9-1-1** for an emergency or immediate danger.

Argus should not add tactical instructions for confronting, restraining, pursuing or disarming anyone.

# 6. Reporting is not accusing

A central applied skill should be converting concern into a factual report.

Teach a compact reporting structure:

1. **Observed:** what exactly was seen, heard, received or documented?
2. **When/where:** when and where did it occur?
3. **Pattern/change:** is this repeated, escalating or different from known baseline?
4. **Context:** what relevant relationship, grievance or preceding event is known?
5. **Source:** first-hand, documented, or reported by someone else?
6. **Immediate safety:** is anyone in immediate danger now?
7. **Unknowns:** what important information is missing?

Example:

> “Yesterday and today, Alex sent the same coworker four unwanted messages after being told not to contact them. Two messages said the coworker would ‘pay’ and one referred to hurting them. I saw screenshots of the messages. I do not know whether Alex has made any immediate plan or is nearby.”

This is stronger than:

> “Alex is dangerous and crazy.”

The course should consistently reward factual reporting and penalize unsupported labels.

# 7. Safe authored scenario design

## 7.1 Core scenario loop

Each scenario should expose a bounded set of facts, then ask the learner to:

1. identify direct observations versus interpretations;
2. classify the situation as **ordinary**, **concerning**, **urgent**, or **insufficient information** where appropriate;
3. identify the strongest reason for the classification;
4. identify one fact that should **not** be treated as threat evidence;
5. identify the most important missing information;
6. choose the next safe reporting/escalation action;
7. receive a short source-backed explanation.

A second beat should sometimes add a new fact and ask the learner to reassess. This makes **change over time** an actual practiced skill rather than a definition.

## 7.2 Scenario construction rules

Every authored scenario should:

- use observable behaviour;
- contain enough context to justify the intended answer;
- avoid demographic cues unless the explicit lesson is that they are irrelevant;
- avoid graphic violence;
- omit operational attack details;
- avoid teaching how to select targets, defeat security, acquire weapons or stage attacks;
- include benign comparators so the course does not train hypervigilance;
- include ambiguous cases where **insufficient information** is correct;
- distinguish welfare concern from violence concern;
- avoid mental-health diagnosis as a learner task;
- avoid requiring the learner to personally confront a concerning person;
- make emergency cases obvious enough that the learner is not learning a pseudo-clinical imminence test.

## 7.3 Recommended scenario families

### Scenario A — appearance without behaviour

A commuter feels uneasy about another passenger because of the passenger’s clothing, age and appearance. The passenger is quietly waiting for a train and displays no threatening behaviour.

**Correct:** ordinary / no behavioural basis for threat concern.  
**Lesson:** identity and appearance are not evidence.

### Scenario B — common behaviour in context

A person in an unfamiliar station repeatedly checks signs, the platform display and two exits while holding a phone with a map open.

**Correct:** ordinary.  
**Lesson:** looking around or checking exits is common and contextually explained.

### Scenario C — disturbing but isolated expression

A student submits a fictional story containing violence. There are no threats, named targets, recent behavioural changes or corroborating concerns.

**Correct:** insufficient information / do not infer intent from the creative work alone.  
**Lesson:** content needs context.

### Scenario D — hobby without violent context

A coworker has long participated in lawful sport shooting and posts ordinary range photos. No grievance, threat, fixation, behavioural change or unsafe conduct is described.

**Correct:** ordinary.  
**Lesson:** lawful weapons interest alone is not a targeted-violence indicator.

### Scenario E — accumulating concern

Over several weeks, a worker repeatedly sends unwanted messages to a former colleague after being told to stop. The messages become increasingly hostile, refer to revenge and include a statement suggesting the colleague will be harmed. A supervisor confirms the pattern.

**Correct:** concerning.  
**Action:** factual reporting through workplace/security or police channels as appropriate; no personal confrontation.  
**Lesson:** repetition, target, escalation and violent communication accumulate.

### Scenario F — baseline change plus threat-related communication

A person who is well known to friends becomes markedly withdrawn over a short period, repeatedly speaks about an unresolved grievance and begins telling several people that violence may be the only solution.

**Correct:** concerning, not a diagnosis.  
**Missing information:** exact statements, target, immediacy, other observed behaviour, current location/safety context.  
**Lesson:** change plus violent framing warrants reporting, but the learner still cannot predict violence.

### Scenario G — political or ideological belief without violent behaviour

A person strongly advocates a controversial political position online. The scenario contains no threat, violent advocacy directed at a target, stalking, leakage or preparation behaviour.

**Correct:** ordinary with respect to threat recognition.  
**Lesson:** unpopular or extreme-seeming beliefs are not by themselves evidence of targeted violence.

### Scenario H — mental-health label without threat behaviour

A learner is told that a neighbour has a psychiatric diagnosis. The neighbour is calm, interacts normally and has made no threats or concerning approaches.

**Correct:** ordinary.  
**Lesson:** diagnosis is not a threat classification.

### Scenario I — concerning communication, unclear immediacy

A friend receives a message from someone describing revenge against a named person, saying they are out of options and implying that harm is coming, but giving no clear time or immediate location.

**Correct:** concerning; report promptly and communicate the exact wording.  
**Lesson:** absence of a precise time does not make a communication irrelevant, but Argus should not pretend the learner can determine professional risk level.

### Scenario J — emergency

A person is currently trying to force entry toward a named individual while shouting an immediate threat to kill them.

**Correct:** urgent.  
**Action:** prioritize personal safety and contact 9-1-1.  
**Lesson:** when danger is immediate, stop assessing and escalate to emergency response.

### Scenario K — new information changes the answer

**Beat 1:** A coworker angrily says, “I’m done with this place,” after a disciplinary meeting. No violence is mentioned.  
**Initial answer:** insufficient information / ordinary frustration.

**Beat 2:** The learner later sees repeated messages to a named manager that describe revenge and violent harm.  
**Revised answer:** concerning.

**Lesson:** reassessment matters; one earlier fact should not anchor the judgment.

### Scenario L — report quality

Two reports describe the same event:

- “Jordan looked psycho and dangerous.”
- “Jordan paced outside Pat’s office for 20 minutes after being told to leave and twice said Pat would ‘regret this.’”

**Correct:** the second report is substantially more useful.  
**Lesson:** report behaviour, not diagnosis or impression.

# 8. Assessment model

## 8.1 What should be memorized

Finite recall is appropriate for:

- behaviour over profile;
- threat ≠ threat communication;
- static versus dynamic distinction;
- warning behaviour as dynamic/accelerating change;
- the names and high-level meanings of the eight warning-behaviour concepts;
- the three Argus response categories;
- immediate danger → emergency response;
- report facts, context, chronology and uncertainty;
- professional assessment is outside course scope.

## 8.2 What must be scenario-practiced

Scenario practice is required for:

- ordinary versus concerning ambiguity;
- integrating multiple weak indicators;
- recognizing escalation;
- resisting demographic and diagnostic shortcuts;
- identifying missing information;
- changing an answer when new facts arrive;
- deciding when “insufficient information” is best;
- writing a concise factual report;
- recognizing when further engagement is inappropriate because the situation is urgent.

Memorizing the eight warning-behaviour labels without this application layer would not satisfy the completion claim.

## 8.3 Scoring rubric

Score each scenario across five dimensions, 0–2 each.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Behavioural grounding** | Relies on stereotype, impression or diagnosis. | Mixes facts with inference. | Uses concrete observed behaviour/communications. |
| **Context and pattern** | Treats one cue as decisive. | Notices some context. | Correctly weighs baseline, repetition, escalation and corroboration. |
| **Uncertainty** | Claims certainty/prediction. | Acknowledges some uncertainty. | Explicitly identifies material unknowns and uses “insufficient information” when warranted. |
| **Classification** | Clearly disproportionate. | Defensible but misses important detail. | Ordinary/concerning/urgent classification fits the authored evidence. |
| **Response** | Confronts, investigates, profiles or delays an emergency. | Partly appropriate. | Uses factual reporting or emergency escalation proportionately. |

### Safety gate

A learner should not pass a scenario if they:

- classify based primarily on protected demographic traits;
- diagnose mental illness as proof of danger;
- propose personally investigating, following, searching or confronting the person;
- recommend tactical violence;
- ignore clearly immediate danger in order to gather more information.

## 8.4 Completion standard

Recommended completion requirement:

- finite recall check: **≥ 85%**;
- scenario set: **≥ 80% of available rubric points**;
- no safety-gate failure on the final set;
- at least one correctly handled **ordinary**, **concerning**, **urgent** and **insufficient-information** scenario;
- at least one scenario in which the learner revises an earlier judgment after new information.

The course should show the learner exactly what the completion claim means and what it does not mean.

# 9. Common misconceptions to teach explicitly

## “There is a dangerous-person profile.”

False. Professional targeted-violence guidance rejects demographic profiling.

## “A single warning sign means someone will attack.”

False. Warning behaviours are contextual indicators of concern or accelerating risk, not deterministic predictors.

## “If someone never makes a direct threat, there is no threat.”

False. Some people who pose a threat never directly threaten the eventual target.

## “Anyone who makes a direct threat will become violent.”

False. Direct threats matter, but many threateners do not act. Context and surrounding behaviour remain necessary.

## “Mental illness is a violence detector.”

False. A diagnosis is not a substitute for behavioural evidence. Argus should never ask learners to diagnose strangers.

## “Odd behaviour is suspicious behaviour.”

False. Unusual does not mean dangerous. A deviation from baseline may justify attention or welfare concern without implying violent intent.

## “More indicators can simply be added up.”

False. Professional threat assessment is not an unweighted checklist. Relationships among behaviours, context, timing and corroboration matter.

## “No observable warning sign means no danger.”

False. Some intent or progression may remain private or unobserved.

## “The correct response is to confront the person and see what they say.”

False for this course. Concerning situations should move toward appropriate reporting and professional assessment, not amateur confrontation.

## “Reporting means accusing someone of being dangerous.”

False. A useful report describes observable facts and uncertainty so an appropriate authority can decide what follows.

# 10. Safety and ethics boundaries

This course must not include:

- demographic profiling;
- attacker “types” based on race, religion, ideology, gender or personality stereotype;
- amateur psychiatric diagnosis;
- instructions for surveilling or investigating another person;
- instructions for searching someone’s devices, property or online accounts;
- weapon-selection or weapon-use instruction;
- target selection, reconnaissance, access testing or security-evasion instruction;
- detailed attack-pathway mechanics that would improve an attacker’s planning;
- restraint, pursuit, disarming or confrontation tactics;
- actuarial-looking scores that imply professional violence prediction;
- a quiz claiming to determine whether a real person is dangerous.

Authored scenarios should use fictional people and bounded facts. The product should never invite the user to input a real person’s details and receive a “risk score.”

If Argus later supports open-ended AI evaluation, the system prompt should refuse to classify real individuals as dangerous and redirect toward factual documentation, immediate-safety guidance and qualified local help.

# 11. Ontario / Canadian treatment

## 11.1 Professional assessment remains professional

The Canadian Association of Threat Assessment Professionals defines TAM as operational work concerned with assessing and managing violence risk and expects substantial scientific knowledge, training, practical experience and competence with relevant assessment methods.

The OPP’s Threat and Behaviour Analysis Team likewise consists of specially trained personnel using empirically supported risk-assessment tools and concepts.

These sources reinforce the Argus boundary: **civilian recognition and reporting are teachable; professional threat assessment is not being reproduced in the app.**

## 11.2 Multidisciplinary escalation

Ontario’s provincial police/school-board protocol describes multidisciplinary threat-management teams and states that situations judged to present a potential risk of violence should involve police assistance. The protocol also notes that multidisciplinary school teams are not substitutes for police threat-assessment units.

Argus should generalize the principle rather than teach school administrative procedure:

> **Serious concern should move toward the responsible multidisciplinary or public-safety system, not remain with a lone amateur assessor.**

## 11.3 Emergency versus non-emergency

For Ontario learners:

- **9-1-1** is the correct emergency channel when there is immediate danger or an emergency;
- non-immediate concerns may be reported through the relevant institution, local police non-emergency service or other designated safety channel.

The course should avoid hard-coding a single non-emergency number because municipal police services vary. A jurisdiction-aware reference card can link to current Ontario/local guidance later.

# 12. Recommended lesson progression

The course should be approximately **7 lessons + 1 final practicum**, with 35–50 authored scenario items across Learn, Review and Test.

## Lesson 1 — No profile, no prediction

Teach:

- targeted-violence prevention is behaviour-based;
- demographic profiling is invalid and harmful;
- concern is not prediction;
- Argus teaches recognition/reporting, not professional assessment.

Practice:

- remove irrelevant demographic details;
- identify when “insufficient information” is correct.

## Lesson 2 — Observation before interpretation

Teach:

- observable behaviour;
- inference;
- hearsay/source quality;
- factual wording.

Practice:

- convert impressionistic descriptions into objective observations.

Dependency: this lesson should reuse the concepts from **Observation vs Inference** rather than duplicate that course in full.

## Lesson 3 — Static context vs dynamic change

Teach:

- static/historical context;
- dynamic factors;
- baseline and change;
- why change raises questions but does not establish violent intent.

Practice:

- compare stable background information with meaningful new escalation.

## Lesson 4 — Warning behaviours without checklist thinking

Teach high-level meanings of:

- pathway;
- fixation;
- identification;
- leakage;
- energy burst;
- last resort;
- novel aggression;
- direct threat.

Practice:

- identify the concept in short non-operational examples;
- reject “one sign = danger.”

## Lesson 5 — Context and accumulation

Teach:

- content, context and circumstances;
- corroboration;
- pattern accumulation;
- direct threat versus posing a threat;
- re-evaluation when new facts arrive.

Practice:

- two-beat and three-beat scenarios where the classification changes.

## Lesson 6 — Ordinary, concerning, urgent

Teach the Argus action categories.

Practice:

- classify mixed civilian, workplace, transit, school/campus and online scenarios;
- include benign and ambiguous cases at least as often as urgent ones to avoid threat inflation.

## Lesson 7 — Report, do not diagnose

Teach:

- factual reporting structure;
- exact wording and chronology;
- uncertainty and missing information;
- appropriate channel selection;
- 9-1-1 for immediate danger;
- no confrontation or amateur investigation.

Practice:

- improve bad reports;
- choose the next safe action.

## Final practicum — Concern, Context, Escalation

Use 10–12 mixed scenarios with no lesson labels.

Required mix:

- 3 ordinary/benign;
- 2 insufficient-information;
- 4 concerning;
- 2 urgent;
- at least 2 scenarios where new information changes the correct answer;
- at least 2 explicit anti-profiling/bias traps;
- at least 1 factual-report rewrite.

The practicum should test judgment under uncertainty rather than recall of terminology.

# 13. Product treatment

## Learning shape

**Hybrid:** finite recall + authored scenario application.

This is a strong candidate for the generic scenario-practice capability proposed in #104 because its useful skill is conditional judgment, not vocabulary recall.

Recommended item types:

- classify: ordinary / concerning / urgent / insufficient information;
- observation vs inference;
- identify relevant vs irrelevant evidence;
- select strongest missing-information question;
- identify escalation/change from baseline;
- choose the safer reporting response;
- improve an impressionistic report;
- reassess after new information;
- identify which warning-behaviour concept is illustrated.

Do not use free-form “is this person dangerous?” prompts.

## Relationship to other proposed courses

Recommended sequence within the broader practical-skills programme:

1. **Situational Awareness I — Baseline, Anomaly, Reassess**
2. **Observation vs Inference**
3. **Threat Recognition I — Concern, Context, Escalation**
4. **Bias Control in Behavioural Judgement**
5. **De-escalation I — Communication Fundamentals**
6. **Crisis Active Listening**

Threat Recognition should come after Observation vs Inference because the learner must already understand that an observed fact and an inferred motive are different things.

De-escalation should come after threat recognition so the learner is not trained to keep talking in scenarios where disengagement or emergency escalation is more appropriate.

# 14. Research limitations Argus should preserve

The evidence base supports structured, behaviour-focused threat assessment as professional practice, but several limits matter for course claims.

- Much targeted-violence research is retrospective: researchers study known attackers after the outcome is known.
- Rare-event prediction creates severe false-positive problems.
- Some warning-behaviour studies use small samples or limited comparison groups.
- Definitions of targeted violence and warning behaviours are not perfectly uniform across studies.
- Behaviours common among attackers may also occur among many non-attackers.
- Not every relevant thought or behaviour is observable before an attack.
- Professional assessment depends on collateral information, corroboration, multidisciplinary expertise and case management that an app scenario cannot reproduce.
- Research is stronger for identifying **areas of concern worth assessing** than for generating precise individual probabilities of violence.

A 2023 study of targeted violence among persons found not criminally responsible explicitly notes the need for better comparison groups and prospective research before stronger predictive claims can be made. A 2024 NCITE state-of-the-science review likewise reflects the field’s continuing effort to establish measurement quality and effectiveness.

Argus should preserve that uncertainty rather than simplify it away.

# 16. Final course decision

**Ship.**

The topic fits Argus unusually well if its scope remains narrow.

It teaches a real, transferable practical skill: noticing behaviour, controlling inference, recognizing escalation, tolerating uncertainty, and reporting concerns proportionately. It also creates a useful bridge between Situational Awareness, Observation vs Inference, Bias Control and De-escalation.

The course should **not** teach learners to decide who is dangerous. It should teach them to recognize when the available evidence supports one of four conclusions:

- ordinary;
- concerning;
- urgent;
- insufficient information.

The final completion claim should remain exactly:

> **Given authored civilian scenarios, the learner can distinguish ordinary, concerning and urgent situations using observable behaviour, context, change from baseline, pattern accumulation and uncertainty; identify important missing information; and choose an appropriate reporting or emergency escalation response without demographic profiling, amateur diagnosis, tactical analysis or claiming to predict violence.**
