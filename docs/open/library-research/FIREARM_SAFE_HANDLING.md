# Canadian Firearm Safety II — Safe Handling Fundamentals

**Status:** deep research / course recommendation  
**Date:** 2026-09-17  
**Issue:** #104  
**Implementation:** out of scope  
**Shipped:** no (checked 2026-09-25) — the completion claim needs scenario practice; see `../ISSUE_104_SCENARIO_ITEMS.md`  
**Jurisdictional anchor:** Canada / Ontario delivery context  
**Role in Argus:** knowledge retention and authored scenario rehearsal **only**, as a companion to formal Canadian Firearms Safety Course (CFSC) instruction  

## Recommendation in one sentence

Build this as a compact safety-memory course that reinforces **muzzle control, trigger discipline, condition verification, safe transfer, ammunition matching, range-state behaviour and abnormal-condition stop rules**, while explicitly refusing to treat app completion as evidence that the learner can physically load, unload, clear or otherwise manipulate a firearm safely.

---

## 1. Research conclusion

This is a strong Argus course candidate if it is kept narrower than a conventional firearms course.

The Canadian Firearms Safety Course already has a clear doctrine for the knowledge Argus should reinforce. The recurring logic is deliberately conservative:

- assume condition rather than infer it;
- maintain muzzle control continuously;
- keep the trigger finger off the trigger and outside the trigger guard except when firing is actually intended and permitted;
- independently establish that a firearm is unloaded rather than relying on another person’s assurance;
- pass or accept only open and unloaded firearms;
- load only when actual use is intended and only where discharge is safe and lawful;
- match ammunition to the firearm’s markings rather than by appearance or physical fit;
- obey range state and cease-fire commands immediately;
- treat unusual firing conditions as reasons to **stop**, preserve safe direction and investigate safely rather than continuing.

The RCMP/Canadian Firearms Program handbook also makes a crucial product-design point: firearm actions differ, detailed loading/unloading methods differ, and the handbook’s general procedures do not replace the owner’s manual or qualified assistance. The official CFSC is not merely a reading exercise. The RCMP describes the course as including both written and practical testing, and Ontario’s Firearms Safety Education Service describes a minimum eight-hour CFSC plus written and practical tests.

That gives Argus a defensible role: **retention and judgement**, not manipulation training.

The course should therefore be designed around three explicit layers:

1. **Finite knowledge Argus can test** — safety rules, terminology, recognition of safe/unsafe conditions, ammunition identification principles, range-state rules and abnormal-condition responses.
2. **Scenario judgement Argus can rehearse** — what principle governs a situation, what the safest next decision is, when to stop, when information is insufficient, and when to defer to an instructor/range officer/owner’s manual.
3. **Hands-on manipulations Argus must not certify** — operating particular actions, physically loading/unloading, demonstrating PROVE on real firearms, checking a bore on a particular firearm, handing/receiving while controlling real weight and geometry, or resolving a malfunction.

The result should feel like a safety doctrine course that makes formal CFSC instruction easier to retain, not an online substitute for that instruction.

## 3. Exact competency and completion claim

### Competency claim

After completing **Canadian Firearm Safety II — Safe Handling Fundamentals**, the learner can, in authored Canadian safety scenarios:

- identify a safe-handling violation involving muzzle direction, trigger contact, assumed firearm condition or reliance on a mechanical safety;
- explain why an open action, an empty-looking magazine or another person’s assurance is not by itself proof that a firearm is unloaded;
- identify the purpose and broad logic of independently PROVEing a firearm safe before handling, passing or accepting it;
- recognize the safety conditions that should exist before a firearm is passed or accepted;
- distinguish universal loading/unloading safety principles from action-specific manipulations that require supervised instruction;
- match ammunition by authoritative firearm/ammunition markings rather than appearance, guesswork or physical fit;
- identify correct behaviour when a cease-fire is called or when the range is not active;
- recognize suspected misfire, hangfire, squib/primer-pop or bore-obstruction situations as **stop conditions** requiring safe direction and controlled resolution;
- choose when to stop handling and defer to a range officer, qualified instructor, gunsmith, owner’s manual or other competent authority.

### Completion claim

> **Completed Canadian Firearm Safety II — Safe Handling Fundamentals:** demonstrated recall of core CFSC safe-handling principles and applied them successfully across authored safety scenarios involving firearm condition, transfers, ammunition, range commands and abnormal conditions.

### What completion must never imply

Completion does **not** mean that the learner:

- has completed or passed the Canadian Firearms Safety Course;
- has passed any CFSC written or practical examination;
- is eligible for, or has been granted, a Possession and Acquisition Licence;
- has demonstrated safe physical handling of a real firearm;
- can safely operate an unfamiliar firearm action;
- can load, unload, clear, inspect, repair or service a firearm without supervised instruction;
- can diagnose or resolve a malfunction from text or images alone;
- has demonstrated range competence;
- has received marksmanship instruction;
- has received tactical, defensive, concealment or use-of-force training;
- should disregard a range officer, instructor, manufacturer instruction or current law because an Argus answer differs.

This non-claim should appear in the course description and completion screen, not only in internal documentation.

## 4. The core doctrine Argus should retain

## 4.1 Muzzle control is continuous, not a single checklist step

The CFSC’s Vital Four includes **control the muzzle direction at all times**. For Argus, the important concept is the word **continuous**.

Muzzle control applies:

- before the firearm’s condition has been established;
- while opening or examining the action;
- while loading or unloading;
- while handing or receiving;
- while talking, thinking, changing position or responding to another person;
- during a suspected misfire or other abnormal condition;
- while placing a firearm down;
- whenever the firearm is in a person’s hands.

A “safe direction” is contextual. Argus should not teach a simplistic universal answer such as “always point at the floor” or “always point upward.” The safest available direction is the direction that, in the actual environment, minimizes the consequence of an unintended discharge. At a firing range, the CFSC normally requires the muzzle to remain downrange. In other contexts, the learner must consider where people are and what lies in the potential line of fire.

### What Argus can test

- whether muzzle control is being maintained during an otherwise routine action;
- whether a learner notices that a person has allowed the muzzle to cross another person while talking or repositioning;
- whether “I checked that it was unloaded” is wrongly being used as justification for relaxed muzzle control;
- whether the learner understands that safe direction can change with the environment.

### What Argus should not simulate

Argus should not ask the learner to imitate or “practice” muzzle orientation with a real firearm using the phone. Physical geometry, surroundings, balance and other people matter too much for an app to validate the manipulation.

---

## 4.2 Trigger discipline is a handling rule, not a marksmanship technique

The CFSC doctrine is that the trigger finger remains **off the trigger and outside the trigger guard** during handling until firing is intended under safe, permitted conditions.

Argus should treat this as a binary handling invariant:

- handling / checking / passing / loading / unloading / repositioning: finger remains outside the trigger guard;
- a mechanical safety does not authorize trigger contact;
- an unloaded status does not authorize casual trigger contact;
- attention being elsewhere is not an exception.

The course should not teach trigger press, trigger reset, firing cadence or any other performance technique. Those are outside this course and unnecessary to achieve the safety objective.

### Useful misconception to test

> “The safety is on, so it is fine for my finger to rest on the trigger.”

Incorrect. The CFSC explicitly warns that mechanical safeties can fail and should never be relied upon as the primary protection against an unintended discharge.

---

## 4.3 Mechanical safeties are supplementary

The 2014 CFSC handbook states that mechanical devices can fail and that safe handling by the person holding the firearm remains the most important safety control. It instructs users to use the safety but never rely on it to prevent firing.

Argus should retain exactly that relationship:

**safe handling rules remain in force regardless of safety position.**

This is worth testing because learners can otherwise treat the safety as permission to relax muzzle or trigger discipline.

Do not require learners to identify the location, appearance or operation of particular manufacturers’ safety mechanisms. Those are model-specific manipulations.

---

## 4.4 Firearm condition is established, not assumed

A major learning objective should be eliminating shorthand such as:

- “It looks unloaded.”
- “The magazine is out.”
- “The action is open, so it must be safe.”
- “The last person said it was clear.”
- “I checked it five minutes ago.”

The CFSC doctrine uses **PROVE** to establish an unloaded condition:

- **P** — Point the firearm in the safest available direction.
- **R** — Remove all ammunition.
- **O** — Observe the chamber(s).
- **V** — Verify the feeding path.
- **E** — Examine the bore for obstructions.

This course is a companion to **Firearm Safety I — ACTS & PROVE**, so it should not spend most of its time re-teaching the acronym. The important advanced retention point is the **condition model** behind it:

> A firearm is treated as loaded until its condition has been personally established through the prescribed safety process, and that established status is not permanent once the firearm leaves the person’s direct control.

The 2014 handbook states that the firearm is unloaded and safe **until it leaves the direct control of the person who unloaded and PROVEd it safe**. That is a high-value doctrine for scenarios.

### Direct-control scenarios

Argus should ask questions such as:

- You PROVEd a firearm safe, set it on the bench and remained beside it. Another person picks it up, examines it and returns it. What should you assume before handling it again?
- You watched an instructor unload a firearm from across the room. Is watching sufficient to replace your own condition check before accepting it?
- A detachable magazine has been removed. What condition remains unknown?

The goal is not to make the learner mechanically faster. It is to make them unwilling to infer condition from incomplete cues.

---

## 4.5 Passing and receiving: open, unloaded, independently verified

The 2014 handbook states:

> **Pass or accept only open and unloaded firearms.**

Argus should teach the safety logic without turning it into a choreography tutorial.

### Knowledge rule

Before a firearm is handed to another person:

- its unloaded condition must be established;
- the action should be open whenever the design permits;
- muzzle direction and trigger discipline remain controlled through the transfer.

The receiver should not treat the giver’s assurance as proof. Once the receiver takes control, they establish condition themselves using the CFSC safety doctrine.

### Why this matters

The transfer creates a change of control. The new handler does not inherit the previous handler’s certainty. This is the same principle that applies when a firearm has left direct control for any reason.

### Hands-on boundary

Argus must **not** claim to teach the physical hand placement, angle, balance, action manipulation or body positioning for transferring a particular firearm. Those details belong in supervised CFSC practice because barrel length, action type, weight and firearm geometry vary.

---

## 4.6 Loading and unloading: teach invariants, not mechanical recipes

The handbook provides action-specific loading and unloading procedures, but it also explicitly says firearms have unique aspects, details can vary significantly, and the owner’s manual is a primary source for the particular firearm.

That is the basis for a strict Argus boundary.

### Argus should teach these universal safety principles

Before loading:

- establish the firearm’s condition using ACTS/PROVE;
- confirm chamber/bore are free of obstructions;
- maintain the safest available muzzle direction;
- keep anything from contacting the trigger;
- identify the correct ammunition using authoritative markings;
- load only when actual use is intended and only where the firearm can be safely and legally discharged;
- use the firearm’s mechanical safety as applicable, while continuing all other safety rules.

During unloading:

- maintain safe muzzle direction and trigger discipline throughout;
- remove ammunition from the firearm;
- leave the action open when the design permits;
- confirm the chamber(s) are empty;
- verify the feeding path is empty;
- examine for bore obstruction as required by the CFSC doctrine;
- recognize that a particular firearm’s exact sequence may differ.

### Argus should not teach these manipulations

Do not create text, animation or step ordering for:

- how to operate a bolt action;
- how to cycle a pump;
- how to work a lever action;
- how to load or unload a semi-automatic firearm;
- how to manipulate a break/hinge action;
- revolver loading/unloading sequences;
- muzzleloader loading or unloading;
- clearing a specific stoppage or jam;
- disassembly or maintenance procedures.

These are exactly the activities in which a learner can know the words but still be unsafe with the physical object.

### Product rule

A question may ask **which principle has been violated** during a described loading/unloading situation. It should not ask the learner to reconstruct a model-specific manipulation sequence from memory.

## 4.7 Ammunition compatibility: markings outrank appearance

The CFSC treats ammunition selection as safety-critical. Its general instruction is to select correct ammunition by matching the **data stamp on the firearm** with the **head stamp on the cartridge/shell or the ammunition-box information**.

The finite knowledge worth retaining is:

- use only ammunition for which the firearm is designed;
- match authoritative markings rather than relying on colour, apparent diameter, memory or whether the cartridge physically fits;
- a cartridge fitting into a chamber is not proof that it is correct ammunition;
- do not experiment with unfamiliar or uncertain ammunition;
- damaged, badly corroded or otherwise questionable ammunition should not be treated as normal serviceable ammunition;
- original packaging is useful because it preserves manufacturer identification and cartridge information;
- black powder and smokeless powder are not interchangeable; muzzleloading-specific content requires additional instruction and is not a target for this course.

### Identification vocabulary Argus can test

- **data stamp:** firearm marking identifying required cartridge/shell information;
- **head stamp:** cartridge-base marking that identifies cartridge information and often manufacturer information;
- **ammunition box label:** another authoritative source for ammunition designation and manufacturer information;
- **calibre / gauge:** identification concepts that must be interpreted with the exact firearm/ammunition marking, not guessed from appearance.

Argus should avoid building a game around memorizing broad inventories of cartridge types. The useful competence is the **matching process and refusal to guess**.

### Scenario principle

If the learner cannot establish compatibility confidently from the markings and appropriate documentation, the safe answer is **do not load it; stop and get qualified guidance**.

## 4.8 Range commands are state changes

The CFSC handbook gives examples of typical range commands:

- “The range is active.”
- “Cease-fire.”
- “The range is no longer active.”

It also warns that commands and signals vary between sports, ranges and jurisdictions and that users must understand the commands used at the particular range.

Argus should therefore teach **state-based behaviour**, not assume every Canadian range uses identical words.

### Active range

The learner follows the range officer’s commands and the range’s local rules. Loading occurs only where and when permitted.

### Cease-fire

The safety principle is immediate compliance. The 2014 handbook’s standard sequence is:

- firing stops at once;
- firearms are unloaded;
- actions are opened;
- firearms are placed down with muzzles in the safe downrange direction;
- shooters step back behind the cease-fire line;
- firearms and ammunition are not handled during the cease-fire;
- activity resumes only under further range commands.

The handbook also states that **in an emergency, anyone can call a cease-fire**.

Argus can test this strongly because it is judgement rather than marksmanship.

### High-value range misconceptions

- “I can finish the current shot after hearing cease-fire.” — No.
- “The gun is unloaded, so I can adjust it during the cease-fire.” — No firearm handling during the cease-fire under the standard CFSC rule.
- “Only the range officer can call a cease-fire.” — The CFSC handbook says anyone can call one in an emergency.
- “All ranges use the exact same commands.” — No; local commands/signals vary and must be learned before participation.

---

## 4.9 Misfire and hangfire: treat uncertainty conservatively

The CFSC handbook distinguishes:

- **misfire:** a cartridge does not fire after the firing mechanism is activated;
- **hangfire:** ignition is delayed after the primer has been struck;
- **primer pop / squib load:** an abnormal discharge in which normal powder ignition is absent or ineffective, producing unusual report/recoil and creating a possible bore obstruction.

For modern ammunition, the handbook’s safety-points summary says that when a misfire occurs, the shooter should **slowly count to 60 while keeping the muzzle pointed in a safe direction**, then remove the cartridge following safe procedures and carefully inspect the bore for obstruction.

### What Argus can teach

The memory rule should be:

> **No normal shot = stop. Maintain safe direction. Keep handling conservative. Allow for delayed ignition. Do not immediately open or re-fire. Resolve under the CFSC procedure / range supervision.**

Argus may test the CFSC’s 60-second wait because it is finite doctrine, but it should not turn the subsequent clearing operation into a virtual step-by-step manipulation exercise.

### Scenario examples

- The trigger is activated and there is a click but no normal discharge. A friend immediately reaches toward the action. What is the safety problem?
- A cartridge does not fire. The shooter turns sideways to ask the range officer what happened. What principle must still be maintained while seeking help?
- A shooter is unsure whether the cartridge is a misfire or hangfire. What assumption is safer?

Correct reasoning should prioritize **safe direction, delayed-fire possibility and stopping normal activity**.

---

## 4.10 Suspected squib or bore obstruction: do not fire again

A suspected obstruction is a stop condition.

The 2014 CFSC handbook teaches examination of the bore for lubricant, rust or obstructions as part of PROVE and before loading. It also warns that a primer pop/squib can occur without the normal noise or recoil and that a bore obstruction can create a dangerous condition.

### Argus doctrine

If firing feels or sounds abnormal, or an obstruction is suspected:

- stop normal firing/handling activity;
- maintain safe muzzle direction and trigger discipline;
- do **not** fire another cartridge to “see if it clears”;
- establish an unloaded condition using safe procedures;
- have the bore inspected for obstruction using the proper method for that firearm;
- if the learner is not trained or confident, defer to the range officer, instructor, gunsmith or other qualified person.

The handbook advises that, whenever possible, bore examination is done from the breech/rear after the firearm has been made safe, and that an obstruction that cannot be properly addressed should be handled by an expert.

Argus should not teach improvised obstruction removal.

## 6. Scenario-design model

The course should not be a deck of definitions alone. The highest-value material is judgement under mundane distraction.

Each scenario should ask one of four things:

1. **What safety principle is controlling here?**
2. **What information is still unverified?**
3. **What is the safest next decision?**
4. **What should stop immediately?**

### 6.1 Scenario rubric

Score answers against five dimensions:

- **Muzzle:** did the learner preserve the safest available direction?
- **Trigger:** did the learner keep the trigger/guard untouched during handling?
- **Condition:** did the learner independently establish or preserve knowledge of condition?
- **Control:** did the learner recognize change of handler, range state or abnormal condition?
- **Deference:** did the learner stop and seek qualified help rather than improvise when the situation became model-specific or uncertain?

The safest correct answer should not always be “continue.” Argus should reward **stop / insufficient information / get the range officer / consult the owner manual** where appropriate.

### 6.2 Scenario set

#### Scenario A — “I already checked it”

A learner PROVEs a firearm safe. They put it on a bench. Another person picks it up, closes the action briefly and returns it. The learner says, “It was unloaded before.”

**Correct principle:** the previous check no longer establishes current condition because the firearm left direct control. Treat it as loaded and re-establish condition before further handling.

#### Scenario B — Magazine removed

Someone removes a detachable magazine and announces, “Clear.”

**Correct principle:** magazine removal alone does not establish an empty chamber or feeding path. Condition remains unverified.

#### Scenario C — Open action, unknown history

A firearm is handed over with the action visibly open. The giver says it is unloaded.

**Correct principle:** open is a good transfer condition but not a substitute for the receiver independently PROVEing it safe.

#### Scenario D — Safety on

A person rests a finger on the trigger because the mechanical safety is engaged.

**Correct principle:** trigger discipline remains in force; mechanical safeties can fail and are supplementary.

#### Scenario E — Conversation distraction

At a bench, a handler turns to answer a question and the muzzle rotates away from the safe range direction.

**Correct principle:** muzzle control is continuous and does not pause during conversation.

#### Scenario F — Unknown ammunition in a tray

Two cartridges look nearly identical. One came from the correct ammunition box; the other is loose and its marking is hard to read.

**Correct principle:** do not choose by visual similarity or fit. Match authoritative firearm/ammunition markings; if uncertain, do not load the cartridge.

#### Scenario G — “It fits”

A cartridge can be inserted into the chamber, so the learner assumes it is compatible.

**Correct principle:** physical fit is not compatibility proof. Match the firearm data stamp to the cartridge/head stamp or ammunition-box identification.

#### Scenario H — Load early

A learner loads before the range officer authorizes loading because they expect the line to become active shortly.

**Correct principle:** range commands control. Load only when permitted and when actual use is intended.

#### Scenario I — Cease-fire mid-task

A cease-fire is called while the learner is preparing to fire.

**Correct principle:** stop immediately; follow the range’s cease-fire procedure. There is no “finish this shot first.”

#### Scenario J — Adjusting an unloaded firearm during cease-fire

The firearm has been unloaded and action opened. The learner reaches forward to adjust its position during the cease-fire.

**Correct principle:** under the standard CFSC range rule, no firearm or ammunition handling occurs during the cease-fire; step back and wait for further commands.

#### Scenario K — Emergency cease-fire

A shooter notices a person moving into an unsafe area but hesitates because the range officer has not called cease-fire.

**Correct principle:** the CFSC handbook states that anyone may call a cease-fire in an emergency.

#### Scenario L — Click, no shot

The trigger is activated and there is a click but no normal discharge. The learner immediately starts opening the action.

**Correct principle:** treat the event conservatively as a possible hangfire; maintain safe direction and follow the CFSC wait/abnormal-condition procedure rather than immediately manipulating the action.

#### Scenario M — Turning after a misfire

After a suspected misfire, the shooter turns toward the range officer while still holding the firearm.

**Correct principle:** seeking help is appropriate, but muzzle direction must remain controlled while doing so.

#### Scenario N — Weak report

A shot produces a noticeably abnormal report/recoil. The next cartridge is ready.

**Correct principle:** stop. A squib/primer-pop or bore obstruction must be considered; do not fire another round until the firearm has been safely checked.

#### Scenario O — Visible bore debris

Before loading, the learner notices possible debris in the bore and says, “It will probably blow out.”

**Correct principle:** do not fire with a suspected obstruction. Make the firearm safe and have the obstruction properly addressed.

#### Scenario P — Passing a loaded firearm

A person wants to hand a loaded firearm to a friend “just for a second.”

**Correct principle:** do not pass or accept a loaded firearm; the CFSC rule is open and unloaded transfer.

#### Scenario Q — Correct answer is deference

A learner cannot determine how an unfamiliar action opens and considers experimenting until it moves.

**Correct principle:** stop. The 2014 handbook expressly says not to handle a firearm one is not comfortable with and to consult the owner’s manual / qualified individual for firearm-specific operation.

#### Scenario R — Local range wording differs

A range uses lights and commands different from the examples in the handbook.

**Correct principle:** the CFSC examples are not universal. Learn and obey the commands/signals of the specific range before handling or firing.

## 7. Misconceptions worth targeting directly

The course should contain explicit misconception items because safe handling fails through overconfidence and shorthand as often as through forgotten vocabulary.

### “Unloaded means safe to point anywhere.”

False. Muzzle control remains continuous regardless of believed condition.

### “If the magazine is removed, the firearm is unloaded.”

False. The chamber and feeding path still require verification.

### “If the action is open, I do not need to check it myself.”

False. Open is a preferred transfer state, not proof by itself.

### “The person handing it to me checked it, so I do not need to.”

False. The receiver establishes condition independently.

### “The safety is on, so trigger contact is acceptable.”

False. Mechanical safeties can fail.

### “If ammunition fits, it is compatible.”

False. Compatibility is established by the firearm/ammunition markings and manufacturer information, not fit.

### “A misfire means nothing happened, so opening the action immediately is safest.”

False. Delayed ignition is possible; the CFSC doctrine requires safe direction and a wait before further handling.

### “A weak or odd shot should be followed by another shot to clear the problem.”

False. An abnormal report/recoil can indicate a squib or obstruction; stop and inspect safely.

### “Only a range officer can call a cease-fire.”

False in an emergency under the CFSC handbook.

### “Once a firearm has been PROVEd safe, it stays safe for the rest of the session.”

False. The status is only known while it remains under the direct control of the person who established it.

### “Reading the manual or passing an Argus course means I can safely manipulate that firearm.”

False. Knowledge and physical competence are different claims.

## 8. Assessment design

## 8.1 Finite recall

Recommended recall items:

- identify the continuous muzzle-control rule;
- identify trigger-finger position during handling;
- identify the relationship between mechanical safety and handling discipline;
- recall the direct-control principle after PROVE;
- identify correct transfer condition: open and unloaded;
- identify the ammunition-marking sources used for compatibility;
- recognize typical range-state commands;
- recall what cease-fire means behaviourally;
- distinguish misfire, hangfire and squib/primer-pop at a conceptual level;
- recall the CFSC 60-second misfire wait rule;
- identify suspected bore obstruction as a no-fire condition.

Do not make the learner memorize firearm action manipulation sequences.

## 8.2 Scenario application

Use short authored vignettes with one or two relevant facts and at least one plausible but unsafe distractor.

Good question types:

- **Choose the safest next decision.**
- **What remains unverified?**
- **Which safety rule is being violated?**
- **What changed that means the firearm’s condition must be re-established?**
- **Should the learner continue, stop, or defer?**
- **What additional information would be required before proceeding?**

Avoid questions asking:

- “Which exact lever do you move first?”
- “How do you clear this specific action?”
- “How do you remove this jam?”
- “How should you grip this firearm?”
- “How do you improve accuracy or speed?”

## 8.3 Failure model

Safety-critical errors should be weighted more heavily than ordinary recall misses.

Examples of critical scenario errors:

- choosing an answer that points a firearm at a person;
- approving trigger contact during handling;
- firing again after a suspected obstruction;
- immediately opening/manipulating after a suspected misfire rather than treating delayed ignition as possible;
- selecting uncertain ammunition by appearance or fit;
- continuing to fire after cease-fire;
- accepting another person’s assurance as a substitute for condition verification.

Argus should require correction/reteaching after these errors rather than merely advancing after showing the answer.

This is still **knowledge remediation**, not a claim of corrected physical behaviour.

## 9. Course-content boundary with Firearm Safety I — ACTS & PROVE

This course should assume Firearm Safety I exists and avoid duplicating it.

### Safety I owns

- the Vital Four ACTS;
- PROVE in order;
- why each element exists;
- universal assumption that every firearm is loaded until PROVEd safe;
- basic distinction between memory of the procedure and hands-on competence.

### Safety II owns

- applying those rules while distracted or during transitions;
- condition changes after loss of direct control;
- passing/receiving;
- loading/unloading **principles**;
- ammunition matching;
- range-state behaviour;
- misfire/hangfire/squib/obstruction stop rules;
- knowing when not to proceed without supervised help.

This keeps the sequence cumulative rather than repetitive.

## 10. Safety and editorial boundaries

### 10.1 No marksmanship

Exclude:

- stance for accuracy;
- sight alignment or sight picture;
- breath control;
- trigger press technique;
- recoil management;
- follow-through;
- target engagement speed;
- shot placement.

Range content exists only to teach safe state/command behaviour.

### 10.2 No tactical or defensive employment

Exclude:

- defensive firearm use;
- concealment;
- drawing/presentation;
- room clearing;
- target prioritization;
- retention techniques;
- movement with a firearm for tactical advantage;
- use-of-force decision making;
- methods for increasing lethality, speed or effectiveness.

### 10.3 No malfunction-clearing instruction

The course may teach that an abnormal condition is a **stop-and-make-safe / seek-qualified-help event**. It should not teach mechanical clearing procedures for stoppages, jams or failures.

### 10.4 No substitute for current law

Storage, display and transportation law belongs in the separate current-law course/research. The 2014 handbook contains legal material that may have changed since publication. Safety II should not use those passages as legal authority.

### 10.5 No substitution for owner manuals

The CFSC handbook itself emphasizes that firearms have unique mechanical features and that detailed operation should be learned from the appropriate owner manual and qualified instruction.

Argus should reinforce that habit.

## 11. Recommended lesson sequence

A compact course should be **8 lessons plus a mixed capstone**, with finite recall embedded between scenarios.

### Lesson 1 — Safety does not expire

**Purpose:** carry ACTS/PROVE into applied handling.

Teach:

- muzzle and trigger discipline remain continuous;
- unloaded status does not cancel handling rules;
- a safety mechanism is supplementary;
- firearm condition knowledge is temporary and tied to direct control.

Practice:

- “what changed?” scenarios after a firearm leaves the learner’s control.

### Lesson 2 — Know the condition, do not infer it

**Purpose:** eliminate shortcut reasoning.

Teach:

- open action is not enough;
- magazine removal is not enough;
- another person’s statement is not enough;
- independent verification is normal, not mistrust.

Practice:

- identify what remains unverified in partial-condition scenarios.

### Lesson 3 — Passing and receiving safely

**Purpose:** apply condition and control at handoff.

Teach:

- pass/accept only open and unloaded firearms;
- receiver establishes condition independently;
- muzzle and trigger discipline continue through the transfer.

Practice:

- judge whether transfers should proceed, stop or be reset.

**Explicit boundary:** physical transfer must be learned and assessed hands-on.

### Lesson 4 — Loading and unloading: the invariants

**Purpose:** teach the safety envelope without mechanical tutorials.

Teach:

- only load for actual use where safe/legal;
- PROVE first;
- check for obstruction;
- maintain muzzle/trigger discipline;
- use correct ammunition;
- verify unloaded condition at the end;
- action-specific details belong to supervised instruction and owner manuals.

Practice:

- identify violated principle in short loading/unloading vignettes.

### Lesson 5 — Ammunition: match, do not guess

**Purpose:** make ammunition compatibility a verification task.

Teach:

- firearm data stamp;
- cartridge/shell head stamp;
- ammunition-box identification;
- physical fit and visual similarity are insufficient;
- uncertainty means stop.

Practice:

- choose between verified, uncertain and clearly inappropriate ammunition situations without requiring broad cartridge memorization.

### Lesson 6 — Range state and cease-fire

**Purpose:** make range commands automatic safety-state changes.

Teach:

- active / cease-fire / no-longer-active concepts;
- range commands/signals vary locally;
- immediate stop on cease-fire;
- unload/open/place safe/step back under standard CFSC procedure;
- no firearm/ammunition handling during cease-fire;
- anyone may call emergency cease-fire.

Practice:

- fast judgement scenarios with distracting unfinished tasks.

### Lesson 7 — Misfire and delayed ignition

**Purpose:** prevent unsafe immediate manipulation after “nothing happened.”

Teach:

- misfire vs hangfire;
- preserve safe direction;
- CFSC 60-second wait rule;
- do not resume normal handling immediately;
- subsequent physical resolution is supervised / firearm-specific.

Practice:

- identify unsafe reactions to a click/no normal discharge.

### Lesson 8 — Abnormal report, squib and obstruction

**Purpose:** teach stop conditions.

Teach:

- abnormal report/recoil can signal a problem;
- suspected obstruction means do not fire again;
- make safe and inspect appropriately;
- uncertain learner defers to qualified help;
- no improvised clearing.

Practice:

- choose stop/escalate rather than continue.

### Capstone — Safe handling judgement

Use 12–16 mixed scenarios where the learner cannot know the topic in advance.

Required scenario coverage:

- loss of direct control;
- unsafe muzzle during conversation;
- trigger contact with safety engaged;
- handoff;
- uncertain ammunition;
- premature loading;
- cease-fire;
- suspected misfire;
- suspected squib/obstruction;
- unfamiliar action requiring deference.

At least two scenarios should have **“stop / insufficient information / get qualified help”** as the best answer.

---

## 12. Recommended size and learning shape

Suggested first implementation:

- **8 lessons**;
- **35–50 finite recall items** total, heavily recycled until stable;
- **30–40 authored scenario items** across lessons;
- **12–16 mixed capstone scenarios**;
- short correction cards after critical safety errors;
- no manipulable 3-D firearm model;
- no timed speed metric for handling decisions;
- no scoring language implying certification or firearm proficiency.

A learner should be able to finish the content in a few sittings, then retain it through spaced scenario review.

The course is valuable precisely because the doctrine is finite. It should not inflate into a general firearms encyclopedia.

## 13. Implementation notes for later course authoring

### Copy style

Use direct safety language:

- “Stop.”
- “Condition is not yet established.”
- “Do not load uncertain ammunition.”
- “Cease-fire means stop immediately.”
- “A safety mechanism does not replace muzzle or trigger discipline.”
- “This manipulation requires supervised hands-on instruction.”

Avoid macho, tactical or identity-based framing.

### Visuals

Useful visuals, if later created, should be diagrammatic and non-operational:

- safe/unsafe muzzle-direction scene diagrams;
- trigger guard with finger clearly outside it;
- generic firearm data-stamp location concept paired with generic cartridge head-stamp concept;
- range-state diagram showing firing line / cease-fire line and “no handling” state;
- condition-knowledge flow: **under direct control and PROVEd → leaves control → condition unknown again**.

Avoid detailed cutaways or action-operating sequences that could become manipulation instruction.

### Question authoring

Every firearm question should pass this test:

> Does the learner need to know **what safety decision applies**, or are we teaching **how to operate the object**?

Only the first belongs in Argus Safety II.

## 14. Explicit non-claims for product copy

The course should display language equivalent to the following before completion:

> **Argus is a study aid, not firearms training.** This course reinforces safety knowledge drawn from Canadian Firearms Safety Course material. It does not replace the CFSC, a qualified instructor, a range officer, a firearm manufacturer’s instructions or supervised hands-on practice. Completing it does not demonstrate that you can safely handle, load, unload, clear, inspect or use a firearm.

And on the completion screen:

> **Completion records knowledge and scenario performance only. It is not a firearms-safety certificate, licence qualification or proof of physical handling competence.**

These are not legalistic footnotes. They are part of the course’s instructional truthfulness.

## 16. Final recommendation

**Ship as a researched course candidate, but keep it explicitly subordinate to formal CFSC instruction.**

The strongest Argus treatment is not “learn how to operate firearms.” It is:

> **retain the Canadian safety rules well enough to notice unsafe assumptions, stop at abnormal conditions, verify instead of guess, and know when hands-on competence is required.**

That produces useful knowledge without pretending that a phone can teach or assess physical firearm handling.

### Recommended Argus sequence

1. **Firearm Safety I — ACTS & PROVE** — universal handling doctrine and why it exists.
2. **Firearm Safety II — Safe Handling Fundamentals** — condition, transfer, ammunition, range state and abnormal-condition judgement.
3. **Firearm Storage, Display & Transportation** — current Canadian legal requirements, versioned separately against current federal law.

### Explicit final non-claim

> **Argus completion demonstrates only recall and authored-scenario judgement. It does not demonstrate firearm-handling competence and must not be presented as equivalent to supervised CFSC instruction, CFSC testing, licensing, range qualification or any other practical firearms credential.**
