# Observation vs Inference — Argus course research

**Issue:** #104  
**Branch:** `research/issue-104-practical-skills-expansion`  
**Status:** deep research / product recommendation  
**Implementation:** explicitly out of scope  
**Shipped:** no (checked 2026-09-25) — the completion claim needs scenario practice; see `../ISSUE_104_SCENARIO_ITEMS.md`  
**Jurisdictional emphasis:** Ontario / Canada  
**Research date:** 2026-09-17

## Recommendation

**Ship, as a foundational Argus practical-psychology course.**

Observation vs Inference is unusually well suited to Argus because it has a finite conceptual core, broad real-world transfer, strong Ontario professional-training support, and a natural scenario-practice format. It is also a useful prerequisite for later courses in situational awareness, interviewing, incident reporting, bias control, and behavioural threat recognition.

The course should not teach that humans can become perfectly objective observers. Perception and memory are selective and reconstructive. The practical competence is narrower and more defensible:

> **Notice when a statement goes beyond the available evidence, preserve the difference between what was observed and what was concluded, express uncertainty explicitly, and seek more information when a conclusion is underdetermined.**

That boundary matters. In Ontario professional material, security guards are instructed to keep reports factual and objective rather than opinion-based or assumption-based; private investigators are trained to record accurate details, draw conclusions carefully, re-evaluate situations, use interview techniques, and avoid discrimination and stereotyping. Justice Canada material on witness interviewing separately emphasizes non-suggestive questioning and avoiding contamination of a witness's independent recollection. The research literature explains why these safeguards matter: confirmation bias, correspondence bias, source-monitoring errors, post-event misinformation, anchoring/availability/representativeness, and miscalibrated confidence can all turn an initial interpretation into something that feels more factual than it is.

This is therefore not a course about suppressing inference. **Inference is necessary. Unlabelled, overconfident or weakly supported inference is the problem.**

# 1. Evidence base

## 1.1 Ontario professional-training material

Ontario's current security-guard syllabus is direct about the distinction Argus wants to teach.

The Ministry of the Solicitor General's [Security guard study guide — report writing](https://www.ontario.ca/page/security-guard-study-guide) states that reports should stick to factual information and observations rather than opinions and assumptions. It also asks the writer to preserve who, what, when, where, why and how, while only describing motives when they are actually evident.

The formal [Security guard training syllabus — Section 4: Report writing](https://www.ontario.ca/document/training-syllabus-security-guards/section-4-report-writing) requires objective, standardized reports; factual information; actions and behaviours; accurate notes; and exercises that detect missing information and bias. Its suggested methodology explicitly contrasts objective reporting with emotional terms and introspection.

The [Security guard training syllabus — Section 3: Basic security procedures](https://www.ontario.ca/document/training-syllabus-security-guards/section-3-basic-security-procedures) adds observation of the physical environment, attention to detail, situational awareness, initial checks that establish a benchmark for later observation, hazard recognition, behavioural assessment and role-play.

Ontario's [Private investigator training syllabus — Section 5: Investigative techniques](https://www.ontario.ca/document/training-syllabus-private-investigators/section-5-investigative-techniques) requires investigators to observe the physical environment, identify unusual behaviour or situations, record relevant details accurately, continually re-evaluate conditions, cross-reference notes and reports, interview witnesses, assess credibility and reliability, and produce objective reports based on factual information. Importantly, it distinguishes observation/reporting work from the later act of drawing conclusions.

The [Private investigator training syllabus — Section 6: Ethical reasoning and decision-making](https://www.ontario.ca/document/training-syllabus-private-investigators/section-6-principles-ethical-reasoning-and-decision-making) explicitly connects situational pattern recognition and anomaly detection with the need to address discrimination, prejudice and stereotyping.

Ontario's 2023 coroner inquest recommendations included a concrete professional version of the same boundary: police reports should be factual, objective and professional, and free from assumptions, speculation, stereotypes and arbitrary editorial views. See [2023 coroner's inquests' verdicts and recommendations](https://www.ontario.ca/page/2023-coroners-inquests-verdicts-and-recommendations).

The Ontario Centre of Forensic Sciences also models explicit epistemic separation. Its [reporting guidance](https://www.ontario.ca/page/reporting-forensic-examination-results-and-opinions) distinguishes analytical results from opinion and requires assumptions, limitations and alternative explanations where relevant. That is a useful professional analogue for Argus: the learner should know whether a statement is a result, an interpretation, an opinion, or an unresolved limitation.

## 1.2 Ontario human-rights boundary

The Ontario Human Rights Commission's [racial profiling guidance](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/what-racial-profiling-fact-sheet) defines racial profiling as safety or security action that relies on stereotypes about race, colour, ethnicity, ancestry, religion or place of origin rather than reasonable suspicion. The OHRC contrasts this with attention to actual behaviour or specific information about suspected activity.

This is directly relevant to the course. A learner must not be rewarded for converting demographic appearance, accent, dress, disability, religion, perceived class, homelessness, mental-health presentation or other identity-linked characteristics into a threat inference. The course should repeatedly separate:

- **observable behaviour:** "the person tried three locked doors in succession";
- **identity or appearance:** "the person wore religious clothing";
- **inference:** "the person may be looking for an unlocked entrance";
- **unsupported profiling:** "the person looks like someone who would steal."

The first can be relevant. The second is usually irrelevant to a safety inference. The third is a hypothesis to test. The fourth is not acceptable reasoning.

## 1.3 Canadian interviewing and memory guidance

Justice Canada's [Interview techniques](https://justice.canada.ca/eng/rp-pr/jr/tiprhtc-rptctctp/p8.html) review identifies the cognitive interview as a well-supported method for obtaining fuller witness recall while preserving accuracy. Its practical implication for Argus is not to teach a professional forensic interview, but to show why a witness's first-person account should be elicited before an interviewer supplies interpretations.

The federal-provincial-territorial [Working Group on the Prevention of Miscarriages of Justice](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/rp-pr/cj-jp/ccr-rc/pmj-pej/p5.html) specifically warns that investigators can colour a witness's account by communicating their own assessment of a case or accused. Its recommendations favour exhausting a witness's own recollection before introducing conflicting evidence and using non-suggestive questioning.

This supports a key course rule:

> **When gathering information from another person, do not hide your inference inside the question.**

Compare:

- weaker: "Why was the man acting suspiciously?"
- stronger: "What did you see him do?"
- weaker: "Did she look angry when she left?"
- stronger: "What did you notice about her words, voice or actions when she left?"

The stronger version preserves the witness's observation rather than recruiting the witness into the interviewer's interpretation.

## 1.4 Psychology and judgement literature

This course has a strong research basis, but the evidence does **not** justify claiming that an app can make perception unbiased.

Key findings relevant to the course:

- Nickerson's review, ["Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many Guises"](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175), describes the tendency to seek or interpret evidence in ways that favour an existing belief or hypothesis. Once a learner decides "this person is suspicious" or "this client is resistant," later ambiguous details can be assimilated to that story.
- Gilbert and Malone's ["The Correspondence Bias"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7870861/) describes the tendency to infer enduring personal traits from behaviour that may be explained by the situation. "He avoided eye contact" does not establish "he is dishonest." "She answered abruptly" does not establish "she is hostile."
- Tversky and Kahneman's ["Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17835457/) describes predictable errors associated with representativeness, availability and anchoring. A vivid resemblance to a familiar pattern can feel more diagnostic than it is, and an initial theory can anchor later interpretation.
- Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay's [source-monitoring framework](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8346328/) addresses errors in identifying where remembered information came from. This is important when a person later cannot distinguish what they directly observed from what they were told, inferred, read, or reconstructed.
- The misinformation literature shows that post-event information can alter later reports. Elizabeth Loftus's 2026 retrospective, ["The history of an idea: The misinformation effect"](https://doi.org/10.1111/lcrp.70020), summarizes a half-century of work showing that misleading information introduced after an event can enter later memory reports.
- Fleming's 2024 review, ["Metacognition and Confidence: A Review and Synthesis"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37722748/), is relevant to explicit uncertainty. Confidence is itself an inference and can diverge from task performance. Argus should therefore teach confidence as information to calibrate, not as proof that a conclusion is true.
- Bond and DePaulo's meta-analysis, ["Accuracy of Deception Judgments"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16859438/), found average unaided lie/truth judgement only modestly above chance. This is useful corrective evidence against turning gaze, fidgeting, nervousness or apparent confidence into "he is lying" conclusions.

The practical synthesis is simple: **observe first, preserve source, infer explicitly, test alternatives, and calibrate confidence.**

# 2. Competency boundary

## 2.1 What Argus should teach

The learner should be able to:

- distinguish a directly reported observation from an interpretation, inference and assumption;
- identify when a statement contains more certainty than the evidence supports;
- preserve the source of information: seen, heard, measured, reported by another person, inferred, or unknown;
- convert loaded or conclusory descriptions into observable detail;
- state a reasonable inference as a hypothesis rather than a fact;
- generate at least one plausible alternative explanation when evidence is ambiguous;
- identify the additional information that would discriminate between competing explanations;
- choose **insufficient information** when the presented evidence does not justify a conclusion;
- keep identity stereotypes and diagnostic labels out of safety and threat judgements;
- produce short, chronological, objective incident descriptions;
- ask information-gathering questions that do not embed the interviewer's preferred inference.

## 2.2 What Argus should not claim to teach

Completion must not imply that the learner:

- is an investigator, interviewer, security professional, consultant, clinician or threat assessor;
- can reliably determine motive, intent, deception, dangerousness or mental state from appearance or behaviour;
- can make a professional violence-risk assessment;
- has become free of cognitive bias;
- can accurately reconstruct events that were not attended to or remembered;
- can replace contemporaneous notes, evidence, corroboration or professional procedures with intuition;
- can treat an inference as fact because it later turned out to be correct.

Correct guesses are still guesses if the evidence available at the time did not support them.

# 3. The teachable distinction

Argus should teach five related categories. They should be treated as a reasoning vocabulary, not as mutually exclusive metaphysical categories.

| Category | Operational definition for Argus | Example | Main error to prevent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Observation** | Information directly perceived or recorded, described as specifically as the evidence permits. | "At 18:42 he pulled the locked north door twice, then walked to the west entrance." | Adding motive, character or emotional labels. |
| **Interpretation** | A meaning or category assigned to an observation. | "He appeared frustrated." | Presenting a label as if it were the raw event. |
| **Inference** | A conclusion that goes beyond direct observation and is supported to some degree by evidence. | "He may not know which entrance is open." | Treating a plausible explanation as the only explanation. |
| **Assumption** | A premise being treated as true without adequate support or without being made explicit. | "He works here, so he must have authorization." | Letting an untested premise silently drive the conclusion. |
| **Uncertainty** | What is not known, how confident the learner should be, and what additional evidence could change the conclusion. | "We do not know why he tried the doors; ask whether he needs assistance." | Forcing a definite answer when the evidence is underdetermined. |

Two nuances should be explicit.

First, **observation is not a perfect recording of reality**. Attention is selective, perception is interpreted, and memory can change. "Observation" in this course means the closest available description to what the learner directly perceived or what an instrument recorded, while preserving uncertainty and source.

Second, **interpretation and inference are not forbidden**. They are indispensable to useful judgement. The requirement is to mark the transition from evidence to meaning.

# 4. Recommended Argus framework

Do not teach a popular management diagram as if it were a scientific law. Argus can instead use a transparent, source-backed reasoning sequence of its own.

## 4.1 The five-step reasoning sequence

### 1. Record

What was actually seen, heard, measured, recorded or reported?

Use concrete verbs, chronology, quantities and quotations where useful.

### 2. Separate

Which parts of the account are:

- direct observation;
- another person's report;
- interpretation;
- inference;
- assumption;
- unknown?

### 3. Explain

What are the plausible explanations?

A strong answer need not generate many alternatives. In most Argus scenarios, two materially different hypotheses are enough to expose premature closure.

### 4. Calibrate

How strongly does the evidence support the conclusion?

Use a small vocabulary rather than fake precision:

- established by the presented evidence;
- supported but not established;
- possible;
- insufficient information;
- contradicted by the presented evidence.

### 5. Test or act

What additional observation, question, record or safe action would reduce uncertainty?

In safety scenarios, the learner does not need certainty before taking a proportionate precaution. The distinction is between **uncertainty about explanation** and **certainty required for action**.

Example:

> Observation: smoke is visible under an electrical-room door.  
> Inference: there may be a fire or electrical fault.  
> Unknown: exact cause.  
> Action: treat the hazard seriously and follow emergency procedure; do not wait to establish the cause.

The course must not accidentally teach "do nothing until certain."

# 5. Concepts worth teaching

## 5.1 Description before conclusion

A description that can be checked is more useful than a character judgement.

- "He raised his voice and said, 'Give it back now'" is more useful than "he became aggressive."
- "She looked at her phone during four of five questions and answered after several seconds" is more useful than "she was disengaged."
- "The customer moved within roughly one metre after being asked twice to stay back" is more useful than "the customer was threatening."

Labels can be added later if the task requires them, but the observable substrate should remain available.

## 5.2 Source preservation

Learners should mark where a fact came from.

Compare:

- "The vehicle struck the post at 14:10." — claims direct/event knowledge.
- "I saw the vehicle strike the post at approximately 14:10." — preserves observer and uncertainty.
- "A bystander told me the vehicle struck the post." — preserves hearsay source.
- "The damage pattern is consistent with the vehicle striking the post." — preserves inference.

This is particularly important after multiple conversations, where source-monitoring errors can blur direct experience, later information and inference.

## 5.3 Behaviour is not motive

The course should repeatedly challenge motive attribution.

Observation:

> "The person looked behind them four times while walking one block."

Possible explanations include:

- checking whether someone is following;
- looking for a friend;
- checking traffic;
- anxiety;
- distraction;
- no meaningful reason.

"Conducting counter-surveillance" is a much stronger inference and is not established by the observation alone.

## 5.4 Emotion labels are interpretations

"Angry," "nervous," "calm," "evasive," "hostile," "confused" and "aggressive" can be useful shorthand, but they are not raw observations.

Argus should ask learners to recover the underlying evidence:

- voice volume;
- words used;
- pace of speech;
- repeated movements;
- distance changes;
- facial expression;
- response latency;
- actions.

Even then, emotion should often remain an interpretation: "appeared upset" rather than "was angry."

## 5.5 Confidence is not evidence

A confident witness, colleague or learner may be correct, but confidence alone does not establish accuracy. Argus should score the evidential basis of the answer, not rhetorical certainty.

## 5.6 Alternative hypotheses prevent premature closure

The course should normalize the question:

> **What else could explain this?**

The point is not endless scepticism. The point is to interrupt confirmation bias before a first story becomes the only story.

## 5.7 Evidence can justify action without proving intent

Street-awareness and threat scenarios need this distinction.

If someone repeatedly closes distance after clear requests to stop, the learner may choose distance, exit, assistance or emergency action based on observable behaviour without claiming to know the person's motive, diagnosis or future intention.

A proportionate safety response can be justified by behaviour and context even when motive remains uncertain.

# 6. Applications

## 6.1 Incident reporting

This is the clearest Ontario-supported application.

### Weak

> A suspicious male became aggressive and tried to break in.

Problems:

- "suspicious" is an interpretation without supporting behaviour;
- "aggressive" is a label without observable detail;
- "tried to break in" assigns intent;
- the description contains no chronology, source or specific action.

### Stronger

> At approximately 21:14, I saw an adult pull the locked east entrance handle three times, look through the glass, then strike the door once with an open hand. I did not see the person damage the door or use a tool. I could not determine why they were attempting the entrance.

This account preserves what was observed and explicitly refuses an unsupported motive inference.

## 6.2 Interviewing

The course should train learners to detect inference embedded in questions.

### Weak

> Why did the driver speed away after he hit the cyclist?

This assumes both collision and intent to flee.

### Stronger sequence

> What did you see the vehicle do?  
> What happened next?  
> Which part did you see directly?  
> What makes you think the driver knew there had been a collision?

The final question can examine the witness's inference after the observation has been preserved.

## 6.3 Consulting

Consulting work often turns observations into diagnoses too quickly.

### Weak

> The client is resistant to change.

Possible observations behind that claim might be:

- two leaders challenged the timeline;
- attendance at training was 54%;
- a team continued using an old process;
- three requested decisions remain unresolved.

Those facts do not all imply the same explanation.

### Stronger

> Adoption is lower than planned: 54% of the target group attended training, three decisions remain unresolved, and the legacy process is still in use in two teams. Possible explanations include capacity constraints, unresolved design questions, weak sponsorship, or resistance. Current evidence does not distinguish among them.

The next move is to seek discriminating evidence rather than naming a personality or culture problem prematurely.

## 6.4 Street and transit awareness

Scenarios should focus on observable movement, access, distance, hazards and changes in context, not on "spotting bad people."

### Scenario

On a subway platform, a person changes position twice and is now standing closer to you. They are also looking repeatedly toward the stairs.

Weak conclusion:

> They are preparing to rob me.

Better reasoning:

> The closing distance is observable. The repeated looks toward the stairs are observable. Intent is unknown. Move to a more comfortable position or nearer other people if desired and continue to reassess.

Argus can reward a proportionate precaution while still marking motive as **insufficient information**.

## 6.5 Threat assessment

This course should be a prerequisite to, not a substitute for, a later behavioural-threat course.

The Canadian [Public Safety insider-risk guidance](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/nhncng-crtcl-nfrstrctr/index-en.aspx) states that there is no particular profile that readily identifies a malicious insider and emphasizes behaviour. The FBI's behavioural-threat guidance likewise states that no single behaviour predicts targeted violence and that assessment depends on multiple facts, context and professional judgement; see [Behavioral Analysis](https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-investigate/behavioral-analysis).

For Argus, the lesson is narrow:

- report or respond to **behaviour and context**;
- do not infer dangerousness from identity or appearance;
- do not treat one ambiguous behaviour as predictive;
- do not diagnose mental illness;
- do not infer a violent plan because someone looks angry, nervous or unusual;
- do not require certainty before taking an ordinary safety precaution or reporting genuinely concerning behaviour through appropriate channels.

# 7. Common cognitive errors and misconceptions

## 7.1 "If my inference is reasonable, it is an observation"

False. A reasonable inference may be useful and well supported, but it remains an inference.

## 7.2 "Inference is bad"

False. Useful decisions require inference. The course should reward explicit, evidence-proportional inference.

## 7.3 "Objective reporting means never using judgement"

False. Selection, relevance and interpretation require judgement. Objective reporting means preserving the evidential basis and avoiding unsupported editorial claims.

## 7.4 Confirmation bias

Once a hypothesis is formed, ambiguous details may be interpreted as support. Argus should ask for a potentially disconfirming observation or alternative explanation.

## 7.5 Correspondence bias / dispositional overreach

A single behaviour is converted into a stable trait:

- late -> unreliable;
- quiet -> deceptive;
- direct -> hostile;
- nervous -> guilty;
- disorganized -> intoxicated.

The learner should consider situational explanations before inferring character.

## 7.6 Representativeness

Something resembles a familiar pattern, so the learner treats it as belonging to that category without enough evidence.

"This looks like shoplifting behaviour" should trigger the question: **which specific behaviour, and what else would fit?**

## 7.7 Availability

A recent alarming event makes a similar explanation feel more likely. After hearing about transit theft, an ambiguous proximity change may be interpreted as theft preparation.

## 7.8 Anchoring

An early label controls later interpretation. Once a person is called "agitated," later behaviour is read through that frame.

## 7.9 Hindsight conversion

After the outcome is known, earlier ambiguous behaviour can look obvious and predictive. Course feedback should assess what the learner could justify **at the decision point**, not what became true later.

## 7.10 Source confusion

The learner remembers a detail but not whether it was seen, heard from a witness, shown in a later photo or inferred. Scenario questions should sometimes ask not only **what is known** but **how it is known**.

## 7.11 Mind-reading

Claims about motive, emotion or intent are treated as direct perception.

- "He wanted to intimidate me."
- "She knew the answer was false."
- "He was casing the building."

These may be hypotheses. They are not directly observable.

## 7.12 Body-language certainty

The course should explicitly reject "one cue = one meaning" folklore. Bond and DePaulo's deception meta-analysis found unaided lie/truth judgements only modestly above chance; later reviews likewise find non-verbal deception cues weak and unreliable. Eye contact, fidgeting, posture or nervousness should not be taught as lie detectors.

## 7.13 "Insufficient information" as cowardice

This is the most important misconception for Argus to reverse.

When evidence does not discriminate between materially different explanations, "insufficient information" is a **correct conclusion**, not a failure to decide.

# 8. Scenario types

The course should use short authored scenes with enough detail to make some conclusions supportable and others underdetermined.

## 8.1 Classification scenes

Present one sentence at a time and classify the statement:

- observation;
- interpretation;
- inference;
- assumption;
- uncertainty / unknown.

These are suitable for early learning but should not dominate the course.

## 8.2 Rewrite scenes

Convert a loaded statement into observable language.

Prompt:

> Rewrite: "The customer was acting threateningly."

Available scene evidence:

> The customer stepped toward the counter twice, pointed at the employee, raised their voice, and said, "You're going to fix this now."

A strong answer preserves the behaviours and quotation.

## 8.3 Evidence-to-hypothesis scenes

Show several observations and ask which conclusions are:

- established;
- supported but not established;
- possible;
- not supported;
- impossible to determine from the evidence.

## 8.4 Competing-explanation scenes

Require the learner to identify at least one plausible alternative explanation.

Example:

> A meeting participant repeatedly checks the clock and gives short answers.

Possible hypotheses:

- disengaged;
- late for another commitment;
- anxious;
- distracted by an urgent problem;
- naturally concise.

The correct answer may be that the evidence does not distinguish among them.

## 8.5 Source-tracking scenes

Mix:

- direct observation;
- another person's statement;
- a later message;
- a photograph;
- an inference.

Ask the learner to reconstruct which claims came from which source.

## 8.6 Interview-question scenes

Choose the question least likely to contaminate the account.

Example:

Witness: "Then he went toward the exit."

Choices:

- "Why was he trying to escape?"
- "Was he running because he knew police were coming?"
- "What did you see him do as he went toward the exit?"
- "He looked nervous, right?"

The third preserves the witness's account.

## 8.7 Incident-report scenes

Give unordered facts and ask for a concise chronological report. Ontario's security-guard syllabus already uses essentially this learning shape.

## 8.8 Street/transit scenes

Use ordinary urban situations:

- someone repeatedly changing distance;
- an unattended bag near a doorway;
- a person testing multiple entrances;
- a platform argument increasing in volume;
- a vehicle circling a pickup area;
- a person appearing to follow the learner across two turns;
- a blocked exit;
- a sudden environmental change.

The learner must distinguish **observable reason for attention** from **unsupported story about intent**.

## 8.9 Consulting/work scenes

Use:

- low adoption data;
- missed deadlines;
- meeting behaviour;
- customer complaints;
- process variance;
- conflicting stakeholder accounts.

Ask what is fact, what is interpretation, and what evidence would test the proposed diagnosis.

## 8.10 Threat-recognition boundary scenes

Present behaviour that may justify reporting or a precaution but does not establish future violence.

The learner should be able to select:

> "The behaviour is concerning enough to report/reassess, but the person's motive and future action cannot be determined from this information."

That is exactly the reasoning shape later behavioural-threat content should build on.

# 9. How Argus should reward "insufficient information"

This is a core product requirement, not an edge case.

## 9.1 Treat it as a first-class answer

"Insufficient information" must appear in the same visual and scoring position as substantive conclusions. It should not be a timid escape hatch such as "none of the above."

It should be correct when:

- two or more materially different explanations remain compatible with the evidence;
- a motive, intent, diagnosis or trait is asked for but only behaviour is provided;
- the source of a claim is unknown or contaminated;
- an essential discriminating fact is absent;
- the question asks for more precision than the observation permits.

## 9.2 Do not make uncertainty always correct

Otherwise learners will game the course by refusing to infer.

"Insufficient information" should be wrong when the supplied evidence directly establishes the proposition or strongly supports the requested level of conclusion.

Example:

> Video shows a person pick up a red bag from seat 4 and carry it out of the room.

Question: "Did the person carry the red bag out of the room?"

"Insufficient information" is wrong.

Question: "Why did the person take the bag?"

"Insufficient information" may be correct unless other evidence is supplied.

## 9.3 Score overclaiming and underclaiming symmetrically

Argus should teach calibration, not chronic scepticism.

- **Overclaim:** chooses a definite explanation that exceeds the evidence.
- **Underclaim:** chooses insufficient information despite decisive evidence.
- **Calibrated answer:** matches the strength of the conclusion to the evidence.

## 9.4 Reward the missing-information question

Where feasible, a full-credit uncertainty answer should include what information would help.

Example:

> "Insufficient information to infer intent. A direct question, access record, or observation of what the person does next would help distinguish an access problem from attempted unauthorized entry."

This converts uncertainty into active information seeking.

## 9.5 Confidence should modify feedback, not become false precision

For later scenario practice, Argus can ask for low / medium / high confidence.

Feedback should be stricter when a learner is confidently wrong than when they are tentatively wrong, because the course aims to improve calibration. Do not require numeric probability estimates unless a later Argus-wide confidence model is deliberately designed.

# 10. Scoring rubric

A scenario should be scored on reasoning quality, not merely the final label.

Recommended 10-point rubric:

| Dimension | Points | Full-credit behaviour |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| **Evidence separation** | 0–3 | Correctly distinguishes observation/source from interpretation or inference. |
| **Support calibration** | 0–2 | Conclusion strength matches the evidence; uses insufficient information when warranted. |
| **Alternative explanation** | 0–1 | Recognizes a materially plausible alternative where ambiguity exists. |
| **Missing information / next test** | 0–1 | Identifies information that would reduce the relevant uncertainty. |
| **Source/chronology precision** | 0–1 | Preserves who observed/reported what and the relevant sequence. |
| **Bias / profiling discipline** | 0–1 | Does not use identity stereotypes, diagnosis or unsupported character claims. |
| **Proportionate action distinction** | 0–1 | Separates uncertainty about explanation from a justified safety/reporting action. |

### Critical-error override

A learner should not pass a scenario merely by accumulating points if they commit a severe reasoning error such as:

- treating race, religion, ethnicity, disability or another protected/identity characteristic as evidence of criminal or violent intent;
- asserting a mental-health diagnosis from ordinary behaviour;
- claiming a single body-language cue establishes deception;
- inventing a directly observed fact that was not present in the scenario;
- converting "possible" into "certain" when the course explicitly withheld the necessary evidence.

These should trigger corrective feedback and a retry rather than punitive language.

# 11. Examples of strong and weak reasoning

## Example A — building entrance

**Evidence:** A person pulls two locked doors, looks through the glass, checks their phone, then walks to a third entrance.

**Weak:** "They are casing the building."

**Why weak:** Assigns criminal preparation without discriminating evidence.

**Strong:** "They tried two locked entrances and moved to a third. They may be looking for an open entrance; intent is not established. Continue observing or ask whether they need assistance if appropriate."

## Example B — interview

**Evidence:** A witness says, "She came out quickly and got into the car."

**Weak:** "She fled the scene."

**Why weak:** "Fled" imports motive and urgency.

**Strong:** "The witness reports that she came out quickly and got into the car. Whether she was trying to flee is not established by that statement alone."

## Example C — consulting

**Evidence:** Project attendance fell after a schedule change; two team leads also raised concerns about workload.

**Weak:** "The organization is resistant to transformation."

**Why weak:** Stable trait/culture conclusion from limited situational evidence.

**Strong:** "Participation fell after the schedule change, and two leads reported workload concerns. Capacity or scheduling is one plausible explanation; current evidence does not establish broader resistance."

## Example D — apparent nervousness

**Evidence:** A person avoids eye contact, rubs their hands and pauses before answering.

**Weak:** "They're lying."

**Why weak:** Non-verbal deception cues are weak and these behaviours have many explanations.

**Strong:** "They showed gaze aversion, hand rubbing and delayed responses. Those observations do not establish deception. Verify the account against independent information."

## Example E — safety under uncertainty

**Evidence:** On a quiet platform, a stranger changes direction when you do and continues closing distance after you move away once.

**Weak:** "They are definitely following me, so I should confront them."

**Also weak:** "I cannot prove anything, so there is no reason to act."

**Strong:** "The repeated direction change and continued closing distance are observable. Intent is uncertain. A proportionate response is to move toward other people/staff or leave the setting while continuing to reassess."

The teaching target is neither paranoia nor passivity.

# 12. Finite recall versus scenario practice

## 12.1 Finite recall material

This course has a small stable knowledge layer:

- observation;
- interpretation;
- inference;
- assumption;
- uncertainty;
- source preservation;
- confidence is not evidence;
- behaviour is not motive;
- one cue rarely establishes intent or deception;
- alternative hypotheses;
- confirmation bias;
- correspondence bias;
- representativeness;
- availability;
- anchoring;
- source-monitoring error;
- misinformation / contamination;
- objective-reporting principles;
- non-suggestive information gathering;
- behaviour/context over demographic profiling.

This can be learned with ordinary recall/check interactions.

## 12.2 Scenario-practice material

The competence itself requires application:

- classify mixed statements;
- rewrite loaded descriptions;
- choose calibrated conclusions;
- detect unsupported motive attribution;
- select "insufficient information";
- generate alternatives;
- choose a missing-information question;
- preserve information source;
- avoid leading interview questions;
- write short objective incident summaries;
- distinguish a justified precaution from an unsupported threat conclusion.

A flashcard-only course would miss the central skill.

# 13. Course design

## Recommended size

This should be a **small foundational course: 5 lessons + 2 checkpoints + 1 mixed final scenario set**.

Target authored inventory:

- about **18–24 finite knowledge items**;
- about **28–36 short scenarios** across the course;
- **8–12 final mixed scenarios** sampled across reporting, interviewing, consulting and public-space awareness;
- enough scenario variants that "insufficient information" is not positionally or semantically predictable.

The course should be comfortably smaller than a professional report-writing or investigative-interviewing programme. Its job is to establish a transferable reasoning habit.

## Lesson 1 — What did you actually observe?

Teach:

- observation versus interpretation;
- concrete behavioural description;
- source preservation;
- observation is fallible, not a camera recording.

Practice:

- statement classification;
- rewrite labels into observable detail.

## Lesson 2 — From observation to inference

Teach:

- inference versus assumption;
- reasonable inference versus established fact;
- behaviour is not motive;
- alternative explanations.

Practice:

- evidence-to-hypothesis scenes;
- competing explanations.

## Checkpoint 1 — Evidence separation

Short mixed classification and rewrite set. No threat material yet.

## Lesson 3 — Bias and memory

Teach:

- confirmation bias;
- correspondence bias;
- representativeness, availability and anchoring;
- source-monitoring error;
- post-event misinformation;
- confidence is not proof.

Practice:

- contaminated timeline;
- source tracking;
- hindsight trap;
- body-language overreach.

## Lesson 4 — Reporting and interviewing

Teach:

- factual/objective report principles from Ontario training;
- chronology and source;
- fact versus conclusion;
- open/non-suggestive questions;
- witness account before interviewer theory.

Practice:

- incident report repair;
- select the least-leading follow-up;
- distinguish witness statement from interviewer inference.

## Checkpoint 2 — Reporting under uncertainty

A short incident with incomplete evidence. Learner must produce a calibrated description and explicitly mark what remains unknown.

## Lesson 5 — Decisions under uncertainty

Teach:

- "insufficient information" as a correct answer;
- overclaim versus underclaim;
- information that would discriminate between hypotheses;
- action thresholds can differ from certainty thresholds;
- behaviour/context over profile;
- safety action without unsupported motive attribution.

Practice:

- transit/public-space scenes;
- consulting diagnosis scenes;
- low-level threat-recognition boundary scenes.

## Final — Mixed application

Use 8–12 scenarios in which the learner must repeatedly decide:

1. what is observed;
2. what is inferred;
3. what remains unknown;
4. whether a conclusion is sufficiently supported;
5. what additional information would help;
6. whether a proportionate action is justified despite uncertainty.

At least two final scenarios should have **insufficient information** as the best conclusion, and at least two should contain enough evidence that choosing insufficient information is an error. This prevents a blanket uncertainty strategy.

---

# 14. Completion model

Recommended completion standard:

- demonstrate recall of the five-category distinction and core bias concepts;
- achieve at least **80% overall** on the final mixed scenario set;
- achieve at least **75% on evidence-separation items**;
- correctly handle at least **80% of deliberately underdetermined items**, including selecting insufficient information when warranted;
- commit **no unresolved critical profiling/diagnosis/fabrication error** in the final set;
- allow retries with changed scenario details rather than repeating an identical answer pattern.

The exact thresholds are product choices rather than scientific constants. The important design requirement is that course completion depends on calibrated scenario reasoning, not memorizing definitions.

---

# 15. Safety and anti-profiling boundaries

This course should have explicit editorial constraints.

- Never teach race, ethnicity, religion, nationality, accent, disability, homelessness, gender expression, clothing style or socioeconomic appearance as threat cues.
- Do not teach diagnostic labels from casual observation.
- Do not imply that nervousness, gaze aversion, fidgeting, confidence, unusual affect or social awkwardness establishes deception or dangerousness.
- Do not reward learners for guessing intent when the evidence supports only behaviour.
- Do not frame ordinary public-space observation as covert surveillance training.
- Do not teach pursuit, confrontation, restraint, weapons use or tactical engagement.
- When a scenario contains an immediate safety issue, prioritize ordinary emergency guidance and proportionate disengagement/reporting over analysis.
- Preserve the distinction between noticing a behaviour and deciding what it means.
- Preserve the distinction between a reasonable precaution and a claim about someone's character or future conduct.

A useful recurring feedback line is:

> **You can respond to the behaviour without pretending to know the motive.**

# 16. Product treatment in Argus

This should be treated as a **foundation course for scenario judgement**, not as an advanced professional specialty.

Recommended relationships:

- **Situational Awareness I** should introduce baseline/anomaly/reassessment; Observation vs Inference then teaches epistemic discipline about what those anomalies do and do not mean.
- **Interviewing I** should assume the learner can separate a witness's statement from the interviewer's interpretation.
- **Objective incident reporting** can either remain embedded here or become a later focused writing course.
- **Threat Recognition I** should require this course or repeat its central rules: behaviour, context, multiple indicators, no demographic profile, no single cue, uncertainty remains explicit.
- **Bias control in behavioural judgement** can deepen the psychology later; this course needs only the biases that directly affect evidence separation.

If Argus eventually supports course prerequisites, this is a strong candidate to sit early in the human/field-skills sequence.

# 18. Proposed course structure

**Course:** Observation vs Inference  
**Family:** Practical psychology / field skills  
**Prerequisite:** none; recommended after or alongside Situational Awareness I  
**Format:** finite recall + authored scenario application  
**Size:** 5 lessons, 2 checkpoints, 1 mixed final; approximately 18–24 knowledge items and 36–48 scenario items including final variants  
**Primary transfer domains:** incident reporting, interviewing, consulting, street/transit awareness, later behavioural-threat assessment  
**Explicit non-goals:** deception detection, mental-health diagnosis, profiling, professional investigation certification, violence prediction

Sequence:

1. **Observation, interpretation and source** — describe what was actually perceived or reported.
2. **Inference, assumption and alternatives** — make conclusions explicit and test competing explanations.
3. **Bias, memory and confidence** — confirmation, correspondence, anchoring/availability/representativeness, source confusion and misinformation.
4. **Reporting and interviewing** — objective chronology, source preservation and non-suggestive questions.
5. **Uncertainty and proportionate action** — reward insufficient information, identify the next discriminating fact, and act safely without pretending to know motive.
6. **Mixed final** — cross-domain scenarios requiring evidence separation, calibrated inference and uncertainty handling.

# Exact completion claim

> **Completed Observation vs Inference:** can distinguish direct observations and reported information from interpretations, inferences and assumptions in authored scenarios; preserve source and chronology; identify plausible alternatives and missing information; choose **insufficient information** when the evidence does not justify a conclusion; and produce concise, behaviour-based descriptions without demographic profiling or unsupported claims about motive, deception, diagnosis or threat.

This completion claim is intentionally narrower than professional field competence. It demonstrates successful performance on Argus-authored reasoning scenarios, not certification as an investigator, interviewer, security professional, consultant or threat assessor.
