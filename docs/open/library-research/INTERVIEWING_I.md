# Interviewing I — Cooperative Information Gathering

**Status:** deep research / course design only  
**Date:** 2026-09-17  
**Issue:** #104  
**Implementation:** explicitly out of scope  
**Shipped:** no (checked 2026-09-25) — the completion claim needs scenario practice; see `../ISSUE_104_SCENARIO_ITEMS.md`  
**Jurisdictional anchor:** Ontario, Canada  
**Course family:** field and human skills / interviewing and memory

## Recommendation

**Ship as a scenario-first foundational course, with a deliberately narrow competence claim.**

Ontario's required private-investigator material already establishes interviewing as a practical professional skill rather than a trivia subject. The current training syllabus requires detailed knowledge of interview techniques, distinguishes interviewing from interrogating, names narrative and cognitive interviews, requires note-taking and statement-taking procedures, and recommends demonstration plus coached role-play. The companion study guide adds several useful operational fundamentals: know the purpose of the interview, prepare before starting, let a cooperative witness speak without interruption, take notes, and complete the investigative report immediately afterward.

The psychology literature supports the same basic direction but adds important constraints. Human memory is reconstructive and susceptible to post-event misinformation. Interviewer wording can introduce information. Free narrative and open, witness-compatible prompts reduce interviewer input, but no question format guarantees accuracy. Later follow-up can elicit useful new information while also producing less accurate detail than the initial account. A good course therefore cannot teach a formula for extracting "the truth." It should teach a disciplined way to obtain an account while minimizing avoidable contamination and preserving uncertainty.

The appropriate Argus course is **Interviewing I — Cooperative Information Gathering**: preparation, open narrative, neutral follow-up, clarification, uncertainty, source separation, contemporaneous notes, and the boundary between interviewing and interrogation.

It should **not** teach coercive interrogation, confession elicitation, suspect-pressure tactics, deception detection from body language, formal child-forensic interviewing, or the full Cognitive Interview as though an app lesson created professional competence.

---

## 1. Competency boundary

The course should train one bounded competency:

> **Obtain and preserve a cooperative person's account with minimal interviewer contamination.**

That decomposes into observable sub-skills:

- define the purpose and information objectives before questioning;
- invite an initial account with a broad, neutral prompt;
- avoid unnecessary interruption while the person is giving that account;
- use follow-up questions based on details the person has already introduced;
- move from broad prompts to specific clarification only when needed;
- avoid questions that supply an answer, assume an unreported fact, or import another source's account;
- clarify ambiguous references, sequence and source of knowledge;
- accept and record uncertainty rather than forcing precision;
- distinguish what the interviewee said from what the interviewer inferred;
- create prompt, objective notes and preserve important qualifiers;
- recognize when the interaction has crossed outside cooperative information gathering and should not be improvised into an interrogation.

### What completion must not imply

Completion does **not** establish that the learner can:

- conduct a police or criminal-suspect interrogation;
- conduct a legally sufficient custodial interview;
- take a formal statement in every Ontario legal or employment context;
- perform the full Cognitive Interview competently;
- conduct specialist forensic interviews with children or vulnerable witnesses;
- determine whether a person is truthful from demeanour, confidence or body language;
- prevent memory error or recover an objectively complete memory;
- replace professional training, supervision, organizational policy or legal advice.

The product claim must remain about performance in **authored cooperative-interview scenarios**.

## 2. Ontario professional baseline

### 2.1 Ontario private-investigator syllabus

Ontario's current *Training syllabus for private investigators* is the primary local authority for course scope. Section 5, Investigative Techniques, places interviewing alongside evidence handling, surveillance and report writing and requires detailed knowledge of:

- the difference between interviewing and interrogating;
- narrative versus cognitive interviews;
- civil versus criminal interviews;
- note-taking procedures;
- statement-taking protocols and law relating to statements;
- communication theory;
- assessment of witness credibility, reliability and suitability.

The syllabus recommends instruction, demonstration, role-play with coaching and feedback, and assessment through role-play. That learning shape fits Argus's proposed scenario system much better than a recall-only deck.

Section 7, Key Principles of Communication and Interaction, adds active listening, note-taking, avoiding personal bias/opinion, probing questions, accurate communication, rapport and role-play. Those requirements reinforce the idea that the skill is interactive and applied.

The ministry's syllabus page states that Ontario is modernizing the Private Security and Investigative Services Act framework and developing new standardized basic-training curricula for future implementation, while existing requirements remain in force. Any later implementation issue should therefore re-check the Ontario sources before freezing course copy.

### 2.2 Ontario study-guide practices worth retaining

The ministry's current private-investigator study guide gives a practical interview sequence that is useful as a local starting point:

- know the purpose of the interview;
- review existing information and determine what information is still needed;
- prepare questions and materials;
- identify yourself and explain the interaction appropriately;
- once the person begins talking, do not interrupt unnecessarily;
- take notes without allowing note-taking to dominate the interaction;
- close courteously;
- write the investigative report immediately after the interview;
- maintain thorough and accurate notes because they may later support recollection and reporting.

For statements, the guide emphasizes preserving the witness's recollection and giving the witness an opportunity to review and correct a formal written statement before signing it.

### 2.3 What Argus should not inherit uncritically

The Ontario syllabus is occupational training, not a complete contemporary witness-memory standard. It names topics such as statement analysis, profiling and credibility assessment without providing the scientific limits that an Argus course should require.

Argus should therefore interpret "credibility" conservatively. This course should teach the learner to assess the **quality and provenance of information** — direct observation, hearsay, uncertainty, corroboration, internal ambiguity — rather than trying to read honesty from nervousness, eye contact, confidence or other folk cues.

## 3. Evidence base from interviewing and witness-memory research

### 3.1 Memory is reconstructive, not a recording

A witness's account is not a playback of a stored video. Episodic memory is reconstructive. Attention at the time of the event, later information, repeated retelling, interviewer wording, expectations and source confusion can all affect what is later reported.

A 2024 systematic review of adult eyewitness misinformation research describes the misinformation effect as a robust consequence of reconstructive episodic memory: post-event misinformation can reduce accuracy, and no individual cognitive trait makes a person immune. Source-monitoring errors are a central mechanism — a person may remember a detail but misattribute where that detail came from.

**Argus implication:** teach the learner to protect the source of the interviewee's account. Do not introduce details merely because the interviewer already knows or suspects them.

### 3.2 Begin with an open account

Professional investigative-interview guidance and cognitive-interview research converge on obtaining an initial account with minimal interviewer input.

Ontario's study guide says not to interrupt once the interviewee has started talking. The UK College of Policing likewise recommends open-ended questions at the start because they allow an unrestricted account and are less likely to be influenced by the interviewer. Cognitive-interview literature similarly emphasizes transfer of control to the witness, an uninterrupted narrative, and follow-up after the first account.

The point is not that an open narrative is complete. It often is not. Its value is that the interviewee selects the content and wording before the interviewer starts narrowing the search space.

**Argus implication:** an interview scenario should often reward **waiting** or a minimal continuation prompt rather than immediately asking the next prepared question.

### 3.3 Use witness-compatible follow-up

The strongest low-risk follow-up questions are based on information the interviewee has already supplied.

Examples:

- "You mentioned a bag. Tell me more about the bag."
- "You said you heard a sound before you turned around. Tell me about that sound."
- "When you say 'near the entrance,' what location do you mean?"

This is sometimes described as witness-compatible questioning: the interviewer works from the witness's own representation rather than imposing the interviewer's preferred structure or vocabulary.

A useful question sequence is:

1. broad invitation;
2. open follow-up on a detail already mentioned;
3. neutral who/what/where/when/how clarification when necessary;
4. narrow closed clarification only after broader recall has been exhausted.

This is a **priority order**, not a ban on specific questions. Specific questions can be necessary. The skill is to delay them until they are needed and phrase them without supplying the answer.

### 3.4 Open follow-up is not a guarantee of accuracy

A key corrective for course design comes from experimental work on follow-up questions. Kontogianni and colleagues found that open follow-up questions elicited meaningful new information beyond an initial account, but the new information was less accurate than the initial account in their experiments.

This matters because a simplistic course could accidentally teach "open-ended = reliable." That would be wrong.

**Argus implication:** score question quality and information provenance separately from the truth of the resulting answer. Later detail should not automatically be treated as equivalent in certainty to an initially volunteered detail.

### 3.5 Avoid leading and contaminating questions

A leading question suggests the desired answer. A contaminating question goes further by importing information that the interviewee has not independently supplied.

Examples:

- leading: "Was the jacket red?"
- neutral: "Describe what the person was wearing."
- contaminating: "Another witness said the jacket was red. What colour did you see?"
- neutral: "What, if anything, do you remember about the person's clothing?"

Leading questions can alter later reports, and post-event misinformation can become confused with the original memory source. Repetition can strengthen misinformation effects.

**Argus implication:** the learner should sometimes be explicitly rewarded for **not using known external information in the question**.

### 3.6 Clarify without forcing precision

A cooperative interview still needs clarification. The interviewer may need to establish what a pronoun refers to, where something occurred, which event came first, whether a detail was directly perceived, or whether a time is an estimate.

The correct response to uncertainty is not pressure.

The learner should preserve statements such as:

- "I don't know."
- "I don't remember."
- "I think it was around 8, but I didn't check the time."
- "I heard someone say it; I didn't see it myself."
- "I'm not sure those were the exact words."

Research on eyewitness decisions shows value in allowing people to withhold an answer when unsure rather than forcing a choice. More broadly, witness-memory guidance recommends telling interviewees not to guess and allowing uncertainty to remain uncertainty.

**Argus implication:** "insufficient information," "unknown" and "approximate" must be legitimate successful outputs.

### 3.7 Confidence is not a general credibility shortcut

Modern eyewitness research requires nuance. Under specific, uncontaminated identification conditions, an eyewitness's initial confidence can carry useful information about identification accuracy. But confidence can also be altered by feedback, repeated retrieval and later contamination, and confidence is not a general-purpose test of whether a narrative detail is true.

This course should therefore teach a narrow rule:

> **Record expressed certainty when relevant; do not convert confidence, fluency or demeanour into a truth score.**

That avoids both outdated "confidence means nothing" messaging and equally poor "confidence means accuracy" reasoning.

### 3.8 The Cognitive Interview has evidence, but it is not an app-level beginner skill

Ontario's syllabus explicitly names the Cognitive Interview. The technique has a substantial research history. A 2010 meta-analysis covering 46 published articles found a large increase in correct details and a smaller increase in errors compared with control interviews; an earlier field study also reported improved recall after trained detectives used the method.

The full Cognitive Interview is a multi-component interviewing protocol grounded in memory retrieval and communication techniques. It is not just "ask open questions." Proper use requires more training and judgement than this course can establish.

**Argus implication:** Interviewing I should teach the term, evidence rationale and foundational behaviours it shares with good witness interviewing, but should explicitly stop short of claiming to train the full protocol.

### 3.9 Information gathering is distinct from accusatorial interrogation

Ontario requires learners to distinguish interviewing from interrogating. Argus should make that distinction operational rather than dramatic.

For this course:

- **interviewing** means a planned conversation whose purpose is to obtain and clarify an account while preserving the person's own information and uncertainty;
- **interrogation** means suspect-focused questioning where obtaining admissions or resolving culpability is central and where different legal, ethical and professional safeguards apply.

A 2014 meta-analysis comparing accusatorial and information-gathering approaches in suspect-interrogation research found that information-gathering approaches preserved, and in some experiments increased, true confessions while reducing false confessions relative to accusatorial approaches. The authors also cautioned that the evidence base contained a limited number of independent samples.

Argus does not need the mechanics of either interrogation style. The relevant lesson is simpler:

> **Do not turn a cooperative information-gathering interview into an improvised pressure exercise.**

If the person's role, legal status, safety context or organizational procedure changes, the correct next step may be to stop, seek supervision, or move to the appropriate trained/legal process.

## 4. Teachable framework

A six-phase framework is sufficient for Interviewing I.

### Phase 1 — Prepare

Before the conversation, define:

- the purpose of the interview;
- the specific information gaps that matter;
- what is already known from other sources;
- which known details must **not** be planted into the interviewee's account;
- logistics, policy and any role-specific legal requirements;
- a small set of topic areas rather than a rigid script.

The interviewer should enter with objectives, not with a conclusion that the witness is expected to confirm.

### Phase 2 — Orient

At the start:

- identify the purpose and roles in plain language;
- explain the expected format;
- make it acceptable to say "I don't know," "I don't remember" or "I'm not sure";
- make it acceptable to correct the interviewer;
- ask the person to use their own words.

The goal is not a scripted speech. It is to reduce social pressure to agree, guess or please the interviewer.

### Phase 3 — Obtain the account

Start broad.

A good first substantive prompt is structurally like:

> "Tell me what you remember about what happened, in your own words."

Then listen. Do not interrupt merely because a prepared question comes to mind. Note the follow-up topic and let the person finish.

Minimal encouragers can be appropriate, but the interviewer should avoid confirming the truth of specific details while the account is still developing.

### Phase 4 — Clarify and extend

Work from the person's own words.

Preferred order:

- open depth prompt: "You mentioned X. Tell me more about X."
- open sequence prompt: "What happened next?"
- neutral specific clarification: "Where was the vehicle when you first noticed it?"
- narrow closed clarification if still necessary: "Was that before or after you entered the lobby?"

Ask one question at a time. Avoid compound questions that make it unclear which part the person is answering.

"Why" questions are not automatically improper, but they can sound accusatory or invite post-hoc explanation. Where the objective is descriptive fact, formulations such as "What happened before you decided to leave?" or "What led you to do that?" will often be clearer.

### Phase 5 — Check and preserve uncertainty

Before closing:

- resolve ambiguous pronouns, locations and sequence where possible;
- distinguish direct observation from information heard from someone else;
- preserve estimates as estimates;
- preserve "not sure" rather than editing it out;
- neutrally summarize important points and allow correction;
- ask whether there is anything else the person remembers that has not been discussed.

A summary is a check, not an opportunity to rewrite the person's account into the interviewer's preferred story.

### Phase 6 — Record and close

During and immediately after the interview, keep the source of each claim clear.

A useful discipline is to distinguish:

- **statement:** what the interviewee actually reported;
- **qualifier:** how certain, approximate or second-hand the interviewee said it was;
- **interviewer inference:** the interviewer's interpretation, kept separate from the statement;
- **follow-up/corroboration need:** what should be checked elsewhere.

Ontario's study guide supports prompt reporting and thorough, accurate notes. Argus should teach that notes are an evidentiary and memory-support tool, not permission to silently convert interpretation into fact.

## 5. Question taxonomy for Argus

The learner does not need a large jargon set. Five categories are enough.

| Question type | Purpose | Example | Default treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open invitation | Obtain the person's account with minimal interviewer structure | "Tell me what you remember about what happened." | Preferred early |
| Witness-compatible open follow-up | Expand a detail the person already introduced | "You mentioned a bag. Tell me more about the bag." | Preferred follow-up |
| Neutral specific clarification | Resolve a defined missing detail | "Where were you standing when you heard that?" | Use after broader account |
| Narrow closed clarification | Confirm a limited point already in play | "Was that before or after the train arrived?" | Use sparingly |
| Leading/contaminating | Suggest an answer or introduce an unreported fact | "The man had a knife, right?" | Avoid |

Two classifications cut across all five:

- **compound:** asks multiple questions at once;
- **loaded/accusatory:** embeds blame, judgement or a contested assumption.

An open grammatical form can still be contaminating. For example, "How fast was the red car going?" is open in response length but presupposes that the car was red and moving fast.

## 6. Separating statements from interviewer inference

This should be one of the course's strongest practical skills because it connects directly to `OBSERVATION_VS_INFERENCE.md` and future reporting/evidence courses.

Consider:

> Witness: "He kept looking toward the door, spoke very quickly, and his hands were shaking."

A defensible note is:

> Witness reported that the person looked toward the door repeatedly, spoke quickly and had shaking hands.

An interviewer inference is:

> He was nervous because he knew he had done something wrong.

The second statement may be a hypothesis. It is not what the witness reported, and it should not be silently merged into the interview record.

### Recommended Argus classification exercise

Give the learner a transcript plus a proposed note and ask them to mark each sentence as:

- direct statement;
- interviewer inference;
- ambiguous source;
- unsupported addition.

Then require a corrected note.

This is more useful than asking learners to memorize the definition of "inference" again. It forces provenance tracking under realistic conditions.

## 7. Contemporaneous-note discipline

Interview notes should preserve **what was said, when, by whom, and with what uncertainty**, without becoming a second source of contamination.

Interviewing I should teach the following habits:

- record basic interview metadata required by the scenario: date/time, location, participants and purpose;
- capture exact wording only when it is genuinely material and clearly mark quotations as quotations;
- otherwise use accurate paraphrase without adding interpretation;
- record important qualifiers such as "approximately," "I think," "I heard," and "I did not see";
- mark questions or topics to revisit instead of interrupting an ongoing narrative;
- keep the interviewer's hypotheses separate from the witness's account;
- complete the report promptly after the interaction;
- preserve corrections rather than silently rewriting the history of the account.

Argus should not teach one universal legal statement format. Ontario organizations may have specific procedures, and formal statements can carry requirements beyond this introductory course.

## 8. Common errors and misconceptions

### "A good interviewer asks lots of questions"

Not necessarily. In the opening account, the better move may be to listen. Excessive questioning can fragment the narrative and increase interviewer input.

### "Open questions cannot lead a witness"

False. An open question can still contain assumptions: "Why did he threaten you with the knife?" supplies both a threat and a knife if those facts have not been independently introduced.

### "Closed questions are always bad"

False. Narrow closed questions can efficiently clarify an already established point. They are weaker as the default discovery method because they constrain the response and can import assumptions.

### "More detail means a more accurate account"

False. Additional follow-up can produce useful details and additional errors. Detail quantity and accuracy must not be treated as the same variable.

### "A witness who changes a detail is lying"

False. Memory, misunderstanding, ambiguity, source confusion, stress, later information and ordinary error can all produce inconsistency. Inconsistency can justify clarification or corroboration; it is not a diagnosis of deception.

### "A confident witness is probably right"

Too broad. Confidence can be informative in some tightly defined eyewitness-identification conditions, but it is not a general credibility meter and can be affected by feedback and contamination.

### "The interviewer should fill gaps from other evidence"

False. Known external details belong in the investigator's evidence set, not inside the witness's memory report unless there is a justified later reason to test a specific proposition. Even then, the source must remain explicit.

### "The Cognitive Interview is an advanced version of asking lots of memory questions"

False. It is a structured, evidence-based professional protocol with multiple retrieval and communication components. Interviewing I should teach its existence and rationale, not pretend to confer proficiency.

### "Interviewing is interrogation with a softer tone"

False for Argus purposes. Cooperative interviewing seeks an account; interrogation raises different legal, ethical and professional issues and is outside this course.

## 9. Transcript/scenario exercise design

The central Argus prompt should be:

> **What should the interviewer do or ask next, and why?**

Each scenario should provide:

- the interview objective;
- what the interviewer already knows from other sources;
- the transcript so far;
- any uncertainty the witness has explicitly expressed;
- three or four plausible next actions/questions;
- a request for the learner to select the best next move;
- a short explanation requiring the learner to name the principle involved.

The scenarios should deliberately include cases where the correct answer is:

- let the person continue;
- ask an open follow-up based on their own words;
- clarify an ambiguous term;
- accept "I don't know";
- avoid introducing a known detail;
- record a qualifier rather than resolving it;
- separate inference from the statement;
- stop rather than convert the interaction into an improvised interrogation.

### Scenario 1 — Do not interrupt the first account

**Objective:** learn what a commuter observed after hearing a disturbance on a subway platform.

**Transcript**

> Interviewer: "Tell me what you remember about what happened, in your own words."
>
> Witness: "I got off the train and started toward the stairs. I heard a loud bang behind me, then I turned and saw two people near the bench. One of them—"

**Candidate next moves**

- "Was the bang a gunshot?"
- "What were the two people wearing?"
- Allow the witness to continue.
- "Which one started the fight?"

**Best next move:** allow the witness to continue.

**Why:** the witness is already producing the initial narrative. Every question offered would interrupt that account, and three introduce assumptions that have not been established. The interviewer can record possible follow-up topics and return to them later.

### Scenario 2 — Follow the witness's words

**Objective:** clarify what happened to an unattended item in a building lobby.

**Transcript**

> Witness: "A man came in, put a bag beside the plant, looked around for a bit, then left through the side door."

**Candidate next moves**

- "Was it a black backpack?"
- "You mentioned a bag. Tell me more about the bag."
- "Why was he acting suspiciously?"
- "Did he leave it there on purpose?"

**Best next move:** "You mentioned a bag. Tell me more about the bag."

**Why:** it asks for more detail about an item the witness independently introduced without supplying colour, type, intent or a threat interpretation.

### Scenario 3 — Clarify an ambiguous reference

**Objective:** establish the sequence of a workplace incident.

**Transcript**

> Witness: "Sam spoke to Alex by the loading door. Then he went back there and the alarm started."

**Candidate next moves**

- "When you say 'he,' who do you mean?"
- "Why did Sam set off the alarm?"
- "Did Alex follow Sam?"
- "Was Sam angry?"

**Best next move:** "When you say 'he,' who do you mean?"

**Why:** the transcript is ambiguous about the person referenced. The other questions build new facts or motives on top of an unresolved pronoun.

### Scenario 4 — Preserve uncertain timing

**Objective:** establish an approximate incident timeline.

**Transcript**

> Witness: "It was around 7:30, I think. I didn't look at the clock."

**Candidate next moves**

- "So 7:30 exactly?"
- "Could it have been 7:45?"
- "What, if anything, helps you place it around 7:30?"
- "You must remember the time if you remember everything else."

**Best next move:** "What, if anything, helps you place it around 7:30?"

**Why:** it can reveal the basis of the estimate without forcing false precision. The note should preserve that the time is approximate and that the witness did not directly check a clock.

### Scenario 5 — Do not import another witness's detail

**Objective:** obtain an independent description of a vehicle.

**Interviewer-only information:** another witness reported a red hatchback.

**Transcript**

> Witness: "I saw a car pull away from the curb just after the shouting stopped."

**Candidate next moves**

- "Was it the red hatchback?"
- "Another witness said it was red. What colour did you see?"
- "Describe the vehicle as best you can."
- "How fast did the red car leave?"

**Best next move:** "Describe the vehicle as best you can."

**Why:** it preserves an independent account. The interviewer's external knowledge should remain outside the witness's memory report at this stage.

### Scenario 6 — Separate observation from interpretation

**Objective:** clarify why a witness described a person as angry.

**Transcript**

> Witness: "He was really angry when he came into the store."

**Candidate next moves**

- "What did you see or hear that led you to describe him as angry?"
- "Was he angry because the cashier refused him?"
- "How dangerous did he look?"
- "He was shouting, right?"

**Best next move:** "What did you see or hear that led you to describe him as angry?"

**Why:** "angry" is an interpretation. The question asks for the observable basis without assuming the witness's interpretation is wrong or true.

### Scenario 7 — Preserve wording uncertainty

**Objective:** document what was said during an argument.

**Transcript**

> Witness: "I think she said something like 'leave me alone,' but those might not have been the exact words."

**Candidate next moves**

- "Write down that she said 'leave me alone' verbatim."
- "Are you sure she didn't say 'get away from me'?"
- "That's fine. What do you remember about what was said, even if the words are not exact?"
- "Pick which phrase sounds closest."

**Best next move:** "That's fine. What do you remember about what was said, even if the words are not exact?"

**Why:** it preserves the witness's uncertainty and may elicit meaning or context without falsely upgrading a paraphrase into a quotation.

### Scenario 8 — Do not improvise an interrogation

**Objective:** obtain a cooperative employee's account of missing inventory.

**Transcript**

> Employee: "I finished at six and left through the west door."
>
> Interviewer-only information: access logs now create a potential inconsistency that could change the employee's status in the investigation.

**Candidate next moves**

- accuse the employee of lying and demand an admission;
- threaten consequences unless the employee changes the account;
- continue only within the authorized cooperative interview plan, or stop and follow the appropriate organizational/legal process if the role has materially changed;
- pretend the interviewer has video evidence to force a reaction.

**Best next move:** continue only within the authorized cooperative interview plan, or stop and follow the appropriate organizational/legal process if the role has materially changed.

**Why:** Interviewing I does not train suspect interrogation or pressure tactics. A change in status is a procedural boundary, not an invitation to improvise coercion.

## 10. Scenario scoring rubric

Each authored response can be scored on six dimensions.

| Dimension | Strong response | Weak response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neutrality | Does not imply the desired answer | Suggests, presupposes or pressures |
| Witness compatibility | Uses details already supplied by the interviewee | Imports investigator vocabulary or external facts |
| Information value | Advances a defined objective or lets a useful narrative continue | Asks a low-value or premature detail |
| Uncertainty preservation | Allows unknown, approximate and second-hand information to remain qualified | Forces precision or strips qualifiers |
| Source discipline | Keeps witness statement, external evidence and interviewer inference distinct | Blends sources together |
| Scope/safety | Remains within cooperative information gathering | Drifts into improvised interrogation or coercion |

Some answers should be deliberately close. The explanation should teach **why one question is better at this point in the interview**, not imply that professional interviewing always has a single perfect line.

### Critical-error flags

Regardless of total score, some responses should trigger explicit correction:

- supplying a central unreported fact;
- telling the witness what another witness said before obtaining an independent account;
- forcing a choice when "unknown" is legitimate;
- rewriting an uncertain paraphrase as an exact quotation;
- presenting interviewer inference as witness statement;
- threatening, deceiving or pressuring for an admission.

## 11. Assessment model

Interviewing I should combine finite recall with scenario application.

### Finite recall layer

Keep this small. Learners should retain:

- the six interview phases: prepare, orient, obtain account, clarify/extend, check, record/close;
- the five question categories;
- the rule that broad narrative precedes narrow clarification;
- the difference between statement, qualifier and interviewer inference;
- the meaning of leading/contaminating questions;
- the fact that memory is reconstructive and uncertainty is valid;
- the course boundary between cooperative interviewing and interrogation.

### Scenario layer

The main assessment should use short transcripts with changing details.

Recommended item types:

- choose the next question/action;
- rank three candidate questions by contamination risk;
- identify what assumption a weak question introduces;
- rewrite a leading question neutrally;
- mark which transcript details came from the witness versus the interviewer;
- correct a note that converted inference into fact;
- decide whether to clarify, wait, accept uncertainty or stop;
- explain in one sentence why the selected next move is stronger.

### What should not be assessed

Do not score learners on:

- extracting a confession;
- judging whether the witness is lying;
- reading body language as deception;
- maximizing the number of details at any cost;
- reproducing a full formal Cognitive Interview script;
- giving jurisdiction-specific legal advice from memory.

## 12. Relationship to other Argus practical-skills courses

Interviewing I should sit after the foundational observation/judgement material and before any advanced witness-memory course.

It depends conceptually on:

- **Observation vs Inference** — separate what happened from what the observer or interviewer concluded;
- **Bias Control in Behavioural Judgement** — resist confirmation and premature closure;
- **Crisis Active Listening** — listen, paraphrase and preserve the speaker's meaning, though interviewing has a different information objective;
- **De-escalation I** — maintain professional, non-escalatory communication when tension rises.

It naturally feeds later courses in:

- witness memory and Cognitive Interview concepts;
- objective incident reporting;
- evidence continuity;
- credibility/deception limits;
- structured fact-finding for consulting and workplace investigations.

The key distinction from Crisis Active Listening is that Interviewing I has an **evidentiary-information objective**. The interviewer is not merely helping the person feel heard; they are trying to obtain, clarify and preserve an account without unnecessarily changing it.

### Evidence-quality note

The course should distinguish three kinds of support:

- Ontario sources define the local professional-training expectation and terminology;
- professional interviewing guidance translates research into field practice;
- experimental and meta-analytic psychology constrains what Argus should claim about memory, questioning and accuracy.

No single source should be treated as a universal interviewing standard across police, private investigation, workplace fact-finding, journalism, consulting and ordinary civilian conversations. Interviewing I should teach transferable fundamentals while explicitly preserving role-specific legal and policy boundaries.

## 14. Exact completion claim and proposed Argus curriculum

### Exact completion claim

> **Completed learners can, in authored cooperative information-gathering scenarios, prepare an interview objective, elicit an uninterrupted open account, choose neutral witness-compatible follow-up and clarification questions, avoid introducing unreported details, preserve uncertainty, distinguish interviewee statements from interviewer inference, and produce prompt objective notes. Completion does not establish competence to conduct coercive or suspect interrogation, specialist forensic interviews, the full Cognitive Interview, or professional investigations without further training and applicable legal/policy guidance.**

### Proposed Argus curriculum

**Course size:** 7 lessons, 2 short scenario checkpoints, 1 final integrated scenario set. Scenario practice should carry more weight than recall.

| Lesson | Core content | Primary practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Purpose before questions | define objectives, information gaps, known-vs-unknown, avoid hypothesis confirmation | identify good interview objectives; detect biased objectives |
| 2. Let the account emerge | orientation, own words, uncertainty permission, uninterrupted open narrative | choose when to wait versus ask |
| 3. The follow-up ladder | witness-compatible open prompts, 5W/H clarification, narrow closed clarification | choose the next question and explain why |
| Checkpoint A | mixed opening and follow-up transcripts | contamination-risk and next-question items |
| 4. Do not contaminate the source | leading assumptions, external information, compound questions, repeated prompting | rewrite weak questions neutrally |
| 5. Memory, uncertainty and clarification | reconstructive memory, misinformation, source monitoring, estimates, "I don't know" | preserve uncertainty; identify direct versus second-hand knowledge |
| 6. Statements, inference and notes | statement versus interviewer inference, qualifiers, contemporaneous notes, prompt reporting | repair a contaminated/interpretive interview note |
| Checkpoint B | note provenance plus memory limits | classify statement / qualifier / inference / unknown |
| 7. Interview, not interrogation | cooperative boundary, Cognitive Interview recognition, when not to improvise | choose continue / clarify / stop / escalate to proper process |
| Final scenario set | 6–8 short interviews spanning ordinary public, workplace and incident contexts | select the next action/question, justify it, and produce a short objective note |

**Recommended final-assessment rule:** completion should require both the recall layer and the scenario layer. A learner should not pass the scenario layer after a critical contamination error merely because their aggregate multiple-choice score is high. The implementation issue should define the numeric mastery threshold, but critical errors should receive mandatory correction and a fresh scenario before completion.
