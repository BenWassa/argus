# Crisis Active Listening — FBI ALS research and Argus course design

**Status:** deep research / course-design input only  
**Issue:** #104  
**Branch:** `research/issue-104-practical-skills-expansion`  
**Implementation:** out of scope  
**Jurisdiction note:** the communication skill is not jurisdiction-specific; Ontario/Canadian professional context is included where public sources support it.

## Recommendation

**Ship as a bounded Argus course after De-escalation I.**

Crisis Active Listening is a strong fit for the practical-skills expansion because it combines a finite, sourceable framework with communication behaviours that are poorly represented by flashcard recall alone. The FBI Crisis Negotiation Unit has used active listening as a central crisis-negotiation discipline since 1990, and its later standardized training framework identifies eight scorable active-listening responses. The research base supports active listening as trainable and useful for perceived understanding, rapport and communication quality, but the evidence does **not** justify a claim that an app-trained learner can negotiate real crises or that any individual technique reliably causes safe resolution.

Argus should therefore teach the FBI response set accurately, rehearse it through short transcripts, and keep the completion claim narrow.

## Exact completion claim

> **Can identify the FBI Crisis Negotiation Unit’s eight active-listening responses and, in authored text-based scenarios, select or construct context-appropriate examples of them — including paraphrasing, tentative emotion labeling and content-plus-emotion summaries — while recognizing common listening errors such as premature advice, interrogation, unsupported mind-reading and mechanical technique use.**

This completion claim does **not** mean the learner:

- is a crisis or hostage negotiator;
- is trained to manage armed, suicidal, violent or barricaded incidents;
- can assess whether a real person is safe, dangerous, mentally ill or truthful;
- can replace emergency services, police crisis-negotiation teams, clinicians or trained crisis responders;
- has demonstrated live interpersonal skill under pressure.

Argus can claim authored transcript competence. It cannot claim field competence.

# 1. What is the canonical FBI active-listening set?

There is a real seven-versus-eight source discrepancy, and the course should resolve it explicitly rather than quietly choosing one list.

The influential 1997 FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin article by Gary Noesner and Mike Webster described **seven core techniques**:

- minimal encouragers;
- paraphrasing;
- emotion labeling;
- reflecting/mirroring;
- open-ended questions;
- “I” messages;
- effective pauses.

The FBI Crisis Negotiation Unit’s later standardized evaluation framework, published in the FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin in 2015, identifies **eight active-listening responses**:

- I message;
- paraphrase;
- emotion label;
- summary;
- effective pause;
- minimal encourager;
- reflection;
- open-ended question.

The additional separately scored response is **summary**. The 2015 article explicitly presents these as the eight responses used by CNU’s Active Listening Skills Target and cites the 1997 Noesner/Webster work as its source lineage.

### Argus authority decision

For course content, use the **eight-response 2015 FBI CNU framework** as canonical because it is the later, operationalized training-and-evaluation standard. Teach the 1997 seven-technique list as a short provenance note, not as a competing answer learners must memorize.

Do not import commercialized variants, mnemonic rewrites or negotiation-influencer adaptations and label them “the FBI method.”

**Primary sources:**

- FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin, [An Evaluation Tool for Crisis Negotiators](https://leb.fbi.gov/articles/focus/focus-on-training-an-evaluation-tool-for-crisis-negotiators) (2015).
- FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin archive, [Crisis Intervention: Using Active Listening Skills in Negotiations](https://leb.fbi.gov/file-repository/archives/august-1997.pdf) (Noesner & Webster, 1997).
- FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin, [Fifty Years of FBI Crisis (Hostage) Negotiation](https://leb.fbi.gov/articles/featured-articles/fifty-years-of-fbi-crisis-hostage-negotiation) (Noesner, 2024).

# 2. What active listening is doing in the FBI model

The FBI’s crisis-negotiation approach shifted in 1990 away from treating every incident as a bargaining problem. Gary Noesner’s 2024 retrospective describes the operational realization that many critical incidents involved highly emotional or expressive people who were not meaningfully bargaining for a concrete exchange. The programme adopted active listening methods drawn from the person-centred counselling tradition associated with Carl Rogers and made them central to negotiation training.

The intended sequence is broader than “say the right phrase.” The FBI Behavioral Change Stairway places:

1. active listening;
2. empathy;
3. rapport;
4. influence;
5. behavioural change.

For Argus, the important instructional principle is that **listening precedes persuasion**. The learner should repeatedly experience scenarios where the tempting response is advice, reassurance, correction or problem-solving, but the stronger early response is to understand and demonstrate understanding first.

This does not mean advice is always wrong. It means the course should teach **timing**. A response that would be sensible later can be poor while the speaker is still trying to be heard, highly emotional, or clarifying what happened.

The FBI also treats active listening as a skill that requires ongoing practice. Its 2015 evaluation framework scores not only whether a response belongs to an ALS category but also qualitative aspects such as **tone, delivery and timing**. Text-only Argus practice cannot observe vocal tone or embodied delivery, so its completion claim must remain narrower.

# 3. The eight responses

## 3.1 Minimal encourager

### Definition

A brief verbal acknowledgement showing that the listener is present and following, without taking over the conversation. Examples include restrained equivalents of “okay,” “I see,” “mm-hm,” or “go on.”

### When it helps

- the speaker is already talking productively;
- a longer response would interrupt useful disclosure;
- the listener wants to signal attention without changing the subject;
- the next useful information is more likely to come from the speaker than from another question.

### Misuse patterns

- **robotic repetition:** inserting “uh-huh” after every sentence;
- **accidental agreement:** using words such as “right” or “exactly” when the listener means only “I hear you”;
- **performative listening:** producing acknowledgements while ignoring the actual content;
- **premature turn-taking:** using an encourager as a springboard to immediately redirect the conversation back to the listener.

### Argus distinction

A minimal encourager does not add meaning. If the response substantially restates the speaker’s message, it is closer to paraphrase or reflection.

---

## 3.2 Paraphrase

### Definition

Restating the **meaning** of the speaker’s message in the listener’s own words.

### When it helps

- checking whether the listener understood the factual or narrative content;
- simplifying a scattered account without changing its meaning;
- demonstrating that the listener followed more than individual words;
- giving the speaker a chance to correct a misunderstanding;
- slowing a conversation before moving toward problem-solving.

### Misuse patterns

- **parroting:** repeating the same words instead of restating meaning;
- **adding facts:** inserting motives, causes or conclusions the speaker did not provide;
- **selective paraphrase:** reflecting only the part that supports the listener’s preferred solution;
- **premature advice:** attaching “so what you should do is…” to the paraphrase;
- **false certainty:** treating the paraphrase as a verdict instead of a check on understanding.

### Strong form

> “You expected the meeting to settle the issue, but instead you left without knowing what happens next.”

### Weak form

> “So they completely betrayed you and now there is nothing you can do.”

The weak version adds conclusions and intensifies the speaker’s position.

---

## 3.3 Emotion label

### Definition

Tentatively identifying an emotion expressed or implied by the speaker’s words or behaviour.

### When it helps

- the emotional meaning is central but not yet explicit;
- the speaker’s facts and feelings are tangled together;
- naming the emotion may help the speaker confirm, reject or refine the listener’s understanding;
- the listener wants to acknowledge emotional impact without agreeing with every factual claim.

### Misuse patterns

- **mind-reading:** “You are furious because you want revenge.”
- **diagnosis:** replacing an emotion with a psychiatric label;
- **certainty language:** stating an inferred feeling as indisputable fact;
- **emotion escalation:** choosing a stronger label than the evidence supports;
- **invalidating correction:** arguing with the speaker when the label is rejected;
- **agreement confusion:** assuming that recognizing emotion means endorsing the speaker’s accusations or intended actions.

### Preferred Argus form

Use tentative phrasing such as:

- “It sounds like…”
- “It seems as though…”
- “I’m hearing a lot of frustration around…”

The label should remain **correctable**.

### Core lesson

Acknowledging emotion is not the same as agreeing with the speaker’s interpretation of events.

---

## 3.4 Summary

### Definition

A periodic review that brings together the main content of the speaker’s account and the accompanying feelings.

### When it helps

- a conversation has accumulated several points;
- the listener needs to check a shared understanding before moving on;
- the speaker’s story is repetitive or fragmented;
- a transition is appropriate after sustained listening;
- the listener wants to show that both facts and emotional meaning were retained.

### Misuse patterns

- **transcript dump:** repeating every detail instead of identifying the main points;
- **agenda summary:** omitting facts that do not fit the listener’s preferred interpretation;
- **premature closure:** summarizing as a way to end discussion before the speaker is ready;
- **emotion-free recap:** producing a sterile chronology while ignoring the speaker’s emotional meaning;
- **invented coherence:** forcing a clear story onto an account that remains uncertain or contradictory.

### Strong form

> “You have been trying to get an answer for three days, each person has sent you somewhere else, and now you’re frustrated because you still do not know whether the payment is coming.”

A good summary is not necessarily short, but it is **selective and faithful**.

---

## 3.5 Effective pause

### Definition

Purposeful silence before or after something meaningful, allowing the speaker space to continue, think or settle without the listener immediately filling the gap.

### When it helps

- the speaker has just disclosed something significant;
- emotion is high and another verbal intervention would crowd the exchange;
- the listener has asked an important question and should wait for the answer;
- the speaker appears to be gathering thoughts;
- the listener needs to resist reflexive advice or reassurance.

### Misuse patterns

- **punitive silence:** using silence to punish, shame or signal withdrawal;
- **pressure tactic:** treating every pause as a trick to make someone disclose more;
- **awkward overuse:** extending silence beyond what the interaction supports;
- **unsafe persistence:** waiting silently when the situation instead calls for immediate emergency action or disengagement.

### Argus representation

Text scenarios can represent this explicitly as `[pause]`. The learner should sometimes select **no immediate verbal response** as the best next move.

This is important because a communication course that always requires another sentence would teach the wrong habit.

---

## 3.6 Reflection / mirroring

### Definition

Repeating the speaker’s last few words or a short key phrase to invite continuation and show close attention.

### When it helps

- the last phrase appears significant and the speaker may elaborate;
- a direct question would be more intrusive than a short reflection;
- the listener wants to stay with the speaker’s language rather than impose new framing;
- the conversation needs a low-load prompt rather than a complex intervention.

### Misuse patterns

- **echoing mechanically:** repeating every sentence ending;
- **mocking effect:** mirroring tone or wording in a way that sounds sarcastic;
- **overlong repetition:** turning reflection into paraphrase without actually clarifying meaning;
- **amplifying provocative language:** repeating inflammatory or threatening language when a neutral paraphrase would be safer and more useful.

### Example

Speaker: “Nobody there takes me seriously.”  
Listener: “Takes you seriously?”

The reflection is intentionally small. Its value comes from timing, not sophistication.

---

## 3.7 Open-ended question

### Definition

A question that invites more than a yes/no answer and encourages the speaker to explain events, thoughts, priorities or feelings. FBI training materials commonly favour “who,” “what,” “when,” “where” and “how” formulations and caution that “why” can sound accusatory or confrontational.

### When it helps

- important context is missing;
- the speaker is giving very short answers;
- the listener needs the speaker’s account rather than a forced choice;
- the conversation needs clarification without inserting an assumption;
- the speaker appears able and willing to elaborate.

### Misuse patterns

- **interrogation chain:** asking one question after another without reflecting anything back;
- **disguised accusation:** “Why would you do something like that?”;
- **compound question:** asking several things at once;
- **solution-leading:** “What if you just apologize and go home?”;
- **overload:** asking a broad, cognitively demanding question when the speaker is too distressed to organize a long answer;
- **false openness:** asking an open question while clearly signalling the acceptable answer.

### Strong form

> “What happened after you got that message?”

### Weak form

> “Why didn’t you just ignore it?”

---

## 3.8 “I” message

### Definition

In the FBI CNU’s 2015 scoring framework, an I message is an attempt to confront the subject, encourage positive behaviour or discourage negative behaviour using a first-person structure such as “I feel … when you … because …”.

This is the response most likely to be misunderstood if Argus presents all eight techniques as interchangeable forms of empathic listening. It is better understood as a **limited assertive/confrontive response inside the broader active-listening toolkit**.

### When it helps

- rapport already exists and a boundary or behaviour needs to be addressed;
- the listener needs to humanize the exchange without trading accusations;
- a first-person statement can describe impact more safely than blaming language;
- the situation remains appropriate for continued engagement.

### Misuse patterns

- **centering the listener too early:** shifting attention away from a distressed speaker before they feel heard;
- **blame disguised as feeling:** “I feel that you are being irrational.”;
- **moralizing:** using the formula to deliver a lecture;
- **fake emotion:** selecting a feeling merely because the template demands one;
- **premature confrontation:** using an I message before basic understanding and rapport exist;
- **boundary confusion:** continuing to engage when the appropriate response is to disengage, seek help or follow an emergency protocol.

### Argus treatment

Teach I messages **last** among the eight responses and score them conservatively. They should not become the default answer to “what do you say next?” scenarios.

# 4. What the evidence supports

## 4.1 Strongest claim: this is an established professional training framework

The professional-use evidence is clear.

The FBI reports that active listening became the centrepiece of its crisis-negotiation curriculum in 1990, spread through U.S. and international law-enforcement training, and later became part of new-agent interviewing instruction. The FBI CNU’s 2015 evaluation tool gives instructors a standardized way to score eight active-listening responses during training exercises. Its 2024 programme retrospective describes continuing national and international crisis-negotiation training and a database of nearly 11,000 crisis incidents used for research and training.

This supports the claim that the framework is **professionally used, durable and trainable**.

It does not by itself prove that each technique independently causes safe crisis resolution.

## 4.2 Training efficacy: active-listening performance can improve with training

A 2006 study of 45 FBI special agents compared role-play performance before and after the FBI’s two-week National Crisis Negotiation Course. Participants improved significantly on nearly all active-listening skills, and premature problem-solving attempts decreased after training.

This is directly relevant to Argus because it supports two design choices:

- behaviour rehearsal is more appropriate than fact recall alone;
- **not jumping to problem-solving** is itself a trainable distinction.

Limitations matter. The study used a pre/post design, a small professional sample and role-play outcomes. It does not show that a short consumer app course produces equivalent gains or real-incident outcomes.

Source: Van Hasselt et al., [Crisis (Hostage) Negotiation Training: A Preliminary Evaluation of Program Efficacy](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093854805282328), *Criminal Justice and Behavior* (2006).

## 4.3 Role-play validity: experts and nonexperts differ on ALS performance

A 2005 study developed a role-play test from actual FBI-negotiated encounters and found that nonexpert negotiators showed deficiencies across active-listening categories relative to experts. The authors described the evidence as preliminary validation of the assessment method.

For Argus, this is useful evidence that **scenario performance reveals something that a definition quiz does not**.

Source: Van Hasselt et al., [Development and Validation of a Role-Play Test for Assessing Crisis (Hostage) Negotiation Skills](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093854804274374), *Criminal Justice and Behavior* (2005).

## 4.4 Expert-practice evidence: experienced negotiators prioritize listening and empathy

A survey of 188 crisis negotiators from multiple countries, primarily the United States, found active listening, empathy, effective communication and remaining calm among the characteristics most frequently associated by practitioners with effective negotiation; confrontation, arguing, yelling and interrupting were among behaviours to avoid. About half reported using a negotiation model, most commonly the FBI Behavioral Change Stairway Model.

This is professional-consensus evidence, not causal evidence.

Source: Grubb et al., [Crisis (hostage) negotiators weigh in: the skills, behaviors, and qualities that characterize an expert crisis negotiator](https://doi.org/10.1080/15614263.2017.1419131), *Police Practice and Research* (2018).

## 4.5 Real-incident discourse evidence exists, but it is case-based

Terry Royce’s analysis of a New South Wales police negotiation examined a real high-risk encounter and argued that active listening in the early stages was important to the eventual peaceful resolution. Such transcript analyses are valuable because they show techniques operating in sequence and context rather than as isolated phrases.

But a successful single case cannot establish a general causal effect.

Source: Royce, [The Negotiator and the Bomber: Analyzing the Critical Role of Active Listening in Crisis Negotiations](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1571-9979.2005.00045.x), *Negotiation Journal* (2005).

## 4.6 General communication evidence supports perceived understanding

In a controlled study with 115 participants and trained confederates, active-listening responses produced greater perceived understanding than advice or simple acknowledgements. Active listening also produced greater conversational satisfaction and social attraction than simple acknowledgements, although it did not outperform advice on those two outcomes.

This supports the generalization that reflective listening can make people feel more understood. It does **not** show that ordinary social conversations are equivalent to crisis negotiation.

Source: Weger et al., [The Relative Effectiveness of Active Listening in Initial Interactions](https://doi.org/10.1080/10904018.2013.813234), *International Journal of Listening* (2014).

## 4.7 Broader listening evidence is positive but heterogeneous

A registered systematic review and meta-analysis of 664 effect sizes and more than 400,000 observations found a positive overall association between perceived listening and work outcomes, with particularly strong associations for relationship quality. When analyses were restricted to different-source/different-method evidence, effect sizes were smaller but the positive pattern remained. The authors also reported substantial heterogeneity and methodological limitations across the literature.

This is useful support for **general listening quality**, not evidence that the FBI eight-response package is the unique or optimal method outside crisis negotiation.

Source: Kluger et al., [A Meta-analytic Systematic Review and Theory of the Effects of Perceived Listening on Work Outcomes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-023-09897-5), *Journal of Business and Psychology* (2023).

## 4.8 Operational outcome claims need caution

The FBI’s 2024 retrospective states that crisis negotiation achieved resolution rates in the mid- to high-90-percent range based on the Hostage Barricade Database System. That is important operational context, but it should **not** be converted into “active listening works 95% of the time.”

The database reflects complete negotiation systems: containment, time, intelligence, team structure, command, tactical coordination, risk assessment, negotiator expertise and many other factors. Historical operational data also cannot isolate the causal contribution of one communication method.

Argus should therefore avoid numerical efficacy claims for ALS itself.

# 5. Ontario / Canadian professional context

Ontario law now treats crisis negotiation as a specialist police responsibility requiring formal and recurring training. Under Ontario Regulation 87/24, officers assigned crisis-negotiator responsibilities must complete the Ontario Police College “Crisis Negotiator” course or an approved substantially equivalent Canadian Police College course. Ongoing crisis-negotiation training is generally required within 24 months and every subsequent 24 months, subject to the regulation’s incident-based exception.

Source: Ontario, [O. Reg. 87/24: Training](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/240087), sections 23–25.

This supports a strong Argus boundary: real crisis negotiation is a **specialist trained police function** in Ontario. Public regulatory text does not expose the detailed course syllabus, so it should not be cited as independent verification that Ontario uses the exact FBI eight-response set.

Argus may teach general listening concepts to civilians. It must not imply equivalence with Ontario Police College or Canadian Police College crisis-negotiator training.

# 6. How the skills generalize beyond hostage negotiation

The eight responses are useful outside crisis negotiation because their underlying functions are ordinary communication functions:

- encourage the other person to continue;
- check understanding;
- clarify facts;
- acknowledge emotion;
- create conversational space;
- organize a long account;
- ask for missing information;
- state a boundary without immediate blame.

The FBI itself has published guidance applying active listening to routine leadership and workplace communication, explicitly noting that the techniques are useful outside critical incidents. Its 2024 retrospective also says ALS became part of new-agent interview and interrogation training.

Source: FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin, [Leading Through Listening](https://leb.fbi.gov/articles/focus/focus-on-leadership-leading-through-listening) (2016).

### Appropriate Argus generalization domains

Use scenarios such as:

- an upset coworker explaining a project failure;
- a client angry about repeated delays;
- a friend describing a conflict before asking for advice;
- a customer-service conversation where the speaker feels ignored;
- a team member reacting defensively to feedback;
- a public-space disagreement that remains safe enough for verbal engagement;
- a witness or colleague giving a fragmented account that needs clarification.

### Do not generalize into unsupported competence

Do not teach the learner to:

- diagnose mental illness from language or behaviour;
- infer deception from emotional presentation;
- conduct amateur suicide intervention;
- negotiate with armed or violent people;
- remain in unsafe situations because “rapport” might work;
- believe that a scripted label or mirror phrase is inherently de-escalating;
- manipulate others by mechanically applying a formula.

The transferable competence is **better listening behaviour**, not crisis mastery.

# 7. Memorization versus applied communication

This course needs both, but they are not equally important.

## 7.1 Finite recall layer

Memorize only what is genuinely finite:

- the eight FBI CNU active-listening responses;
- a one-sentence purpose for each;
- reflection versus paraphrase;
- paraphrase versus summary;
- emotion label versus factual agreement;
- open versus closed question;
- why “why?” can sound accusatory in high-friction conversations;
- the role of active listening before influence in the Behavioral Change Stairway;
- the rule that techniques depend on timing and delivery.

This layer is suitable for ordinary Argus retrieval practice.

## 7.2 Applied communication layer

Application is the course’s real value. Learners must interpret a short transcript and decide **what function is needed next**.

The app should test whether the learner can:

- notice when the speaker has already given enough information and needs space rather than another question;
- choose paraphrase when content needs checking;
- choose emotion labeling when emotional meaning is central;
- avoid advice before understanding;
- construct a tentative label rather than claim access to another person’s mind;
- produce a summary containing both story and feeling;
- improve responses that are technically “active listening” but clumsy, loaded or mechanical;
- recognize that sometimes no further conversational technique is appropriate.

Technique recall should be necessary but insufficient for completion.

# 8. Transcript-based Argus practice design

The FBI’s own training literature emphasizes role-play, behavioural rehearsal and feedback. Argus cannot reproduce live role-play, but short authored transcripts can preserve the essential decision: **given what was just said, what response best demonstrates useful listening now?**

## Exercise type A — identify the technique

Show one speaker line and one listener response.

Ask:

> Which FBI ALS response is this?

Example:

Speaker: “I called three times and nobody even wrote back.”  
Listener: “Nobody wrote back?”

Correct: **reflection**.

Use near-neighbour distractors, especially reflection versus paraphrase and paraphrase versus summary.

### What it tests

Finite classification only. This is an early lesson format, not sufficient evidence of applied skill.

---

## Exercise type B — choose the next response

Show two to four turns of conversation and four candidate responses.

The best option should depend on context, not merely on spotting a keyword.

Example:

Speaker: “Every time I explain it, they tell me to calm down. Nobody is actually listening.”

Candidates might include:

- “What exactly did they say after that?”
- “It sounds like being dismissed is making this even more frustrating.”
- “You should probably email a manager.”
- “Why are you getting so worked up about it?”

Best: **tentative emotion label**, because the speaker’s immediate concern is not lack of factual detail but feeling dismissed and frustrated.

### What it tests

Technique selection and timing.

---

## Exercise type C — paraphrase

Provide a short speaker statement and ask the learner to choose the best paraphrase from authored options.

Score for:

- preserved meaning;
- no invented facts;
- no advice;
- no stronger accusation than the speaker made;
- own words rather than parroting.

Later versions could allow short free text only if Argus has a reliable rubric-driven evaluator. The first implementation should prefer authored alternatives because correctness is easier to defend.

---

## Exercise type D — emotion label

Provide a transcript with an implied emotional state and ask for the best label.

The correct option should be:

- grounded in the transcript;
- tentative;
- proportionate;
- non-diagnostic;
- separable from agreement with the speaker’s factual claim.

Include cases where **insufficient information** or a softer label is better than a confident interpretation.

Example failure:

> “You’re terrified that everyone is conspiring against you.”

This combines certainty, a strong inferred emotion and endorsement of an unverified interpretation.

A stronger response might be:

> “It sounds like you’re feeling pretty uneasy about what’s been happening.”

---

## Exercise type E — improve a weak response

Show a plausible but flawed listener response and ask which revision is better.

Failure classes should include:

- advice too early;
- closed question when narrative is needed;
- stacked questions;
- “why” accusation;
- mind-reading;
- fake empathy (“I know exactly how you feel”);
- parroting;
- exaggerated emotion label;
- judgment disguised as an I message;
- summary that omits the emotional meaning;
- endless technique use after disengagement/help is appropriate.

This should become the highest-volume practice type because it teaches discriminations that ordinary flashcards miss.

---

## Exercise type F — summarize content + emotion

Give a 4–8 turn transcript and ask the learner to select the strongest summary.

The scoring key should require both:

1. **content:** the main events, concern or unresolved issue;
2. **emotion:** the emotional meaning supported by the transcript.

Distractors should include:

- fact-only summary;
- feeling-only summary;
- overly detailed recap;
- summary with invented motives;
- summary that turns into advice.

This is the best capstone micro-skill because it requires the learner to integrate the conversation rather than merely name a technique.

# 9. Scenario construction rules

## 9.1 Keep the speaker realistic

Do not write speakers as obvious teaching props who conveniently announce one emotion and one fact. Use mild ambiguity, partial information, repetition and ordinary conversational messiness.

## 9.2 Make multiple responses plausible

The learner should sometimes distinguish between two responses that are both socially acceptable but serve different purposes. The explanation should say why one is **better at this moment**, not claim the other would always be wrong.

## 9.3 Do not reward magic-word matching

If every mention of “angry” makes emotion label the correct answer, learners will memorize surface features instead of listening functions.

## 9.4 Include “do less” answers

Sometimes the best response is:

- a minimal encourager;
- a pause;
- a short reflection.

Not every turn needs a sophisticated sentence.

## 9.5 Include limits to engagement

Some transcripts should explicitly make further conversational practice inappropriate, for example:

- an immediate threat of violence;
- a medical emergency;
- a person who is unsafe to approach;
- a situation where the learner is being threatened or trapped;
- a scenario requiring emergency/professional help rather than continued dialogue.

The course should reward recognizing the boundary, not punish the learner for failing to find a clever listening phrase.

## 9.6 Avoid psychiatric theatre

Do not create cartoonishly “psychotic,” suicidal or violent speakers simply to make the course dramatic. If a high-risk scenario is needed to teach the boundary of civilian engagement, keep the required learner action at the level of **stop, create safety, seek appropriate help** rather than amateur negotiation.

# 10. Scoring model

The course should score two different competencies.

## 10.1 Recall score

Measures whether the learner can accurately recognize the framework.

Suggested dimensions:

- eight-response identification;
- technique purpose;
- key distinctions;
- Behavioral Change Stairway order at a high level.

This can use deterministic right/wrong scoring.

## 10.2 Application score

Measures whether the learner can apply the framework in authored transcripts.

Each response can be tagged against five dimensions:

| Dimension | Strong response | Weak response |
|---|---|---|
| Fidelity | stays within the speaker’s actual content | invents motives, facts or conclusions |
| Listening function | matches what is needed now | uses a technique because it is available |
| Emotional calibration | tentative and proportionate | mind-reading, diagnostic or exaggerated |
| Conversational timing | listens before advising/influencing | jumps to fixing, persuading or correcting |
| Safety boundary | recognizes when dialogue is inappropriate | treats listening as a substitute for safety action |

For multiple choice, each distractor should map to a defined failure tag so explanations remain diagnostic.

### Suggested mastery requirement

Completion should require both:

- high recall of the eight-response framework; and
- demonstrated application across **multiple technique families**, not merely an overall percentage inflated by easy identification items.

A learner should not pass while repeatedly failing paraphrase, emotion label and summary — the three responses most central to demonstrating actual understanding.

# 11. Common misconceptions the course should directly correct

## “Active listening means agreeing.”

False. A listener can accurately reflect content and acknowledge emotion without accepting the speaker’s factual claims, conclusions or proposed actions.

## “Emotion labeling means identifying the true emotion.”

False. It is a **tentative hypothesis** offered for correction, not mind-reading.

## “Mirroring and paraphrasing are the same.”

False. Reflection/mirroring repeats a short piece of the speaker’s wording. Paraphrasing restates meaning in new words.

## “Open questions are always better.”

False. A question can interrupt productive disclosure. Sometimes a pause, reflection or encourager is better.

## “Why questions are forbidden.”

Too strong. The FBI training caution is contextual: “why” can sound accusatory or interrogative, particularly in tense conversations. The course should teach the risk, not a universal grammar rule.

## “A technically correct phrase is automatically de-escalating.”

False. Timing, tone, authenticity, relationship, setting and risk all matter. Text practice can only test part of this.

## “The goal is to manipulate the other person into doing what you want.”

This is poor framing. The FBI model places active listening before empathy, rapport and influence because genuine understanding and relationship-building are prerequisites. Argus should teach listening as accurate, respectful communication, not as a covert compliance trick.

## “If listening is working, keep talking to the person.”

False. Safety and role boundaries override conversational practice.

## “Finishing this course means I can handle a real crisis.”

False. The course proves only the narrow transcript-based completion claim stated above.

# 12. Recommended lesson progression

The course should be compact enough to retain but long enough to move from naming techniques to using them in context.

## Lesson 1 — Listen before you solve

**Goal:** establish the core mental model.

Teach:

- active listening as the first stage of the FBI Behavioral Change Stairway;
- listening versus hearing;
- understanding before influence;
- premature advice/problem-solving as a common failure;
- Argus’s competence boundary.

Practice:

- choose listening versus advice;
- identify when the listener changed the subject too early;
- simple “what should happen next?” transcripts.

## Lesson 2 — Keep them talking: encourager, reflection, pause

**Goal:** learn the low-intervention responses.

Teach:

- minimal encourager;
- reflection/mirroring;
- effective pause;
- how they differ.

Practice:

- identify technique;
- choose between a question and a lower-load response;
- `[pause]` as a legitimate answer;
- detect robotic mirroring.

## Lesson 3 — Understand the story: paraphrase

**Goal:** faithfully restate content without adding interpretation.

Teach:

- meaning versus wording;
- paraphrase versus reflection;
- correction as a feature, not a failure;
- avoid added motives and premature solutions.

Practice:

- select best paraphrase;
- remove invented facts;
- improve loaded paraphrases.

## Lesson 4 — Understand the feeling: emotion label

**Goal:** acknowledge emotion without claiming certainty.

Teach:

- tentative labeling;
- emotion versus diagnosis;
- emotion acknowledgment versus factual agreement;
- proportional language.

Practice:

- choose a defensible label;
- soften overconfident labels;
- select “insufficient information” where appropriate;
- reject mind-reading.

## Lesson 5 — Invite more: open-ended questions

**Goal:** gather context without turning the interaction into interrogation.

Teach:

- open versus closed;
- “what/how” formulations;
- why “why” may sound accusatory;
- one question at a time;
- when not to ask another question.

Practice:

- rewrite closed or accusatory questions;
- choose between question, paraphrase and pause;
- identify stacked questions.

## Lesson 6 — Pull it together: summary

**Goal:** integrate factual and emotional meaning.

Teach:

- summary versus paraphrase;
- main points rather than transcript replay;
- content + emotion;
- checking rather than declaring.

Practice:

- 4–8 turn transcript summaries;
- choose the summary that preserves both story and feeling;
- detect invented coherence and advice disguised as summary.

## Lesson 7 — Boundaries and I messages

**Goal:** place the least “listening-like” FBI response in context and reinforce safety limits.

Teach:

- FBI I-message definition;
- use after understanding/rapport rather than as an opening move;
- first-person impact versus blame;
- when continued engagement is inappropriate;
- civilian versus trained crisis-negotiator role.

Practice:

- improve blame-heavy I messages;
- choose listening versus boundary statement;
- identify scenarios where disengagement/help outranks further ALS.

## Lesson 8 — Integrated transcript practice

**Goal:** choose among all eight responses based on context rather than technique prompts.

Practice should mix:

- identify technique;
- choose next response;
- paraphrase;
- emotion-label;
- improve weak response;
- summarize content + emotion;
- recognize when no further conversational technique is appropriate.

No item should tell the learner which technique to use.

## Completion check

Use fresh transcripts not seen in Learn.

Recommended shape:

- 4 recall items;
- 12 applied transcript items;
- at least 2 paraphrase items;
- at least 2 emotion-label items;
- at least 2 summary/integration items;
- at least 1 pause/minimal-response item;
- at least 1 boundary/disengagement item;
- remaining items mixed across the eight responses.

Pass only if the learner meets both the recall threshold and the applied threshold. Do not let perfect definition recall compensate for poor transcript judgement.

# 13. Product treatment

This should not be implemented as an eight-card vocabulary deck.

The recommended Argus treatment is:

- a small finite recall layer for the eight responses and key distinctions;
- a larger authored transcript-practice layer;
- deterministic rubrics and failure tags;
- immediate explanations tied to the listener’s function and timing;
- repeated contrast between **understanding** and **premature fixing**;
- recurring “insufficient information / do less / disengage” answers where warranted;
- no certification language;
- no tactical hostage-negotiation content.

A future generic scenario engine would serve this course well, but the research does not justify building a specialized “negotiator simulator.” The educational value comes from carefully authored conversational discriminations, not theatrical realism.

# 14. Source hierarchy and evidence notes

## Primary / official professional sources

1. Federal Bureau of Investigation. [Fifty Years of FBI Crisis (Hostage) Negotiation](https://leb.fbi.gov/articles/featured-articles/fifty-years-of-fbi-crisis-hostage-negotiation). *FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin*, 2024.  
   - Best source for programme history, 1990 ALS adoption, professional spread, Behavioral Change Stairway context, current training structure and HOBAS context.

2. Dalfonzo, V. A., & Deitrick, M. L. [An Evaluation Tool for Crisis Negotiators](https://leb.fbi.gov/articles/focus/focus-on-training-an-evaluation-tool-for-crisis-negotiators). *FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin*, 2015.  
   - Authority for the current eight-response ALS training/evaluation set and the CNU scoring approach.

3. Noesner, G. W., & Webster, M. [Crisis Intervention: Using Active Listening Skills in Negotiations](https://leb.fbi.gov/file-repository/archives/august-1997.pdf). *FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin*, 1997.  
   - Historical seven-technique formulation and conceptual foundation.

4. Bradley, G., & Jancewicz, J. [Leading Through Listening](https://leb.fbi.gov/articles/focus/focus-on-leadership-leading-through-listening). *FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin*, 2016.  
   - Official FBI discussion of ALS use beyond critical incidents.

5. Ontario. [O. Reg. 87/24: Training](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/240087).  
   - Establishes Ontario crisis-negotiator training and recurring qualification requirements; does not establish detailed ALS curriculum.

## Peer-reviewed research most relevant to Argus design

6. Van Hasselt, V. B., Baker, M. T., Romano, S. J., et al. [Crisis (Hostage) Negotiation Training: A Preliminary Evaluation of Program Efficacy](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093854805282328). *Criminal Justice and Behavior*, 2006.  
   - Pre/post evidence that FBI negotiation training improved active-listening role-play performance and reduced premature problem-solving.

7. Van Hasselt, V. B., Baker, M. T., Romano, S. J., et al. [Development and Validation of a Role-Play Test for Assessing Crisis (Hostage) Negotiation Skills](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093854804274374). *Criminal Justice and Behavior*, 2005.  
   - Supports scenario-based assessment; experts and nonexperts differed across active-listening categories.

8. Royce, T. [The Negotiator and the Bomber: Analyzing the Critical Role of Active Listening in Crisis Negotiations](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1571-9979.2005.00045.x). *Negotiation Journal*, 2005.  
   - Detailed real-incident discourse case; useful for contextual technique application, not causal generalization.

9. Weger, H., Bell, G. C., Minei, E. M., & Robinson, M. C. [The Relative Effectiveness of Active Listening in Initial Interactions](https://doi.org/10.1080/10904018.2013.813234). *International Journal of Listening*, 2014.  
   - Experimental evidence that active listening increased perceived understanding relative to advice and simple acknowledgements.

10. Kluger, A. N., Itzchakov, G., Weinstein, N., et al. [A Meta-analytic Systematic Review and Theory of the Effects of Perceived Listening on Work Outcomes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-023-09897-5). *Journal of Business and Psychology*, 2023.  
   - Broad evidence that perceived listening is positively associated with relationship and work outcomes, with substantial heterogeneity and methodological limitations.

11. Grubb, A. R., Brown, S. J., Hall, P., & Bowen, E. [Crisis (hostage) negotiators weigh in: the skills, behaviors, and qualities that characterize an expert crisis negotiator](https://doi.org/10.1080/15614263.2017.1419131). *Police Practice and Research*, 2018.  
   - International practitioner survey supporting the professional importance of active listening, empathy and calm communication.

## Evidence conclusion

The defensible evidence statement for Argus is:

> **FBI crisis-negotiation training has treated active listening as a core professional skill for decades; active-listening performance can be trained and discriminated in role-play; broader communication research supports benefits for perceived understanding and relationship quality. Evidence is substantially weaker for isolating the causal effect of individual ALS techniques on real crisis outcomes.**

That is strong enough to justify the course and narrow enough to avoid overstating competence or efficacy.
