# Situational Awareness I — Baseline, Anomaly, Reassess

**Status:** deep research / course recommendation  
**Date:** 2026-09-17  
**Issue:** #104  
**Implementation:** out of scope  
**Shipped:** no (checked 2026-09-25) — the completion claim needs scenario practice; see `../ISSUE_104_SCENARIO_ITEMS.md`  
**Jurisdictional anchor:** Ontario / Canada  

## Recommendation in one sentence

Build this as a small, scenario-led Argus course about **noticing context, detecting meaningful change, checking interpretation and updating as conditions change** — not as “street threat detection,” personality reading, tactical surveillance or permanent high alert.

---

## 1. Research conclusion

This is a strong Argus course candidate, but only if the title is interpreted narrowly.

Ontario already treats several of the core ideas as trainable knowledge. The province’s security-guard syllabus explicitly teaches observing the physical environment, attending to environmental details, situational awareness, recognizing typicality, detecting anomalies, establishing an initial benchmark for later observations, identifying hazards, assessing behaviour and prioritizing action. The private-investigator syllabus adds maintaining awareness/vigilance, recording relevant details and **consistently re-evaluating the situation**. Ontario’s ethical-decision and sensitivity material also requires attention to prejudice, stereotyping and one’s own biases.

That is a useful local professional anchor, but it is not evidence that civilians can be trained into reliable real-world threat detectors. The Argus course should therefore extract the defensible cognitive skill underneath the occupational material:

> establish context; notice relevant departures; distinguish observation from interpretation; seek missing information; choose a proportionate low-risk response; reassess when the situation changes.

The cognitive literature makes the limits equally important. People can miss salient events when attention is occupied, can fail to notice large visual changes, and become less reliable during prolonged monitoring. Rare targets are disproportionately easy to miss in visual-search tasks. These findings argue against promising “constant awareness” or complete scene coverage.

Hypervigilance is also the wrong goal. Canadian public-health material recognizes hypervigilance/hyperalertness as part of trauma-related arousal, and academic reviews distinguish useful orienting to relevant information from pervasive threat monitoring that can increase distraction and impair attentional control. The course should explicitly teach **calm, selective reassessment**, not “always be scanning for danger.”

Finally, an anomaly is not a threat and a person who differs from a local social norm is not an anomaly worth escalating. Ontario human-rights guidance defines racial profiling as safety/security action based on stereotypes rather than reasonable suspicion and distinguishes that from attention to actual behaviour or specific suspect information. Professional threat-assessment literature likewise cautions that there is no reliable demographic/personality profile of a violent actor and that isolated behaviours must be interpreted in context.

## 2. Exact competency and completion claim

### Competency claim

After completing **Situational Awareness I**, the learner can, in authored everyday public-space scenarios:

- establish a simple environmental baseline from observable context;
- identify a material change or anomaly **relative to that baseline** without automatically labelling it dangerous;
- separate direct observation from inference, stereotype and unsupported motive attribution;
- identify when information is insufficient and state what additional observation would reduce uncertainty;
- prioritize immediate hazards, access/egress, movement and escalating behaviour over irrelevant detail;
- choose a proportionate response from continuing normally, observing briefly, repositioning/leaving, reporting to responsible staff, or seeking emergency help;
- update the assessment when new information changes the baseline or meaning of an earlier observation.

### Completion claim

> **Completed Situational Awareness I:** demonstrated recall of the course’s baseline/anomaly/reassessment principles and applied them successfully across mixed authored Ontario public-space scenarios, including cases with no anomaly, benign anomalies, ambiguous behaviour and clear safety escalation.

Completion **does not mean** the learner:

- is professionally trained in surveillance, security, investigation, policing or behavioural threat assessment;
- can reliably predict violence or identify a “dangerous person”;
- will notice every hazard or important event in real life;
- has proved field competence under stress;
- has authority to investigate, detain, confront or search anyone.

This distinction is central to whether the course is honest enough to ship.

## 3. Core model: Baseline → Anomaly → Reassess

The course should use a three-part loop because it is memorable and closely matches the strongest Ontario source material.

### 3.1 Baseline

A **baseline** is a temporary description of what is normally happening **here, now, for this setting and purpose**.

It is not a demographic norm, a judgement about who “belongs,” or a fixed expectation that everyone should behave the same way.

Useful baseline dimensions:

- **environment:** lighting, weather, construction, visibility, surface conditions, obvious hazards;
- **movement:** pedestrian, bicycle and vehicle flow; crowd direction; queueing; platform movement;
- **access and egress:** usable exits, blocked routes, entrances, barriers, changing choke points;
- **activity:** what people are generally doing in that setting — boarding, waiting, crossing, queueing, dispersing;
- **social temperature:** calm, hurried, confused, argumentative, celebratory, distressed — described behaviourally rather than diagnostically;
- **operations:** delays, alarms, announcements, closures, staff activity, event conditions.

The learner should be taught to establish only a **coarse baseline**. Trying to encode the whole scene is unrealistic and counterproductive.

### 3.2 Anomaly

An **anomaly** is a departure from the current baseline that may deserve another look.

An anomaly is **not synonymous with danger**.

Examples:

- a previously open station exit is suddenly closed;
- a crowd that was flowing begins compressing around one point;
- a quiet argument becomes shouting plus repeated movement into another person’s space;
- a vehicle begins moving contrary to the expected traffic pattern;
- an object appears where it creates a trip or access hazard;
- an alarm, smoke, strong unusual odour or emergency announcement changes the environment.

The course should deliberately include benign anomalies:

- a person running because they are catching a train;
- staff moving against passenger flow because they are responding to a service issue;
- a traveller repeatedly looking at exits because they are lost;
- a crowd abruptly changing direction after a platform announcement.

The learner must learn that **“unusual” is a prompt to update, not a verdict**.

### 3.3 Reassess

Reassessment prevents the baseline from becoming stale and prevents the first interpretation from becoming an anchor.

The learner should ask:

1. What changed?
2. What do I directly know?
3. What am I inferring?
4. What information would change the assessment?
5. Does this require any action now?
6. What changed after that action or new information?

A good scenario should often change meaning halfway through. For example, movement against crowd flow may initially appear anomalous; a later fire alarm may make it the appropriate evacuation route. The scored skill is updating rather than defending the first answer.

## 4. Concepts worth teaching

### 4.1 Observation before interpretation

Direct observation should be separated from explanation.

- Observation: “The person moved from one doorway to another three times in five minutes.”
- Interpretation: “The person is casing the building.”

The second statement may be one hypothesis, but the first is what is actually known.

This is important enough that the course should score it repeatedly, even though **Observation versus Inference** may later become its own Argus course.

### 4.2 Context determines whether a deviation matters

The same behaviour can mean different things in different settings. Running at a subway platform, concert exit, jogging trail and roadway is not one category of concern.

The course should train comparison against **place + time + current conditions**, not against a learner’s idea of “normal people.”

### 4.3 Prioritize consequential categories

A civilian learner cannot attend to everything. Teach a short prioritization order:

1. immediate physical hazard;
2. route/access/egress changes;
3. vehicle and crowd movement;
4. clear behavioural escalation affecting safety;
5. operational information such as alarms, closures and staff direction;
6. other detail only if it becomes relevant.

This is preferable to vague advice to “keep your head on a swivel.”

### 4.4 Attention is limited

Teach the learner why confidence in perception should be modest.

Relevant findings:

- **inattentional blindness:** an unexpected event can go unnoticed when attention is occupied elsewhere;
- **change blindness:** even large changes can be missed across views;
- **vigilance decrement:** sustained monitoring for infrequent signals tends to become less reliable over time;
- **low-prevalence effect:** rare visual targets can be missed disproportionately often.

The practical lesson is not “scan harder.” It is:

> use simple priorities, reduce avoidable distraction when conditions warrant it, and reassess at natural transition points.

Natural transition points include entering a station, stepping onto a platform, boarding, leaving a venue, crossing a road, moving into a parking area, hearing an alarm, noticing a crowd change, or changing route.

### 4.5 Awareness is not memory perfection

The learner should not be asked to memorize clothing, faces, vehicle plates and all environmental detail continuously.

Argus should reward noticing **what matters to the current decision** and, where reporting is needed, recording concrete details soon afterward.

### 4.6 Hypervigilance is not advanced situational awareness

This course should explicitly reject the idea that a person should remain permanently alert for threats.

Canadian public-health guidance describes trauma-related hyperalertness/hypervigilance alongside concentration problems and other arousal symptoms. Academic work on anxiety-related attention describes hypervigilance as pervasive monitoring for potential danger that may improve some threat detection while also increasing distraction and reducing task focus; systematic review evidence in PTSD does not support a simple claim that hypervigilance produces reliably superior threat detection.

Argus should teach:

- brief, purposeful checks;
- attention appropriate to context;
- return to ordinary activity when nothing warrants escalation;
- no gamification of fear, paranoia or permanent scanning.

The course must never diagnose a learner or another person based on hypervigilant-looking behaviour.

### 4.7 Behaviour is more useful than profile

A person’s race, ethnicity, religion, disability, housing status, clothing style, age, gender expression, accent or apparent mental-health condition is not a safety anomaly.

The OHRC’s distinction is useful: stereotype-based safety scrutiny is profiling; legitimate concern must instead rest on actual behaviour or specific information.

The course should therefore phrase behavioural observations concretely:

- blocking another person’s path;
- repeatedly attempting unwanted physical contact;
- escalating threats;
- tampering with a restricted door;
- stepping into live traffic;
- moving toward a track edge despite warnings;
- abandoning an object in a way that creates an immediate hazard;
- ignoring evacuation instructions while interfering with others.

Even these behaviours do not automatically establish motive or future violence. The learner’s task is deciding what low-risk response is proportionate **now**.

### 4.8 Reposition, report or leave before amateur investigation

For a civilian course, anomaly recognition should lead toward safer options rather than closer inspection or confrontation.

Useful response categories:

- **continue:** nothing material changed;
- **observe briefly:** ambiguity matters but no immediate danger is apparent;
- **reposition / leave:** improve distance, visibility or access to an exit;
- **report:** tell responsible staff or use the applicable reporting channel;
- **emergency help:** call 9-1-1 or use the venue/transit emergency mechanism when immediate police, fire or medical help is required.

For Toronto transit scenarios, TTC guidance provides concrete options: speak to staff, use station/vehicle intercoms, use SafeTTC for discreet reporting, and use emergency alarms or 9-1-1 for actual emergencies.

## 5. Realistic scenario set

Scenarios should look like ordinary Ontario life rather than action-thriller setups. They should be short enough for repeated judgment practice and contain enough irrelevant detail to test prioritization without becoming a hidden-object game.

### Transit

**1. Platform crowd change**  
A normal evening platform becomes increasingly crowded after a delay. The learner should identify crowd compression and blocked movement as the relevant change, not select a particular passenger as “suspicious.” A later announcement changes which exit is usable, forcing reassessment.

**2. Person moving against flow**  
A person repeatedly walks opposite the flow on a platform. Initial answer: anomalous but insufficient information. New information: they are following station signs after missing a transfer. Correct update: benign explanation; no action.

**3. Escalating interaction on a streetcar**  
Two riders begin with a verbal disagreement. One then blocks the aisle and repeatedly closes distance after the other tries to disengage. The learner should track the behavioural change, preserve distance and choose staff/SafeTTC/emergency options according to severity rather than intervene physically.

**4. Unattended item near an access route**  
An item is left where it obstructs movement. The first concern is the concrete access/trip hazard; the learner should not be trained to inspect, touch or diagnose the object. Reporting to staff is the proportionate response absent emergency indicators.

### Street and road

**5. Construction changes a familiar route**  
A normally open sidewalk is closed and sight lines at a crossing are reduced. The learner should update the baseline rather than proceed on habit.

**6. Vehicle movement anomaly**  
A delivery vehicle begins reversing across a pedestrian path while several people are looking at phones. The correct focus is vehicle movement and escape space, not the driver’s appearance or presumed competence.

**7. Nighttime route choice**  
The learner exits a building and finds the usual well-lit route closed. The exercise is to notice the environmental change, identify alternatives and avoid converting ordinary strangers on the alternative route into “threat cues.”

### Venue / public space

**8. Event dispersal**  
After a concert or sports event, normal pedestrian flow changes rapidly. A learner who keeps using the pre-event baseline should fail. The correct response is to rebuild the baseline around current crowd and traffic conditions.

**9. Queue dispute**  
A loud disagreement occurs in a line. Loud speech alone is not scored as danger. The scenario adds or withholds concrete escalation behaviours such as threats, blocked movement or unwanted contact to test proportionality.

**10. Alarm with conflicting social cues**  
An alarm sounds while many people continue sitting because no staff member reacts immediately. The learner should prioritize the alarm/official instruction over social proof and seek current information.

### Everyday indoor space

**11. Café / library baseline**  
A person repeatedly looks toward an exit and checks a phone. That is insufficient to infer criminal intent. New information reveals they are waiting for a pickup. This is an anti-profiling and anti-mind-reading case.

**12. Building access change**  
A door normally used for egress is propped, then later blocked by deliveries. The learner should notice the operational/access change and choose a reporting or alternate-route response rather than investigate who caused it.

Every scenario family should include:

- no anomaly;
- benign anomaly;
- ambiguous anomaly requiring more information;
- clear environmental hazard;
- clear behavioural escalation;
- a mid-scenario update that reverses or materially changes the initial assessment.

## 6. Common errors and misconceptions

The course should explicitly teach against these errors.

### “Situational awareness means noticing everything.”

False. Attention and visual memory are limited. The practical skill is prioritization and updating.

### “An anomaly is a threat.”

False. It is only a departure from the current baseline.

### “The baseline tells me who belongs here.”

False. Baselines describe current environmental and behavioural patterns, not demographic legitimacy.

### “If someone looks nervous, evasive, unusual or out of place, that is evidence of danger.”

False. These are vague interpretations with many benign explanations. Score concrete behaviour instead.

### “My intuition is enough.”

Treat intuition as a prompt to identify the observations producing concern, not as proof. The learner should be able to answer: **what did I actually observe?**

### “Once I understand the scene, I am done.”

False. Conditions change; reassessment is the defining skill.

### “Constant scanning makes me safer.”

Not supported as a general civilian rule. Sustained monitoring is cognitively costly, and hypervigilance is not the target state.

### “More details always improve awareness.”

False. Irrelevant detail competes for attention. The course should score decision-relevant detail.

### “If I am concerned, I should approach to find out what is happening.”

False as a default civilian rule. Distance, leaving and reporting are often safer ways to reduce uncertainty.

### “People who break a social norm are suspicious.”

False. Behavioural or cultural difference is not itself evidence of danger.

### “Training removes inattentional blindness or vigilance limits.”

False. The course can improve habits and judgment in authored practice; it should not claim to remove basic cognitive limits.

## 7. Finite recall versus scenario-practice material

### Finite recall

These are stable enough for ordinary Argus retrieval and spaced review:

- definition of a contextual baseline;
- definition of anomaly as deviation, not threat;
- reassessment loop: **what changed → what is observed → what is inferred → what is missing → what action is proportionate → what changed next**;
- observation versus inference distinction;
- priority categories: hazard, egress/access, movement, behavioural escalation, operational information;
- attention limits: inattentional blindness, change blindness, vigilance decrement, low-prevalence misses;
- hypervigilance is not the course objective;
- behaviour/context over demographic or identity profile;
- uncertainty is a valid answer;
- civilian response categories: continue, observe briefly, reposition/leave, report, emergency help;
- 9-1-1 is for emergencies requiring immediate police, fire or medical assistance;
- TTC-specific reporting mechanisms should be available as Ontario/Toronto reference material, not treated as universal knowledge.

Recommended finite set: **18–24 propositions**, written as concepts and discriminations rather than glossary trivia.

### Scenario practice

These should not be reduced to recall cards:

- establishing a baseline from a scene;
- selecting which change is material;
- distinguishing meaningful change from irrelevant novelty;
- identifying missing information;
- resisting profile-based or motive-based distractors;
- updating after new information;
- choosing a proportionate response;
- recognizing when no action is required.

Recommended authored set: **18–24 scenarios**, with parameterized variants if Argus later supports them. A learner should not be able to pass by memorizing that “the unusual thing is always the answer.”

## 8. Assessment model

A single multiple-choice fact test is not sufficient.

Use three layers.

### Layer A — finite recall

Short retrieval items for the stable concepts above.

Do not ask learners to recall source trivia, academic names or professional jargon merely because it appeared in the research.

### Layer B — observation and classification

Present a short scene, image sequence or text scenario and ask one focused question:

- Which statement is directly observed?
- Which change is actually new relative to the baseline?
- Which detail is irrelevant to the safety decision?
- Is there enough information to infer a problem?

Include **“insufficient information”** and **“no material anomaly”** as frequently correct answers.

### Layer C — dynamic reassessment

Use two- or three-step scenarios. After the learner commits to an initial assessment, reveal new information and require an updated response.

The score should reward changing one’s mind when evidence changes.

### Completion threshold

Recommended product rule:

- complete the finite recall set;
- pass a mixed **12-scenario capstone** at **≥80% overall**;
- additionally answer **all critical-boundary items correctly**: profile-vs-behaviour, anomaly-not-equal-threat, emergency escalation, and willingness to revise after disconfirming information;
- allow bounded retry with different scenarios rather than repeating identical answer positions/content.

The exact percentage is a product threshold, not an empirically validated certification standard. It exists to make Argus completion finite and meaningful, not to imply professional qualification.

### What should not be assessed

Do not score:

- speed as a proxy for awareness;
- face/clothing memorization unrelated to a decision;
- “spot the dangerous person” image tests;
- body-language lie detection;
- demographic matching;
- tactical confrontation choices;
- willingness to take personal risk.

## 9. Safety and anti-profiling boundaries

These are course requirements, not optional disclaimers.

### Boundary 1 — No demographic threat cues

Never author “correct” concern based on race, ethnicity, religion, nationality, housing status, disability, sex, gender expression, clothing associated with a community, accent or perceived mental illness.

A specific official suspect description is a different category, but it is outside this introductory course.

### Boundary 2 — Behaviour must be concrete

Use observable actions, not labels such as “sketchy,” “evasive,” “unstable,” “aggressive-looking,” “doesn’t belong” or “acting suspicious.”

### Boundary 3 — One anomaly cannot establish harmful intent

Even professionally sourced threat-assessment material warns against profiles and isolated warning signs. Situational Awareness I should stop well before formal threat assessment.

### Boundary 4 — No amateur diagnosis

Do not infer intoxication, psychosis, mania, developmental disability or other health status from appearance/behaviour. A learner may notice observable impairment or distress only when it matters to immediate safety.

### Boundary 5 — No confrontation requirement

The course should prefer distance, exiting, reporting and emergency services when appropriate. It should not teach the learner to challenge, follow, search or test a person.

### Boundary 6 — No covert-surveillance instruction

Ontario PI material contains surveillance methods that are occupationally relevant but not needed here. Do not import tailing, concealment, surveillance positioning or methods for avoiding detection.

### Boundary 7 — No “all five senses” literalism

Ontario training suggests using all senses as part of professional observation. A civilian Argus adaptation should **not** encourage touching, tasting, closely smelling or approaching unknown substances or objects. Use available safe sensory information, principally sight and hearing, without increasing exposure.

### Boundary 8 — No promise of personal safety

The course can improve structured noticing in authored scenarios. It cannot guarantee crime prevention, hazard detection or safe outcomes.

## 10. Recommended course structure and size

This should be larger than an ordinary finite reference topic but much smaller than Morse.

### Lesson 1 — Build a baseline

- context is local and temporary;
- environment, movement, access/egress, activity, operations;
- baseline is not a profile of who belongs;
- 3 short scenarios.

### Lesson 2 — Notice anomalies without inventing threats

- material change versus irrelevant novelty;
- benign, ambiguous and consequential anomalies;
- observation versus interpretation;
- 4 scenarios.

### Lesson 3 — Reassess

- first impressions are provisional;
- missing information;
- updating after announcements, movement, new behaviour or environmental change;
- 4 branching scenarios.

### Lesson 4 — Know the limits of attention

- inattentional blindness;
- change blindness;
- vigilance decrement;
- rare-target misses;
- why “notice everything” and “always scan” are bad completion claims;
- 3 scenarios/demonstrations.

### Lesson 5 — Behaviour, bias and proportionate action

- behaviour/context versus stereotype/profile;
- no single anomaly proves intent;
- continue / observe briefly / reposition or leave / report / emergency help;
- TTC/Toronto localized reference examples;
- 4 scenarios.

### Capstone — Mixed public-space reassessment

- 12 scored scenarios drawn across street, transit, venue and indoor-public-space contexts;
- at least 2 no-anomaly cases;
- at least 2 benign-anomaly cases;
- at least 2 cases where new information reverses the initial interpretation;
- at least 2 anti-profiling boundary cases;
- at least 2 clear hazard/emergency escalation cases.

### Total size

Recommended initial authoring budget:

- **5 lessons + 1 capstone**;
- **18–24 finite recall propositions**;
- **18–24 teaching scenarios** plus the 12-scenario capstone pool, with overlap only if the presentation meaningfully changes;
- approximately **35–50 minutes** for a deliberate first pass, split into phone-sized sittings;
- later review should focus on the finite propositions and occasional fresh scenario checks rather than replaying the whole course.

This is enough material to create a real competency boundary without turning the course into professional security training.

## 11. Evidence base and source hierarchy

### Ontario / Canadian authority — primary anchors

1. **Ontario — Security guard training syllabus, Section 3: Basic security procedures**  
   Direct support for physical-environment observation, situational awareness, recognizing typicality and anomalies, establishing a benchmark, identifying hazards, behaviour/environment assessment, prioritization and role-play.  
   https://www.ontario.ca/document/training-syllabus-security-guards/section-3-basic-security-procedures

2. **Ontario — Private investigator training syllabus, Section 5: Investigative techniques**  
   Direct support for environmental observation, unusual situations, maintaining awareness/vigilance, accurate relevant detail and consistently re-evaluating the situation. Occupational surveillance techniques beyond those concepts should not be imported.  
   https://www.ontario.ca/document/training-syllabus-private-investigators/section-5-investigative-techniques

3. **Ontario — Private investigator syllabus, Section 6: Ethical reasoning and decision-making**  
   Direct support for pairing pattern/typicality/anomaly recognition with discrimination, prejudice and stereotyping controls.  
   https://www.ontario.ca/document/training-syllabus-private-investigators/section-6-principles-ethical-reasoning-and-decision-making

4. **Ontario — Security guard syllabus, Section 10: Sensitivity training**  
   Direct support for recognizing personal bias and how bias influences interpretation and interaction.  
   https://www.ontario.ca/document/training-syllabus-security-guards/section-10-sensitivity-training

5. **Ontario Human Rights Commission — What is racial profiling?**  
   Strong Ontario boundary: profiling relies on stereotypes for safety/security scrutiny, while legitimate criminal profiling relies on actual behaviour or information about suspected activity.  
   https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/paying-price-human-cost-racial-profiling/what-racial-profiling

6. **Ontario Human Rights Commission — Policy on eliminating racial profiling in law enforcement**  
   Stronger detailed source on individualized, evidence-based decision-making and the harms/ineffectiveness of stereotype-driven safety decisions.  
   https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-eliminating-racial-profiling-law-enforcement

7. **Public Health Agency of Canada — About trauma and PTSD**  
   Canadian caution against romanticizing permanent high alert; describes hyperalertness as feeling continually on edge or in danger.  
   https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/topics/mental-health-wellness/post-traumatic-stress-disorder/about-ptsd.html

8. **Public Health Agency of Canada — Trauma terminology glossary**  
   Canadian clinical terminology linking hypervigilance with hyperarousal, concentration difficulty and trauma-related symptoms.  
   https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/reports-publications/health-promotion-chronic-disease-prevention-canada-research-policy-practice/vol-43-no-10-11-2023/glossary-common-terms-psychological-trauma-version-3-0.html

9. **TTC — SafeTTC app / safety and security**  
   Localized reporting route for authored Toronto transit scenarios; distinguishes discreet reporting from emergency police/fire/medical response.  
   https://www.ttc.ca/riding-the-ttc/safety-and-security/safe-ttc-app

10. **TTC — Travelling safely / emergency protocols**  
    Localized reference for staff, intercoms, emergency alarms, SafeTTC and 9-1-1.  
    https://www.ttc.ca/riding-the-ttc/safety-and-security/travelling-safely

11. **City of Toronto — Make the Right Call**  
    Localized escalation boundary: 9-1-1 for emergencies requiring immediate police, fire or paramedic response; other channels for non-emergencies.  
    https://www.toronto.ca/home/311-toronto-at-your-service/make-the-right-call/

### Strong academic / professional supplements

12. **Simons & Chabris (1999), _Gorillas in our midst: sustained inattentional blindness for dynamic events_**  
    Foundational evidence that focused tasks can make salient unexpected events go unnoticed.  
    https://www.chabris.com/Simons1999.pdf

13. **Simons & Ambinder (2005), _Change Blindness: Theory and Consequences_**  
    Review showing substantial limits in encoding, retaining and comparing scene information across views.  
    https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00332.x

14. **Thomson, Smilek & Besner (2015), _Reducing the vigilance decrement: The effects of perceptual variability_**  
    Supports the general point that detection performance during sustained monitoring tends to decline over time; use as a limit, not as a civilian operational prescription.  
    https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1053810015000392

15. **Wolfe et al., _Low target prevalence is a stubborn source of errors in visual search tasks_**  
    Evidence that rare targets are disproportionately missed in visual search and that the effect is not simply eliminated by exhorting observers to be careful.  
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2662480/

16. **Richards et al. (2014), _Exploring the function of selective attention and hypervigilance for threat in anxiety_**  
    Useful distinction between selective attention and pervasive hypervigilance; highlights the trade-off between threat monitoring and distraction/task focus.  
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24286750/

17. **Lazarov et al. — systematic review of attention to threat in PTSD**  
    Important corrective: the reviewed eye-tracking literature found little support for a simple enhanced-threat-detection account of PTSD hypervigilance, with stronger evidence for sustained attention once threat is engaged.  
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30178728/

18. **U.S. Secret Service NTAC — Mass Attacks in Public Spaces, 2016–2020**  
    Professional boundary source: no attacker profile; concerning behaviours must be interpreted in context; many pre-attack behaviours are not suspicious on their own. Use only for the behaviour-vs-profile boundary, not to turn this course into threat-assessment training.  
    https://www.secretservice.gov/sites/default/files/reports/2023-01/usss-ntac-maps-2016-2020.pdf

### Evidence limitations

- Ontario security/PI syllabi are **occupational training standards**, not randomized evidence that these methods improve civilian safety outcomes.
- Laboratory attention research establishes important cognitive limits, but it does not directly prescribe an optimal street-awareness scanning pattern.
- Hypervigilance research largely concerns anxiety/PTSD populations; it supports a boundary against permanent threat monitoring, not diagnosis of learners or strangers.
- U.S. professional threat-assessment material is supplementary and jurisdictionally secondary. It should support only general anti-profile/context principles in this course.
- No source reviewed supports a civilian promise that a compact app course can make someone reliably detect violent intent before an incident.

## 12. Ship / revise / reject

**Recommendation: SHIP — with the narrow treatment defined here.**

This should become an Argus **practical scenario course**, not an ordinary flashcard deck and not a “street smarts” or threat-detection course.

### Proposed Argus treatment

- Title: **Situational Awareness I — Baseline, Anomaly, Reassess**.
- Treat the three-part loop as the memorable spine, not as a claim of a proprietary scientific model.
- Use Ontario’s security/PI curricula as the local source anchor while clearly adapting away from occupational surveillance powers and tactics.
- Pair every anomaly lesson with observation-vs-inference and anti-profiling checks.
- Make **“no material anomaly”**, **“benign explanation”** and **“insufficient information”** routinely correct answers.
- Teach attention limits and hypervigilance early enough to prevent the learner from interpreting the course as permanent threat scanning.
- Use ordinary Toronto/Ontario transit, street, venue and indoor-public-space scenarios.
- Make dynamic reassessment the main applied assessment: new evidence should sometimes require the learner to reverse the first judgment.
- Keep the completion claim to authored knowledge/judgment performance. Do not imply certification, professional surveillance ability, violence prediction or demonstrated real-world safety competence.

On that basis, this is one of the strongest first courses in the #104 practical-skills expansion because it creates a reusable learning shape for later observation, threat-recognition, de-escalation and field-reporting courses without prematurely teaching those subjects themselves.
