# Psychological First Aid — Canada-first research

**Status:** deep research for issue #104; implementation out of scope  
**Date:** 2026-09-17  
**Primary authority:** Canadian Red Cross Psychological First Aid (PFA) material  
**Corroborating authorities:** WHO; International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement/IFRC; Public Health Agency of Canada; major reviews of the PFA evidence base

## Executive recommendation

**Ship as an Argus companion course, not as a substitute for formal PFA training.**

Psychological First Aid fits Argus well because it has a finite knowledge core and a strong scenario-practice layer. The Canadian Red Cross currently teaches a **Look, Listen, Link, Live** model for recognizing stress, supporting a distressed person, connecting them to practical and social supports, and maintaining the helper's own coping and resilience. The formal Red Cross course is case-based, which independently supports the Argus design choice to assess application through authored scenarios rather than through recall alone.

The course must remain tightly bounded. PFA is civilian, humane, practical support. It is not diagnosis, psychotherapy, counselling, compulsory emotional disclosure, psychological debriefing, suicide-risk assessment training, or a replacement for emergency, medical, mental-health or safeguarding services.

The research base also requires careful wording. PFA is strongly supported by international guidance and professional consensus, and its component principles align with evidence on safety, calming, efficacy, connectedness and hope. However, direct outcome evidence for PFA as a packaged intervention remains limited and heterogeneous. Argus should therefore teach PFA as **recommended supportive practice**, not claim that it prevents PTSD, treats mental illness, or reliably changes clinical outcomes.

# 1. Exact Argus competency boundary

## Completion claim

> **Can recall the Canadian Red Cross Look, Listen, Link, Live framework; recognize observable signs that someone may be stressed or distressed without diagnosing them; choose calm, non-intrusive and practical supportive responses; connect a person to appropriate personal, community or professional supports; and recognize when ordinary civilian PFA is insufficient and escalation is warranted in authored scenarios.**

This is the strongest defensible completion claim for an app-based course.

Completion **does not mean** that the learner:

- is certified by the Canadian Red Cross;
- has completed the Canadian Red Cross Psychological First Aid course;
- is trained in Mental Health First Aid or another mental-health emergency programme;
- can diagnose a mental disorder, trauma disorder, substance-use disorder or other clinical condition;
- can conduct psychotherapy, counselling, crisis assessment or suicide-risk assessment;
- can safely manage every distressed or violent person;
- can replace professional, emergency, medical, child-protection or organizational response;
- has demonstrated real-world PFA competence under stress.

Argus should display the completion boundary in the course description and completion state, not hide it in secondary documentation.

# 2. What Psychological First Aid is

The Canadian Red Cross describes its PFA programme as a resiliency-based approach to emotional, psychological and social well-being. Current Canadian Red Cross material emphasizes recognizing stress in oneself and others, taking simple practical steps to reduce distress and support recovery, and using the **Look, Listen, Link, Live** model. Its formal course covers stress, distress, loss, grief, trauma, supportive communication, resiliency, protective factors, protection and self-care.

WHO defines PFA in similarly restrained terms: humane, supportive and practical assistance to people who have experienced serious stressors. Its core actions include assessing needs, helping with basic needs, listening without pressuring a person to talk, helping them feel calm, connecting them with information and social supports, and protecting them from further harm.

The IFRC/MHPSS Hub likewise treats PFA as a direct set of actions for helping someone in distress. International Red Cross/Red Crescent material commonly uses **Look, Listen, Link**; the Canadian Red Cross extends this into **Look, Listen, Link, Live**, with a particular emphasis on resiliency, coping and helper self-care.

A useful Argus definition is:

> **Psychological First Aid is respectful, non-intrusive practical and emotional support for a person under significant stress, aimed at immediate safety, needs, connection and coping rather than diagnosis or treatment.**

This definition keeps the subject useful without drifting into clinical territory.

---

# 3. What PFA explicitly is not

WHO's PFA guidance is unusually clear about the negative boundary. PFA is **not** professional counselling, psychological debriefing, a detailed reconstruction of the distressing event, or pressure on a person to describe feelings or reactions. It is also not reserved to mental-health professionals; appropriately bounded supportive actions can be performed by ordinary helpers.

For Argus, this becomes a core contrast set:

| PFA | Not PFA |
| --- | --- |
| Asking what the person needs now | Diagnosing what disorder they have |
| Listening if they want to talk | Pressuring them to recount the event |
| Helping with food, shelter, transport, information or contact | Conducting therapy or trauma processing |
| Acknowledging emotion without claiming certainty | Telling them what they must be feeling |
| Connecting them to trusted people or services | Trying to become their sole source of support |
| Supporting choice and agency | Taking over decisions that the person can make |
| Calm, truthful reassurance about what is actually known | False reassurance or promises that cannot be kept |
| Escalating when safety or need exceeds the helper's role | Persisting beyond competence because the helper wants to solve the problem |

This distinction should be assessed repeatedly. Many weak responses sound compassionate but are still intrusive, overconfident or outside scope.

# 4. The Canadian Red Cross Look, Listen, Link, Live framework

## 4.1 Look

**Purpose:** notice safety, observable stress/distress, urgent needs, available strengths and whether the situation requires more than ordinary peer support.

Canadian Red Cross material directs helpers to look for signs of stress and distress, non-verbal communication, unmet basic needs, violence or risk of harm, people who may require additional attention, and signs of resilience. WHO's related action principle similarly begins with safety, urgent basic needs and serious distress reactions.

For Argus, **Look must never become amateur diagnosis**. The learner should identify observations such as:

- crying, shaking, marked agitation or withdrawal;
- difficulty concentrating or following simple information;
- confusion or apparent disorientation;
- inability to attend to immediate basic needs;
- direct statements about self-harm, suicide or harming another person;
- unsafe surroundings, injury or urgent medical concerns;
- a marked change from a known personal baseline;
- available strengths: calm companions, family contact, transport, shelter, existing coping strategies, community or cultural supports.

The learner should not infer psychiatric labels from appearance or behaviour. “She has PTSD,” “he is psychotic,” or “they are having a panic disorder episode” are outside this course unless the person themselves reports an established diagnosis and it is directly relevant to linking them with their own care.

### Argus teaching rule

> **Observe needs and behaviour; do not convert observations into diagnoses.**

## 4.2 Listen

**Purpose:** make respectful contact, identify the person's priorities, and offer calm attention without forcing disclosure.

Canadian Red Cross material emphasizes approaching calmly, being open to the person's experience and asking whether help is wanted. WHO adds that the helper should ask about needs and concerns, listen if the person wants to speak, avoid pressuring them, and help a very distressed person feel calmer and less alone.

Useful civilian behaviours include:

- introduce yourself when appropriate and explain why you are there;
- ask permission to help rather than assuming help is wanted;
- use a calm tone and simple language;
- ask what the person needs most right now;
- allow silence;
- use short reflections or summaries to confirm understanding;
- acknowledge emotion without asserting a diagnosis or motive;
- do not interrogate for details that are not needed for safety or practical help;
- do not force a person to talk because “processing it” is assumed to be therapeutic.

### Good Argus response pattern

> “That sounds like a lot to deal with at once. What would be most useful right now?”

### Weak response patterns

- “Tell me exactly what happened from the beginning.”
- “You need to get all of this out.”
- “I know exactly how you feel.”
- “You're having a trauma response.”
- “Calm down.”
- “Everything is going to be fine.”

The problem is not merely tone. These responses can pressure disclosure, claim knowledge the helper does not have, diagnose, command emotion or make promises unsupported by facts.

## 4.3 Link

**Purpose:** connect the person to concrete resources, information, trusted people and services that match the need.

This is where PFA becomes explicitly practical. Canadian Red Cross material includes basic-needs resources, loved ones, community/cultural/faith networks and professional or emergency services when necessary. WHO and IFRC similarly emphasize addressing immediate needs, giving accurate information and connecting people to social support and services.

Argus should teach **support by connection rather than support by takeover**.

Examples:

- help identify a safe place to wait;
- assist with a phone call or transportation if wanted;
- connect a person with family, a trusted friend, Elder, faith/community support or workplace support;
- help locate food, shelter, medical care or other immediate practical resources;
- provide accurate information, and say when information is unknown;
- help the person identify their existing coping resources;
- connect to professional or crisis support when the person's needs exceed ordinary peer support.

The helper should not imply that every distressed person needs professional mental-health care. Many crisis reactions are temporary and people differ in what support they want. The role is to notice need, offer options and escalate when the situation warrants it.

### Argus teaching rule

> **Do with, not for, when the person can safely act for themselves.**

## 4.4 Live

**Purpose:** continue with sustainable coping, protective factors and self-care rather than treating PFA as a one-off emotional intervention.

“Live” is the Canadian Red Cross addition that most clearly separates its model from the WHO/IFRC three-action wording. Current Red Cross self-care material frames Live around ongoing coping practices, healthy relationships, boundaries, breaks, asking for help, and maintaining a self-care plan. Canadian Red Cross material on caring for others also emphasizes the helper's own care before, during and after stressful situations.

Argus should teach two interpretations together:

- **for the person being supported:** help restore ordinary coping, choice, routines and existing supportive relationships where possible;
- **for the helper:** monitor personal stress, keep boundaries, use one's own support systems and step away or hand over when capacity is exceeded.

Argus should not turn Live into lifestyle prescription. It should not tell a distressed person that exercise, mindfulness, sleep hygiene or another generic habit is the solution to a crisis. The relevant principle is sustainable coping and connection chosen by the person, not a wellness checklist imposed by the helper.

# 5. Stress and distress: what a civilian learner should recognize

The course needs a small observation vocabulary, but not a diagnostic taxonomy.

## Finite signs worth teaching

A learner should recognize that significant stress can present across several domains:

- **physical:** fatigue, headaches, stomach symptoms, shaking, rapid breathing or other visible signs of arousal;
- **cognitive:** difficulty concentrating, remembering or making simple decisions;
- **emotional:** anxiety, sadness, anger, fear, irritability, numbness or overwhelm;
- **behavioural:** withdrawal, unusual risk-taking, increased substance use, marked agitation or reduced self-care;
- **interpersonal:** conflict, isolation, difficulty engaging with usual supports;
- **functional:** inability to manage basic immediate tasks that the person would ordinarily be expected to handle.

The learner must also know that:

- reactions vary substantially between people;
- visible distress is not proof of mental illness;
- calm presentation is not proof that someone is coping well;
- a single sign rarely establishes what is happening;
- cultural, personal and situational context matters;
- the relevant question for PFA is usually **“what support or safety need is present?”**, not **“what diagnosis explains this?”**

## “Insufficient information” is a correct answer

Scenarios should sometimes reward the learner for refusing to infer more than the scene supports.

Example:

> A coworker is quiet, avoids eye contact and leaves a meeting early after receiving bad news.

A valid answer is not “they are depressed.” A stronger answer is: there may be distress, but the observed behaviour is not diagnostic; a private, non-intrusive check-in may be appropriate.

# 6. Practical assistance before amateur counselling

One of the strongest cross-source themes is that PFA is practical.

A distressed person may need:

- accurate information;
- a safe place;
- water, food, medication access or shelter;
- transport;
- help contacting a loved one;
- privacy;
- medical attention;
- help navigating an unfamiliar service;
- a trusted person to remain nearby;
- time without being pressured to explain what happened.

Argus scenarios should deliberately include cases in which the best next action is **not** a sophisticated emotional response.

Example:

> A person evacuated from an apartment fire is visibly shaken and repeatedly says they cannot find their phone or contact their partner.

The best response may be to help them access a phone, locate the reunification process or contact the relevant service—not to ask them to describe the fire in detail.

This is an important corrective to the common misconception that psychological support primarily means talking about feelings.

# 7. Autonomy, dignity, privacy and consent

WHO guidance emphasizes honesty, respect for the person's decisions, attention to one's own biases, appropriate confidentiality and cultural respect. It explicitly warns helpers not to exploit the relationship, make false promises, exaggerate their skills, force help, pressure people to tell their story or share a person's story unnecessarily.

Argus should turn this into a short ethical operating set:

1. **Offer; do not impose.** A competent adult may decline ordinary PFA.
2. **Be truthful.** Do not invent information or promise an outcome.
3. **Respect agency.** Help the person make their own decisions when they can safely do so.
4. **Protect privacy.** Share personal information only when appropriate to the support role, safety, law or safeguarding obligations.
5. **Stay within role.** Do not claim clinical, legal or emergency competence you do not have.
6. **Adapt respectfully.** Do not assume preferred forms of eye contact, touch, family involvement, faith, culture or coping.

The course should avoid a blanket promise of confidentiality. Real helpers may have legal, organizational or safeguarding duties, and an urgent safety risk can require escalation. Argus should state this boundary without attempting to teach all Canadian provincial and territorial reporting law inside the PFA course.

# 8. Referral and escalation

PFA includes knowing when PFA alone is not enough.

WHO identifies several situations requiring more advanced support, including serious life-threatening injury, distress so severe that a person cannot care for themselves or dependent children, and risk that a person may hurt themselves or others. Canadian Red Cross material likewise instructs helpers to notice safety, violence, unmet basic needs and circumstances that require medical, protection or other services.

Argus should use a simple three-level model.

## Level 1 — ordinary supportive response

Use Look, Listen, Link, Live when:

- the environment is reasonably safe;
- the person can engage or decline support;
- basic needs can be addressed through ordinary help;
- there is no indication of an immediate medical or safety emergency.

## Level 2 — connect to trained or professional support

Escalate or link when, for example:

- distress is persistent or substantially interfering with functioning;
- the person asks for professional help;
- the situation involves needs beyond the helper's knowledge or authority;
- substance use, trauma symptoms, grief or other concerns are more than ordinary peer support can safely address;
- organizational, community, medical or mental-health services are clearly the better resource.

The helper can remain supportive while facilitating the connection; referral is not abandonment.

## Level 3 — urgent/emergency response

Do not continue ordinary PFA as though the situation were routine when there is:

- immediate danger or urgent medical need;
- serious injury;
- a direct threat of imminent harm to self or others;
- inability to maintain immediate safety or basic care;
- another emergency that clearly requires emergency responders.

For Canada, current Public Health Agency of Canada guidance is straightforward:

- **9-1-1** for immediate danger or urgent medical support;
- **call or text 9-8-8** when someone is thinking about suicide; 9-8-8 is available across Canada 24/7.

Argus should teach those two national pathways as reference knowledge, while keeping local/provincial services configurable and date-reviewed.

### Important boundary: suicide

Argus PFA should not teach a lay learner to perform a clinical suicide-risk assessment or decide whether a suicidal disclosure is “serious enough.” It should teach recognition, direct connection to appropriate trained help, and emergency escalation when danger is immediate. The Public Health Agency of Canada also states that asking someone directly whether they are thinking about suicide does not increase suicide risk; this fact may be referenced, but a full suicide-support protocol belongs in a dedicated, separately researched course.

# 9. Self-care and helper limits

Self-care is not an optional appendix in the Canadian Red Cross model. It is part of the programme architecture.

The Argus course should teach the learner to notice when helping is becoming unsafe or unsustainable. Relevant boundaries include:

- the helper is too activated, exhausted or impaired to be useful;
- the helper is becoming the person's only support;
- the helper is taking responsibility for decisions the person can make themselves;
- the situation is exceeding the helper's role, training or authority;
- repeated exposure to distress is affecting the helper's own functioning;
- a handover to another person or service is appropriate.

The course should normalize **ending or transferring support appropriately**. “Stay until everything is solved” is not a valid PFA principle.

Live should therefore appear in both learner knowledge and scenario scoring. A learner who gives a compassionate response but ignores their own safety or refuses to hand over should not receive a perfect score.

# 10. Evidence: what can and cannot be claimed

## 10.1 Strongest defensible claim

PFA is a widely endorsed, consensus-based early support approach whose core practices are consistent with major humanitarian guidance and broader evidence on helpful post-crisis principles.

Hobfoll and colleagues' influential evidence-informed consensus identified five elements for early and mid-term mass-trauma intervention:

- safety;
- calming;
- self/community efficacy;
- connectedness;
- hope.

Those elements map closely to PFA's practical emphasis on safety, calm support, restoring agency, social connection and coping.

## 10.2 Direct PFA outcome evidence remains limited

The evidence should not be oversold.

A 2014 systematic literature review found no solid direct evidence at that time sufficient to determine which PFA practices were effective. A later systematic review published in 2023 found only 12 eligible outcome studies involving 1,437 participants from more than 9,000 screened records; only one was a randomized controlled trial. Results generally pointed in a favourable direction, but inconsistent interventions, weak evaluation designs and high risk of bias prevented strong efficacy conclusions.

A 2024 integrative review found positive signals for anxiety reduction and adaptive functioning in the immediate/intermediate term, while evidence for PTSD and depressive-symptom reduction was less convincing. A 2026 systematic review covering intervention and training literature found recurring themes including rapid assessment/resource connection, social support, active and reflective listening, trauma-informed training and helper self-care, but the existence of more studies does not justify turning PFA into a clinically proven treatment claim.

## 10.3 Product implication

Argus must not say:

- “PFA prevents PTSD”;
- “PFA treats trauma”;
- “this technique is proven to stop a mental-health crisis”;
- “course completion makes you competent to manage trauma victims.”

Argus can say:

> **PFA is a major international early-support framework designed to provide humane, practical, non-intrusive assistance and connection after serious stress. Its component principles are evidence-informed, while direct evidence for the efficacy of PFA packages remains limited and methodologically mixed.**

That wording is both useful and accurate.

# 11. Finite knowledge layer

The course should keep memorization small. The goal is to reserve most effort for judgement practice.

## Required recall

### A. Four-part Canadian Red Cross framework

- Look
- Listen
- Link
- Live

### B. One-sentence meaning of each

- **Look:** notice safety, needs, distress and strengths.
- **Listen:** approach calmly, ask what is needed, listen without pressure.
- **Link:** connect to practical help, accurate information, trusted people and services.
- **Live:** support sustainable coping and maintain helper self-care/boundaries.

### C. Core non-goals

Learner can identify that PFA is not:

- diagnosis;
- psychotherapy/professional counselling;
- psychological debriefing;
- forced disclosure or detailed event reconstruction;
- a substitute for emergency or professional care.

### D. Escalation memory

Learner can recall:

- immediate danger / urgent medical support in Canada: **9-1-1**;
- suicidal thoughts or concern about suicide in Canada: **call or text 9-8-8**;
- ordinary PFA ends where risk, need or complexity exceeds the helper's role.

### E. Ethical memory

Learner can recall:

- offer, do not impose;
- be truthful;
- respect agency and privacy;
- stay within competence;
- adapt to the person's context rather than assuming.

This is enough finite content. Lists of psychiatric symptoms, diagnostic criteria and detailed treatment options should be excluded.

# 12. Scenario-practice design

The course becomes valuable when the learner has to decide **what to do next**, not simply name the framework.

## Scenario family 1 — What should you do first?

Present a short scene with multiple plausible actions.

Example:

> A coworker has just learned that a close family member was hospitalized. They are shaking, staring at their phone and saying, “I can't think.”

Ask:

- What should you do first?
- What observation matters most?
- Which response is supportive but non-intrusive?

Strong answer pattern: check immediate safety/needs, ask what would help now, offer practical connection.

Weak answer pattern: diagnose a panic attack, demand details, immediately give advice.

## Scenario family 2 — Support or intrusion?

Learner classifies responses.

Examples:

- “Do you want to tell me what would help right now?” — supportive.
- “Start from the beginning and tell me everything.” — intrusive.
- “You're safe here right now; we can sit here while you decide what you need.” — potentially supportive if the safety statement is factually true.
- “I promise nothing bad is going to happen.” — false reassurance.

## Scenario family 3 — Practical help beats emotional probing

Example:

> After a building evacuation, a resident is tearful and repeatedly says their medication is still inside.

Ask: what should the helper prioritize?

Expected reasoning: safety and access to the appropriate emergency/building/medical process, not discussion of the resident's feelings about the evacuation.

## Scenario family 4 — Refusal of help

Example:

> A person says, “I don't want to talk. Please leave me alone.” They appear physically safe and there is no stated immediate danger.

Strong response: respect the refusal, make support available if wanted later, and do not force disclosure.

The course must explicitly reward respecting a competent person's choice.

## Scenario family 5 — Link to existing strengths

Example:

> A distressed person says their sister is usually the person who helps them think clearly, but they do not have their phone.

Strong response: offer practical help contacting the sister if the person wants that.

This reinforces agency and connectedness rather than helper dependency.

## Scenario family 6 — Escalation required

Example:

> A friend says, “I don't think I can keep myself safe tonight.”

Strong response: treat this as beyond ordinary PFA; stay focused on immediate safety and connect to trained crisis/emergency support. In Canada, 9-8-8 is appropriate for suicide crisis; 9-1-1 is appropriate for immediate danger or urgent medical need.

The exercise should not ask the learner to score suicide risk.

## Scenario family 7 — Observation without diagnosis

Example:

> A person is pacing, speaking quickly and repeatedly checking the exit after witnessing a collision.

Ask:

- What can you observe?
- What can you infer?
- What should you avoid claiming?

Correct: describe behaviour and possible distress; do not diagnose panic disorder, PTSD or another condition.

## Scenario family 8 — Cultural and personal preference

Example:

> A helper reaches toward a distressed stranger for a reassuring touch. The person moves away.

Ask what should happen next.

Strong response: respect the cue and personal space; do not insist that physical comfort is universally helpful.

## Scenario family 9 — Helper boundary

Example:

> You have spent several hours supporting people after a workplace incident. You are becoming irritable, struggling to concentrate and feel responsible for staying until everyone is okay.

Strong response: recognize reduced helper capacity, use team/support structures, hand over where appropriate and attend to personal needs.

## Scenario family 10 — Accurate information vs reassurance

Example:

> A person asks whether they will be allowed back into an evacuated building tonight. You do not know.

Strong response: say you do not know and help locate an authoritative source.

Weak response: “I'm sure you'll be back inside soon.”

# 13. Scenario scoring rubric

Each applied exercise should score the learner on the same dimensions so the course teaches a stable judgement model.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Safety / urgency** | Misses or worsens an urgent risk | Notices concern but response is incomplete | Correctly prioritizes safety and escalation |
| **Observation vs diagnosis** | Makes unsupported diagnostic claim | Uses ambiguous interpretive language | Stays with observable need/behaviour and uncertainty |
| **Autonomy / non-intrusion** | Pressures, orders or takes over unnecessarily | Partly respects choice | Offers support, permission and meaningful choice |
| **Listening / communication** | Dismissive, leading, falsely reassuring or self-focused | Neutral but weakly responsive | Calm, concise, accurate and person-centred |
| **Practical linking** | Tries to personally solve everything or ignores concrete need | Identifies a support but poorly matches it | Connects the person to the most relevant practical/social/professional support |
| **Role / boundary awareness** | Acts beyond competence or ignores need to hand over | Boundary recognized late | Stays within role and transfers/escalates appropriately |
| **Helper sustainability** | Ignores own safety/capacity | Acknowledges it without action | Uses self-care, team support or handover appropriately |

### Hard-fail conditions

A scenario should be automatically marked unsafe where the chosen response:

- ignores a clear immediate threat to life or safety;
- encourages secrecy around an urgent risk;
- diagnoses a mental disorder from sparse behavioural cues;
- pressures a person to recount a traumatic event;
- recommends replacing emergency/professional care with the learner's support;
- makes a materially false promise about safety, confidentiality or outcome;
- instructs the learner to physically intervene in a dangerous situation outside the stated scenario competence.

A hard fail should trigger explanation and replay, not simply deduct points.

# 14. Misconceptions Argus should correct

## “Psychological first aid means getting someone to talk about what happened.”

Incorrect. PFA includes listening if the person wants to talk; it explicitly rejects pressured disclosure and psychological debriefing.

## “The more distressed someone looks, the more likely they have a mental disorder.”

Incorrect. Acute stress reactions vary, and ordinary PFA does not diagnose.

## “A helper should reassure the person that everything will be okay.”

Incorrect. Reassurance should be truthful and specific. False promises can undermine trust.

## “Good support means solving the person's problems for them.”

Incorrect. PFA supports practical problem-solving and connection while preserving the person's agency where possible.

## “If a person declines help, keep trying until they open up.”

Incorrect in an ordinary safe situation. Offer support and remain available; do not force it.

## “Referral means the helper failed.”

Incorrect. Linking to a more appropriate service is a core PFA action.

## “PFA is a proven treatment for PTSD.”

Incorrect. PFA is not psychotherapy, and the direct intervention evidence is not strong enough for that claim.

## “Self-care is separate from helping others.”

Incorrect within the Canadian Red Cross framework. Helper well-being and boundaries are part of safe support.

# 15. Safety boundaries for authored content

The initial Argus course should use **low- to moderate-complexity civilian situations** and only a small number of explicit escalation cases.

## In scope

- friend, coworker, neighbour or bystander support;
- bereavement, bad news, evacuation, accident aftermath and acute situational stress;
- practical needs and connection to supports;
- ordinary refusal of help;
- recognizing when professional or emergency support is more appropriate;
- national Canadian emergency/crisis references;
- helper self-care and handover.

## Out of scope for the initial course

- diagnosing mental disorders;
- treatment of PTSD, depression, anxiety disorders, psychosis or substance-use disorders;
- detailed suicide-risk assessment or safety-plan instruction;
- hostage/crisis negotiation;
- violent-person management or physical intervention;
- involuntary apprehension or mental-health law procedures;
- detailed child-protection interviewing or legal reporting instruction;
- sexual-assault counselling;
- domestic-violence safety planning beyond directing the learner toward appropriate specialized help;
- prescribing medication, clinical grounding protocols or therapeutic exercises;
- advising a learner to stay in a physically unsafe scene.

Those are distinct competencies and should only appear in separately researched modules, if at all.

# 16. Relationship to formal Canadian Red Cross training

The current Canadian Red Cross formal Psychological First Aid course is substantially larger than the proposed Argus topic. As of this research date, the Red Cross describes the course as:

- **12 hours in class**, or a blended route with **45–90 minutes of online Self-Care plus 7 hours in class**;
- recommended for learners age 16+;
- case-based;
- covering stress, distress, loss, grief, trauma, indicators, vulnerable populations, supportive communication, Look/Listen/Link/Live, resiliency/protective factors, protection, policies/legislation and self-care;
- leading to a **three-year Canadian Red Cross PFA certification** on successful completion.

The Red Cross also offers separate self-paced Self-Care and Caring for Others online courses built around the same four-part framework.

## Argus positioning

Argus should be described as:

> **A source-backed introduction, practice layer and memory companion for core Canadian PFA principles. It is not Canadian Red Cross training or certification.**

Recommended relationship:

- teach the small transferable framework in Argus;
- use authored scenarios to rehearse supportive decisions;
- link learners who want formal competence/certification to the current Canadian Red Cross course;
- never copy Red Cross certification language onto Argus completion;
- do not imply Canadian Red Cross endorsement, partnership or equivalence;
- periodically re-check current Canadian Red Cross course naming, format and national crisis references.

The formal course should remain the preferred next step for a learner who expects to use PFA in volunteering, emergency response, workplace response or another role where structured training matters.

# 17. Proposed Argus course structure

A compact course can remain finite while still requiring meaningful application.

## Lesson 1 — What PFA is, and is not

Finite recall:

- humane, supportive, practical assistance;
- not diagnosis, counselling, therapy or debriefing;
- do not force disclosure.

Practice:

- classify responses as PFA, intrusive, clinical or unsafe.

## Lesson 2 — Look

Finite recall:

- safety;
- basic needs;
- observable distress;
- risk/harm;
- strengths and supports.

Practice:

- identify what is observable, what is missing and whether help is needed.

## Lesson 3 — Listen

Finite recall:

- calm approach;
- permission;
- needs/concerns;
- listen without pressure;
- truthful, simple communication.

Practice:

- choose the next response;
- improve weak responses;
- distinguish acknowledgement from diagnosis or false reassurance.

## Lesson 4 — Link

Finite recall:

- practical needs;
- trusted people;
- accurate information;
- community/cultural supports;
- professional services.

Practice:

- choose the most useful support from several plausible options.

## Lesson 5 — Live, boundaries and self-care

Finite recall:

- sustainable coping;
- helper capacity;
- boundaries;
- handover.

Practice:

- identify when the helper should continue, pause, seek support or transfer responsibility.

## Lesson 6 — Escalation

Finite recall:

- ordinary support vs professional link vs urgent response;
- Canada: 9-8-8 and 9-1-1 boundary.

Practice:

- triage authored cases without asking for diagnosis or clinical risk scoring.

## Final scenario check

Use 8–12 mixed scenarios with changing context. Do not announce which “L” is being assessed. The learner must determine the relevant priority.

Recommended completion rule:

- 100% recall mastery over the small finite set;
- strong scenario performance across all rubric dimensions;
- no unresolved hard-fail safety errors in the final scenario set;
- repeated practice permitted after explanation.

This preserves Argus's mastery model while acknowledging that app scenarios demonstrate bounded judgement, not real-world certification.

# 18. Content-authoring rules

Every authored PFA scenario should pass these checks:

- Is the learner being asked to support rather than diagnose?
- Is there enough information to justify the expected answer?
- Could “insufficient information” reasonably be correct?
- Is a practical need being ignored in favour of emotional dialogue?
- Does the expected response respect consent and agency?
- Does the helper make only factual promises?
- Is cultural or personal preference treated as variable rather than stereotyped?
- Is any emergency or suicide content routed toward trained support rather than amateur assessment?
- Is the helper's own safety and scope protected?
- Does the explanation say **why** an alternative is weaker, not merely label it wrong?

Scenario explanations should explicitly separate:

1. what is observed;
2. what need is reasonably inferable;
3. what remains unknown;
4. the safest useful next action;
5. the point at which the helper should hand over or escalate.

That structure also reinforces the neighbouring Argus courses on Observation vs Inference, Bias Control, De-escalation and Crisis Active Listening.

# 19. Relationship to the wider Argus practical-skills programme

PFA should sit **after** foundational observation and communication skills, not before them.

Recommended sequence:

1. Situational Awareness I — notice changing conditions without profiling.
2. Observation vs Inference — separate evidence from interpretation.
3. De-escalation I — communicate without adding unnecessary conflict.
4. Crisis Active Listening — practise listening tools at greater depth.
5. Threat Recognition I — identify concerning patterns and escalation boundaries.
6. Bias Control — audit judgement under uncertainty.
7. **Psychological First Aid — apply observation, listening, practical support and linking to a distressed person.**
8. Interviewing I — cooperative information gathering with different goals and stricter information-quality requirements.

This order matters because PFA should not become “generic empathy training.” It draws on observation, communication, uncertainty management and escalation already taught elsewhere, then gives those skills a specific civilian-support purpose.

PFA also provides a useful contrast with Interviewing I: the PFA helper does **not** seek a complete account. The person's immediate safety, needs and agency matter more than obtaining detail.

    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41736596/

# 21. Final course decision

**Recommended Argus treatment: ship as a bounded scenario-based companion course.**

The durable core is small: Look, Listen, Link, Live; practical assistance; non-intrusive listening; agency; referral/escalation; helper self-care; and explicit limits. The course earns its place in Argus through applied judgement, not through a large flashcard deck.

The most important design rule is that the learner must repeatedly be rewarded for **doing less when less is appropriate**: not diagnosing, not probing, not forcing help, not promising outcomes, not becoming the only support, and not staying in a situation that requires trained or emergency response.

## Exact completion claim

> **Can recall the Canadian Red Cross Look, Listen, Link, Live framework; recognize observable signs that someone may be stressed or distressed without diagnosing them; choose calm, non-intrusive and practical supportive responses; connect a person to appropriate personal, community or professional supports; and recognize when ordinary civilian PFA is insufficient and escalation is warranted in authored scenarios. Completion is not Canadian Red Cross certification and does not establish counselling, clinical, crisis-assessment or real-world emergency-response competence.**
