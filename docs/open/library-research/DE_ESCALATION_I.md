## Research conclusion

**Ship as a course specification candidate, with a deliberately narrow completion claim.**

There is enough Ontario-specific and Canadian evidence to justify an Argus course on civilian de-escalation communication fundamentals. Ontario's mandatory security-guard curriculum explicitly requires active listening, communication-style adaptation, verbal and non-verbal feedback, empathy, rapport, assertiveness without unnecessary confrontation, posture, tone, spatial distance, eye contact, facial expression and de-escalation techniques; it recommends role-play with feedback as the teaching and evaluation method. Ontario's police mental-health-crisis programme now uses scenario-based, evidence-informed de-escalation training, and its associated DePICT™ research provides a validated observer framework covering calming paralanguage, respectful language, body language, active listening, dignity, validation, collaboration and transparent decision-making.

Canadian public-health guidance independently supports the same relational principles: non-judgmental communication, listening and checking understanding, emotional validation, clear information and expectations, choice, collaboration and safety. Toronto guidance for helping someone in crisis adds an important limit: stay calm, use a low calm voice, do not argue, and stop trying to de-escalate if it is not working; if unsafe, leave and seek emergency help.

International clinical guidance and consensus sources strongly converge on the same communication pattern: self-regulation, respectful personal space, non-provocative body language, concise speech, active listening, emotional acknowledgement, clear limits, choices and safety-based disengagement. However, the empirical evidence that de-escalation training itself reduces violence is much weaker than the consensus around what competent de-escalation communication looks like. Systematic reviews have repeatedly found limited, heterogeneous and methodologically weak outcome evidence.

Argus should therefore teach and assess **recognition and application of communication principles in authored scenarios**. It should not claim that completing the course makes a learner capable of safely managing violent, psychiatric or otherwise dangerous incidents in the field.

# 1. Exact competency and completion claim

## 1.1 Completion claim

> **Can recognize and apply core civilian de-escalation communication fundamentals in authored low- and moderate-intensity scenarios: regulate their own delivery, listen and acknowledge emotion, preserve dignity, communicate clear requests and boundaries, offer workable choices, and identify when to disengage or seek help rather than continue the interaction.**

This claim is deliberately narrower than “can de-escalate people.” De-escalation is interactive and contingent. A learner can use good communication and the other person can still become more distressed, angry or violent.

## 1.2 What completion does not mean

Completion does **not** mean that the learner:

- can safely approach or manage a violent person;
- is a crisis negotiator, mental-health professional, security professional or police officer;
- can diagnose intoxication, psychosis, mania, dementia or any other condition;
- can reliably predict violence from body language or behaviour;
- can compel cooperation through communication;
- should remain in an unsafe interaction to prove that de-escalation works;
- has received restraint, self-defence, use-of-force, weapons or tactical training;
- has demonstrated real-world performance under stress.

## 1.3 Working definition for Argus

For this course:

> **De-escalation is the intentional use of verbal and non-verbal communication to reduce unnecessary interpersonal arousal, preserve dignity, understand the immediate problem, and make the next interaction safer and more workable.**

It is not “winning,” overpowering, persuading at all costs, making the other person admit they are wrong, or obtaining compliance.

# 2. Evidence base and what it supports

## 2.1 Ontario: effective communication is already treated as an applied skill

Ontario's current **Security Guard Training Syllabus, Section 9: Effective Communications** is unusually well aligned with the proposed Argus course. It requires training on active listening; adapting communication style to the audience or situation; verbal and non-verbal feedback; sensitivity and empathy; rapport; managing difficult relationships and potential conflict; assertiveness while remaining professional; maintaining composure; and both verbal and non-verbal communication, including posture, tone, spatial distance, eye contact and facial expressions. The syllabus explicitly includes de-escalation and recommends role-play with feedback, including role-play as part of evaluation.[1]

The corresponding Ontario private-investigator curriculum repeats the same pattern: active listening, clear and concise communication, empathy and rapport, assertiveness without confrontation, posture, tone, spatial distance and scenario-based de-escalation practice.[2]

This is important product evidence. Ontario does not treat these abilities as a list of definitions to memorize. It treats them as communication skills that require application and practice.

## 2.2 Ontario police mental-health-crisis training: scenario practice and observable competencies

Ontario Regulation 87/24 now requires police officers to complete the Mental Health Crisis Response (MHCR) Education and Applied Training programme under the current statutory timetable.[3] Wilfrid Laurier University describes MHCR as a scenario-based, community-co-designed, evidence-informed programme in de-escalation, crisis communication and relational policing, centred on dignity, procedural justice and trauma-informed practice.[4]

The associated **De-escalating Persons in Crisis Competencies Tool (DePICT™)** was published and validated in 2025. It is a 14-item observer-rated competency framework developed through stakeholder focus groups, literature review and simulation testing. Relevant competencies include:

- managing time and distance;
- genuine concern and willingness to help;
- dignity and human connection;
- calming paralanguage: tone, pitch, pace and volume;
- inclusive, non-judgmental and respectful language;
- calming body language and self-regulation;
- self-awareness and flexibility when an approach is not working;
- active listening and allowing emotional expression;
- validation of emotions and experience;
- information gathering and use of resources;
- collaboration and choice;
- clear and transparent decision-making.[5]

Argus should **not** reproduce the police tool wholesale. Several DePICT competencies concern police risk management and professional responsibilities outside a civilian course. The useful lesson is the assessment shape: de-escalation can be decomposed into observable communication behaviours and assessed in standardized scenarios rather than inferred from self-confidence.

## 2.3 Canadian public health: dignity, validation, clarity and choice

The Public Health Agency of Canada's trauma- and violence-informed practice guidance recommends non-judgmental communication, listening and checking understanding, validating experiences, providing clear information and consistent expectations, creating opportunities for choice and collaboration, and allowing people space to express feelings.[6]

Those principles are directly relevant to civilian de-escalation because they reduce avoidable interpersonal threat without requiring the learner to diagnose the other person or know why they are distressed.

## 2.4 Toronto/Ontario crisis guidance: there is a point at which engagement should stop

The City of Toronto's mental-health guidance advises a person trying to help someone in crisis to remain calm, speak in a low calm voice, be non-judgmental, show empathy, avoid arguing or trying to convince, allow silence, and shift toward what can help next. It then gives the necessary safety boundary: **if de-escalation is not working, stop; if you feel unsafe, leave and call 911.**[7]

Current Toronto service guidance distinguishes emergency from non-emergency help. The Toronto Community Crisis Service (TCCS) is a civilian, consent-based, city-wide mental-health crisis service accessed through 211; emergencies still go to 911.[8] Ontario similarly directs people to 911 or an emergency department where someone is in immediate danger.[9] Canada-wide, 9-8-8 is available for suicide crisis support.

This supports making **disengagement and escalation to help a scored course competency**, not a footnote.

## 2.5 Strong professional consensus, but limited causal evidence

NICE guidance on violence and aggression recommends training that covers early signs of agitation, personal space, non-provocative responses, respect and empathy, one primary communicator, safety assessment, non-confrontational clarification and self-management of posture, eye contact and frustration.[10]

The American Association for Emergency Psychiatry's Project BETA consensus similarly emphasizes personal space, non-provocative body language, one verbal lead, concise speech, active listening, identifying wants and feelings, respectful limit-setting, choices and collaborative problem solving.[11]

These sources provide strong convergence on **what skilled communication should look like**, but should not be mistaken for strong experimental proof that a short course prevents violence. A 2023 systematic review of de-escalation training in forensic psychiatric settings found only a small number of methodologically weak studies and concluded that only tentative claims could be made about reductions in violent incidents. The review nevertheless identified consistent skill domains: calm tone, attentive posture, active listening, personal control, respectful engagement, shared problem-solving and limit-setting.[12] Earlier Cochrane reviews likewise found the direct effectiveness evidence sparse.[13]

### Evidence judgment for Argus

| Question | Judgment | Product implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Are the core communication principles well supported across authoritative guidance? | **Yes — strong convergence.** | Teach them. |
| Is scenario practice an appropriate learning method? | **Yes.** Ontario syllabi require role-play; MHCR/DePICT uses standardized simulations. | Make scenarios the primary learning shape. |
| Can communication competencies be observed and scored? | **Yes, within a defined scenario rubric.** | Score observable choices, not confidence. |
| Does a short course prove field competence or guarantee safer outcomes? | **No.** Outcome evidence is limited and real incidents are highly contextual. | Keep the completion claim narrow. |
| Should Argus teach physical restraint or force as part of this course? | **No.** | Explicitly exclude it. |

# 3. Core course model

The simplest useful model is:

> **Regulate yourself → reduce unnecessary pressure → listen → acknowledge → clarify → request or offer options → set boundaries if needed → reassess → disengage or get help when appropriate.**

This is a teaching sequence, not a rigid script. Real interactions may move backward and forward between steps.

The course should repeatedly reinforce three rules:

1. **Your delivery is part of the message.** Tone, pace, volume, posture and distance can reinforce or contradict the words.
2. **Understanding is not agreement.** Acknowledging anger, fear or frustration does not require endorsing an accusation, delusion or inaccurate claim.
3. **Safety outranks conversational persistence.** Good de-escalation includes recognizing when not to continue.

# 4. Curriculum sequence

Recommended size: **seven short units plus a mixed capstone**, with roughly 30–40 authored practice scenarios across the course. The material is small enough to finish, but too applied to compress into one reference page.

## Unit 1 — What de-escalation is for

### Teach

- Reduce unnecessary interpersonal arousal; do not “win.”
- De-escalation is a cooperative possibility, not something one person can force.
- Start with self-regulation: notice your own urgency, irritation, volume and pace.
- A calm delivery should be ordinary and respectful, not artificially soothing or patronizing.
- Slow the interaction when there is no immediate reason to rush.

### Practice

- Identify which responder is escalating through tone or pacing despite acceptable words.
- Rewrite a “calm down” response into something concrete and respectful.
- Choose whether to speak immediately or allow a short pause.

### Key misconception

> “If I stay calm, the other person will calm down.”

No. Your composure removes one possible source of escalation; it does not control the other person's response.

## Unit 2 — Listen before solving

### Teach

- Give the person a chance to explain the immediate concern.
- Use brief encouragers and open questions when appropriate.
- Paraphrase to check understanding rather than to display technique.
- Listen for both content and emotion.
- Do not rush into advice, correction or problem-solving before the concern is understood.
- Silence can be useful; continuous talking can add pressure.

### Practice

- “What should you say next?” after an angry complaint.
- Identify the difference between a genuine clarifying question and a disguised accusation.
- Select the best paraphrase from options that vary in judgment and accuracy.

### Key misconception

> “Active listening means repeating the person's words back to them.”

Mechanical repetition can sound artificial. The objective is accurate understanding and visible attention.

## Unit 3 — Acknowledge emotion without endorsing the claim

### Teach

- Name or acknowledge an apparent emotion tentatively: “That sounds frustrating,” not “I know exactly how you feel.”
- Validate that the person's emotional experience is real even when you cannot confirm their interpretation of events.
- Separate **emotion** from **fact claim**.
- Avoid humiliating, dismissive or contemptuous responses.
- Preserve face where possible; give people a path to change direction without forcing an admission of defeat.

### Practice

- Distinguish “validation” from “agreement.”
- Rewrite: “You're being ridiculous; nobody is following you.”
- Compare: “I can see this is frightening for you” versus confirming an unverified belief.

### Key misconception

> “If I acknowledge their feelings, I am agreeing that they are right.”

No. Emotional acknowledgement communicates understanding of experience, not factual endorsement.

## Unit 4 — Tone, pacing, posture and distance

### Teach

- Use a normal low-to-moderate volume and an unhurried pace when conditions allow.
- Keep posture and facial expression consistent with respectful words.
- Avoid crowding, looming, staring or unnecessary physical pressure.
- Personal space is contextual; do not teach a universal exact distance as a civilian rule.
- Allow the other person room to disengage.
- Eye contact norms vary by person, culture and circumstance; “more eye contact” is not automatically better.

### Practice

- Identify non-verbal contradictions: respectful sentence + aggressive posture or volume.
- Decide which change would reduce pressure: lower volume, add distance, stop interrupting, or all of the above.
- Detect when the learner should simply give the other person space instead of continuing to talk.

### Boundary

This unit is about **not adding pressure**, not tactical positioning. Do not teach fighting stance, cover, containment, weapon access, restraint, reactionary gaps or approach tactics.

## Unit 5 — Clear requests, choices and transparent limits

### Teach

- Keep requests brief, specific and behavioural.
- One request at a time is easier to process than a lecture.
- Explain relevant constraints plainly.
- Offer real choices when real choices exist.
- Do not offer false choices whose outcome is already predetermined.
- A boundary states what you can or cannot do and what you will do next; it is not a threat.
- Consequences, where relevant, should be factual and proportionate rather than humiliating or punitive.

### Useful pattern

> **Acknowledge → boundary → available option.**

Example:

> “I can hear that you're frustrated. I can't change the fee here. I can show you where to appeal it, or we can stop for now.”

### Practice

- Rewrite vague commands into clear requests.
- Distinguish a boundary from an ultimatum.
- Choose between a genuine option and a manipulative “choice.”

## Unit 6 — Do not add unnecessary conflict

### Teach

Common escalation errors:

- arguing about who is right when the immediate goal is safety or workable next steps;
- correcting minor inaccuracies that do not matter to the immediate issue;
- sarcasm, mockery or humiliation;
- interrupting repeatedly;
- matching volume or anger;
- rapid-fire questioning;
- issuing multiple commands;
- insisting on eye contact;
- touching someone to “reassure” them without consent;
- crowding;
- using psychiatric labels or amateur diagnoses;
- making promises you cannot keep;
- demanding that the person “calm down.”

### Practice

- Mark every escalation error in a short transcript.
- Rank several responses by how much unnecessary interpersonal pressure they introduce.
- Rewrite a poor response while preserving the legitimate boundary.

## Unit 7 — Know when to stop

### Teach

Continued engagement is inappropriate when the learner reasonably believes the interaction is unsafe, their presence is worsening the situation, the person clearly wants them to leave and there is no overriding emergency reason to remain, or the problem requires professional/emergency help rather than more persuasion.

The course should teach a simple decision distinction:

- **Ordinary conflict / low-intensity frustration:** continue only if engagement remains workable and voluntary.
- **Non-emergency mental-health crisis:** seek appropriate local crisis support. In Toronto, TCCS is accessed through 211; elsewhere, 211 can connect people to local services.
- **Suicide crisis:** call or text 9-8-8 in Canada for suicide crisis support.
- **Immediate danger, ongoing violence, serious medical emergency or urgent threat to life:** call 9-1-1.

Where safe, disengagement can be plain and non-provocative:

> “I'm going to step away now.”

or:

> “I don't think continuing this conversation is helping. I'm going to get someone who can help.”

### Practice

Every course run should include several scenarios where **the correct answer is to stop talking**.

That prevents Argus from teaching conversational overconfidence.

# 5. Scenario system

The course should be built around dialogue decisions rather than long lectures. Four scenario forms cover the requested skills well.

## 5.1 What should you say next?

Present 2–4 turns of dialogue plus limited context, then ask for the best next response.

Distractors should represent realistic mistakes, not absurd answers:

- premature advice;
- factual correction before acknowledgement;
- fake empathy;
- vague demand;
- unnecessary threat;
- overlong explanation;
- continued engagement when disengagement is better.

## 5.2 Identify escalation / de-escalation mistakes

Present a short transcript and ask the learner to identify all relevant issues.

The learner should distinguish between:

- a merely imperfect response;
- an escalation risk;
- a safety-critical error.

## 5.3 Rewrite the poor response

Practice can allow free-text rewriting followed by a structured self-comparison against an authored rubric. Scored assessment should initially avoid subjective free-text grading and instead use either:

- select-the-best-rewrite;
- choose which principles the rewrite must contain;
- arrange response components into a better sequence.

If Argus later supports reliable authored semantic grading, true free-text scoring can be added. It should not be required for the first course.

## 5.4 Continue, disengage, or seek help?

These should be first-class scenarios, not rare edge cases.

Ask the learner to choose among:

- continue listening / clarifying;
- state a boundary and reassess;
- disengage;
- seek non-emergency support;
- seek emergency help.

The scenario must provide enough explicit facts to make the answer defensible. Do not reward imagined threats or stereotype-based assumptions.

# 6. Scenario rubric

Argus should score **observable communication decisions**, not inferred personality or confidence.

A compact six-dimension rubric is enough for this first course.

| Dimension | 0 — counterproductive | 1 — acceptable / incomplete | 2 — strong application |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Engagement appropriateness** | Continues or intensifies interaction when explicit facts make disengagement/help the appropriate response | Recognizes concern but response is delayed or unclear | Correctly continues, disengages or escalates to help based on stated facts |
| **Self-regulation and delivery** | Adds pressure through hostility, rushing, raised volume or visibly confrontational framing | Neutral delivery | Calm, ordinary, unhurried and proportionate delivery |
| **Dignity and respect** | Shames, mocks, labels, diagnoses or humiliates | Avoids overt disrespect | Uses non-judgmental, face-saving, person-respecting language |
| **Listening and acknowledgement** | Ignores concern, argues immediately, or falsely claims certainty about feelings | Acknowledges content or emotion incompletely | Accurately reflects the concern and acknowledges emotion without endorsing unsupported claims |
| **Clarity and cognitive load** | Long lecture, multiple commands, vague or contradictory demand | Understandable but could be simpler | Brief, specific, one-step-at-a-time communication with time to respond |
| **Agency, requests and boundaries** | Threatens, coerces unnecessarily, offers fake choices, or has no workable next step | Boundary/request is legitimate but awkward | Gives a clear request or boundary and, where available, genuine choices or next steps |

## 6.1 Safety hard gate

A scenario is automatically failed if the learner chooses continued persuasion where the authored facts clearly indicate immediate danger or another explicit stop condition.

This is more important than maximizing the total rubric score.

## 6.2 Do not score style theatre

Do not reward responses merely because they contain stock empathy phrases such as “I hear you” or “I understand.” The response must fit the scenario.

A short direct sentence may be better than a polished therapeutic-sounding paragraph.

## 6.3 Do not score mind-reading

Learners should not receive extra credit for confidently naming an emotion that the scenario does not support. Tentative language is preferable when uncertain.

# 7. Recommended scenario bank

The first course should use ordinary public and interpersonal settings rather than specialist police or psychiatric scenes.

## 7.1 Low-intensity frustration

1. **Apartment lobby delivery dispute** — a resident is angry that a package cannot be found and begins speaking sharply to the concierge. Target: acknowledge + clear next step.
2. **Retail return disagreement** — a customer believes a return policy is unfair. Target: emotion versus rule; transparent limit + available options.
3. **Transit delay** — a passenger angrily complains to another passenger as if they are responsible. Target: do not take bait; concise boundary/disengagement.
4. **Café queue conflict** — someone believes another person cut in line. Target: avoid public humiliation and competitive escalation.
5. **Workplace handoff** — a colleague is visibly frustrated about a missed deadline. Target: listen before defending yourself.

## 7.2 Moderate interpersonal escalation

6. **Building noise complaint** — a neighbour arrives angry and accuses the learner of deliberate disrespect. Target: acknowledge impact without agreeing to motive; specific next step.
7. **Event entry refusal** — a person becomes increasingly angry because a ticket cannot be validated. Target: concise explanation, real options, no argument spiral.
8. **Service desk accusation** — “You're all trying to rip me off.” Target: do not debate global accusation; clarify the specific solvable issue.
9. **Friend overwhelmed in public** — the person is agitated and says everyone is staring at them. Target: reduce pressure, acknowledge distress, ask what would help.
10. **Person repeatedly interrupts** — target: maintain composure and set a respectful conversational boundary.

## 7.3 Disengagement and help decisions

11. **Clear request to be left alone** — no immediate danger is described. Correct response: stop pressing the conversation and give space.
12. **Your presence is escalating the person** — they become more agitated each time you approach. Correct response: step back / disengage rather than “try harder.”
13. **Explicit threat of immediate violence** — correct response: prioritize distance/disengagement and emergency help; no conversational heroics.
14. **Serious medical distress** — correct response: emergency help, not de-escalation dialogue.
15. **Person expresses suicidal thoughts but no immediate act is occurring** — correct response includes connection to 9-8-8 / appropriate crisis support; scenario wording must distinguish this from immediate danger requiring 9-1-1.
16. **Non-emergency mental-health crisis in Toronto** — target: 211/TCCS rather than defaulting every mental-health crisis to police response.

## 7.4 Deliberate ambiguity

At least 20% of scenarios should make **insufficient information** or **ask one clarifying question first** the best answer.

De-escalation training becomes dangerous if it rewards fast certainty from sparse behavioural cues.

# 8. Common errors and misconceptions

## 8.1 “De-escalation means calming the other person down”

Too strong. You can influence the interaction, not control another person's emotional state.

## 8.2 “The calmer-sounding response is always the best response”

No. Overly soft or therapeutic language can be patronizing. Sometimes the best response is a direct boundary or disengagement.

## 8.3 “Empathy means agreement”

No. “That sounds frightening” can acknowledge an experience without confirming the person's explanation for it.

## 8.4 “Good de-escalators never say no”

False. Clear, consistent limits are part of de-escalation. The issue is how the limit is communicated and whether a workable alternative exists.

## 8.5 “A firm boundary should sound tough”

No. Boundary strength comes from clarity and follow-through, not dominance or humiliation.

## 8.6 “Eye contact shows respect”

Sometimes. Sustained or forced eye contact can also feel confrontational. Cultural and individual differences matter.

## 8.7 “There is one correct personal-space distance”

No. Professional sources stress personal space, but exact distance depends on context. Argus should teach **do not crowd** and **allow room to disengage**, not a universal measurement.

## 8.8 “Keep them talking at all costs”

No. Silence may help. The person may want space. Continued engagement may be unsafe or counterproductive.

## 8.9 “Facts will settle an irrational argument”

Not necessarily. Immediate correction can become another contest. Clarify the practical issue first unless the factual correction is safety-critical.

## 8.10 “Never ask why”

Too rigid. A rapid or accusatory “Why did you do that?” can provoke defensiveness, but “why” is not inherently prohibited. Teach the underlying principle: avoid blame-loaded questioning and prefer questions that clarify needs, sequence or next steps.

## 8.11 “Body language lets you tell who is dangerous”

No. This course teaches learners to manage **their own** non-verbal communication and to respond to explicit behaviour/context, not to infer hidden intent from posture, gaze, fidgeting or demographic appearance.

## 8.12 “Mental-health crisis means call police”

No. Response should fit the situation. Non-emergency community crisis services exist; in Toronto, TCCS is available through 211. Immediate danger or urgent medical emergency is a 9-1-1 matter.

## 8.13 “If de-escalation fails, you used the wrong technique”

False. Some people cannot or do not want to engage, some incidents deteriorate despite good communication, and some situations require trained professional or emergency response.

# 9. Safety and ethical boundaries

## 9.1 No restraint or use-of-force content

Exclude:

- holds, takedowns, restraints or breakaway techniques;
- weapon handling or weapon-retention advice;
- self-defence manoeuvres;
- tactical approach, containment or room-control procedures;
- arrest, detention or citizen-arrest tactics;
- force options or escalation-of-force models.

If a source mixes communication with force training, extract only the communication evidence needed for the course.

## 9.2 No “approach the threat” scenarios

Argus should never create a scenario where a civilian learner earns points for approaching an armed, violent or highly unstable person to practise de-escalation.

High-risk scenarios may appear only to test recognition that the learner should disengage and get appropriate help.

## 9.3 No amateur diagnosis

Scenario facts should describe behaviour and statements rather than diagnostic labels wherever possible.

Do not teach the learner to decide whether someone is psychotic, manic, intoxicated, autistic, cognitively impaired or otherwise diagnosable. Those conditions can change communication needs, but diagnosis is outside this course and outside Argus's completion claim.

## 9.4 No demographic profiling

Race, sex, age, housing status, clothing, disability, accent, religion or other identity characteristics must never function as hidden threat cues.

If demographic details appear in a scenario, they should either be irrelevant or directly relevant to respectful communication/accessibility, not danger scoring.

## 9.5 Do not validate delusions or unsupported factual claims

Teach:

> acknowledge the person's fear, anger or distress; do not pretend to know that the underlying claim is true.

This distinction should appear repeatedly in scenarios.

## 9.6 Do not make the learner responsible for another person's safety beyond their role

The course should avoid messaging that implies a civilian must personally resolve a crisis. Calling for help, stepping away, involving a trained service, or respecting a request for space can all be correct outcomes.

## 9.7 Domestic or coercive abuse is not a communication exercise

Do not frame ongoing intimate-partner violence, stalking, coercive control or abuse as something the learner should solve through better de-escalation dialogue. Those situations require safety planning, specialized support and, where necessary, emergency response.

# 10. Assessment design

## 10.1 What should be recalled

Keep the finite recall layer small:

- de-escalation goal and limits;
- self-regulation before persuasion;
- tone / pace / volume / posture / distance matter;
- listen and clarify before problem-solving;
- acknowledgement is not factual agreement;
- concise, specific requests;
- clear boundaries and real choices;
- avoid humiliation, argument spirals and unnecessary pressure;
- reassess whether the interaction is improving;
- disengage or seek help when continued engagement is unsafe or inappropriate;
- current help distinctions: 211/local services, 9-8-8, 9-1-1.

This is roughly **10–12 durable propositions**, not a large deck.

## 10.2 What should be scenario-practised

Most course time should go to:

- selecting the next response;
- identifying why a response may escalate;
- rewriting poor phrasing;
- separating emotion acknowledgement from agreement;
- converting vague demands into specific requests;
- converting threats into factual boundaries;
- choosing whether to continue, pause, disengage or get help;
- adapting when the first approach is not working.

## 10.3 Completion assessment

Recommended final assessment:

- **20 scored scenario decisions** drawn from a larger bank;
- at least 4 “what to say next” items;
- at least 4 escalation-error identification items;
- at least 4 rewrite / best-rewrite items;
- at least 4 continue-vs-disengage-vs-help items;
- remaining items mixed;
- contexts distributed across public space, transit, home/building, service and peer interactions.

Recommended pass rule:

- **80% overall**, and
- **100% on safety-hard-gate items**, and
- no more than one error in the “engagement appropriateness” dimension.

A failed safety-hard-gate item should be retaught before a new attempt.

## 10.4 What Argus may record

On completion, Argus may record that the learner has demonstrated the course completion claim **in authored scenarios**.

Do not display labels such as:

- “de-escalation trained”;
- “crisis responder”;
- “conflict expert”;
- “field ready.”

# 11. Authoring rules for dialogue

To keep scenarios realistic and avoid turning the course into “therapy phrase trivia”:

1. Keep dialogue short. Most scenarios should be 3–7 turns before the decision point.
2. Do not make the correct answer conspicuously nicer than the distractors.
3. Include legitimate firm boundaries as correct answers.
4. Sometimes make silence, space or disengagement correct.
5. Avoid villain dialogue. Most conflict should arise from frustration, misunderstanding, perceived unfairness, distress or competing goals.
6. Do not assume that raised voice equals violence risk.
7. Never use identity as a proxy for danger.
8. Vary the learner's role: peer, neighbour, customer, coworker, bystander, friend.
9. Do not make the learner a pseudo-police officer or pseudo-clinician.
10. Explain the principle after the answer, not just “correct/incorrect.”
11. When more than one response could reasonably work, say so; score against the defined objective rather than pretending dialogue has one magical sentence.
12. Use Canadian/Ontario language and services in safety prompts, but keep the core communication principles jurisdiction-neutral.

# 12. Proposed Argus treatment

## 12.1 Course identity

**Title:** De-escalation I — Communication Fundamentals  
**Type:** finite knowledge + authored scenario application  
**Primary skill:** interpersonal communication under rising tension  
**Jurisdiction:** communication principles general; safety/help references Ontario/Canada, with Toronto examples where useful  
**Prerequisites:** none  
**Follow-ons:** Crisis Active Listening; Psychological First Aid; Conflict Boundaries; Threat Recognition I

## 12.2 Recommended structure

- 7 short Learn units;
- 30–40 practice scenarios;
- 20-scenario final assessment;
- no open-ended AI role-play required for V1;
- no force, restraint or tactical content;
- one compact reference card after completion: **Listen → Acknowledge → Clarify → Request / Boundary → Reassess → Stop/Get Help when needed.**

## 12.3 Why it belongs in Argus

This course fits the #104 editorial direction better than another recall list because it teaches a small body of durable knowledge and then requires repeated judgement in realistic situations. It is useful in ordinary life, public space, consulting, service interactions and peer support without pretending to confer a professional credential.

It also provides a strong test case for Argus's proposed generic scenario-practice learning shape: the answers are not arbitrary, but they cannot be reduced to memorizing a vocabulary.

# Final recommendation

**Ship the research direction.**

Build De-escalation I as a **civilian communication-judgement course**, not a condensed police, security or psychiatric intervention course. Keep the teachable core small; put most of the value into varied authored dialogue scenarios; make dignity, active listening, clear boundaries and disengagement equally important; and make “stop / get help” a scored competency.

The strongest defensible Argus claim is not that a completer “can de-escalate people.” It is that they can **recognize and apply core de-escalation communication principles in authored scenarios and know when continued engagement is inappropriate.**
