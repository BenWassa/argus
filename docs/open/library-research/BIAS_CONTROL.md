# Bias Control in Behavioural Judgement

**Status:** deep research / course-design input  
**Date:** 2026-09-17  
**Issue:** #104  
**Branch:** `research/issue-104-practical-skills-expansion`  
**Implementation:** out of scope  
**Shipped:** no (checked 2026-09-25) — the completion claim needs scenario practice; see `../ISSUE_104_SCENARIO_ITEMS.md`  

## Research question

What should Argus teach a civilian learner about cognitive bias when making real-world observations and behavioural judgements, especially where a mistaken inference could distort incident reporting, street awareness, interviewing or threat recognition?

The useful target is not “becoming unbiased.” Human judgement remains vulnerable to framing, prior beliefs, salient memories, first impressions, social stereotypes, context neglect and overconfidence. The teachable skill is narrower:

> **slow down selected judgements enough to expose the evidence, competing explanations, missing information and confidence level before acting on a conclusion.**

This course should therefore be designed as **bias control**, not bias elimination.

---

# 1. Recommendation

**Ship as a scenario-heavy course in the first practical-skills programme.**

Bias control is not a standalone “diversity awareness” topic and should not be reduced to definitions of cognitive biases. Its value is operational: it improves the judgement discipline required by the adjacent Argus courses on situational awareness, observation versus inference, interviewing and behavioural threat recognition.

The course should teach eight high-value failure modes:

- confirmation bias;
- anchoring;
- availability;
- stereotyping;
- fundamental attribution error / correspondence bias;
- premature closure;
- confusion between confidence and accuracy;
- body-language folklore, especially unsupported deception inference.

The durable learner habit should be a short reasoning sequence:

1. **State the observations.** What was actually seen, heard or recorded?
2. **State the interpretation as a hypothesis.** What do I currently think those facts may mean?
3. **Generate a plausible alternative.** What else could explain the same observations?
4. **Seek discriminating information.** What evidence could weaken, not merely strengthen, my current view?
5. **Identify what is missing.** What context, baseline, chronology, source quality or corroboration would materially change the judgement?
6. **Calibrate confidence.** How certain should I be given the evidence actually available?
7. **Update.** When new evidence arrives, revise the judgement rather than defending the first answer.

This is intentionally procedural. The learner should repeatedly perform the sequence in authored scenarios instead of merely memorizing bias names.

---

# 2. Exact competency boundary

## Completion claim

> **Can identify common bias patterns in authored behavioural-judgement scenarios, separate observations from interpretations, generate a plausible competing explanation, identify decision-relevant missing information, seek disconfirming evidence, and revise a judgement or confidence level when new evidence warrants it.**

## Completion does not mean

Completion must not imply that the learner:

- is unbiased or immune to cognitive error;
- can diagnose personality, mental illness, dangerousness or deception from behaviour;
- can reliably infer intent from body language;
- is qualified to conduct professional behavioural threat assessment;
- can replace corroboration, formal investigation or multidisciplinary review with intuition;
- should ignore urgent safety information while performing an extended reasoning exercise.

In an immediate emergency, safety and appropriate reporting take priority. Bias control improves interpretation; it is not a requirement to delay protective action until uncertainty disappears.

---

# 3. Why this belongs in Argus

Ontario professional-training material already treats bias control as part of competent observation and decision-making.

The Ontario security-guard syllabus requires learners to recognize their own biases and understand how those biases can influence situations. It recommends scenario-based self-assessment, instruction on how bias affects behaviour, and reflection on keeping bias “in check.” The same syllabus trains guards to establish environmental baselines, recognize patterns and anomalies, assess behaviour and make decisions from observations rather than from a static profile.

The Ontario private-investigator syllabus explicitly requires investigators to deal with discrimination and prejudice in an unbiased manner, understand the consequences of stereotyping, and make quick ethical decisions. Its report-writing module requires objective, factual accounts and specifically recommends exercises that identify missing information and bias in reports.

These are useful local anchors for Argus because they establish a professional expectation without pretending that Ontario’s syllabi are themselves a complete cognitive-science curriculum.

The psychology literature supplies the mechanisms and the limits. The evidence base supports three important design conclusions:

- judgement shortcuts are often useful but can create predictable errors under uncertainty;
- simply knowing the name of a bias does not reliably remove it;
- structured prompts that force consideration of alternatives and contradictory evidence are more defensible training targets than promises of “debiasing.”

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of educational debiasing interventions found a small average improvement on targeted bias tasks (`g = 0.26`) but noted uncertain transfer to real-world decision-making and methodological limitations across the literature. A large 2019 meta-analysis of interventions intended to change implicit measures found that changes were generally small and did not reliably translate into behavioural change. Argus should therefore claim **practice in bias-control procedures**, not transformation of the learner’s underlying cognition.

# 4. The eight highest-value judgement failures

## 4.1 Confirmation bias

### Operational definition

Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek, interpret or remember evidence in ways that favour an existing belief, expectation or working hypothesis.

Nickerson’s major review describes confirmation bias as a broad and recurring feature of human reasoning rather than a narrow laboratory curiosity. For Argus, the important form is **asymmetric evidence handling**: once a learner thinks “this person is suspicious,” ambiguous details begin to be interpreted as support while inconsistent details receive less weight.

### Field failure mode

A learner notices a person repeatedly looking toward a doorway and concludes that the person is preparing to flee or enter somewhere improperly. The learner then notices every further glance but ignores ordinary explanations such as waiting for someone, checking transit information or monitoring a pickup.

### Teachable control

Ask:

- What evidence would I expect if my interpretation were wrong?
- What fact in this scenario most weakens my current hypothesis?
- What alternative explanation fits the observations with fewer assumptions?

The classic “consider the opposite” experiments by Lord, Lepper and Preston found that deliberately considering evidence inconsistent with an initial view can reduce biased social judgement in some settings. Koriat, Lichtenstein and Fischhoff likewise found that generating reasons contradicting a chosen answer improved confidence calibration more than generating additional supporting reasons.

### Argus scoring principle

Do not reward the learner merely for identifying “confirmation bias.” Reward the learner for naming the evidence that was discounted and for specifying a useful disconfirming question or observation.

---

## 4.2 Anchoring

### Operational definition

Anchoring occurs when an initial value, label, first impression or early hypothesis exerts disproportionate influence on later judgement, even after new evidence becomes available.

Tversky and Kahneman identified anchoring and insufficient adjustment as a central judgement heuristic. Later reviews describe anchoring as robust across many decision domains.

### Field failure mode

A supervisor says before a shift that a particular person “causes trouble.” A later observer interprets ambiguous conduct through that label. Or an initial incident report says “attempted theft,” causing later reviewers to interpret neutral actions as supporting theft rather than independently reconstructing events.

### Teachable control

Argus should train the learner to separate:

- what was known before the anchor appeared;
- what the anchor claims;
- what new evidence independently supports;
- what evidence would justify changing the initial assessment.

A useful scenario mechanic is to present an early label, then reveal later facts that should force the learner to revise. Refusing to revise should score poorly even if the original judgement was initially reasonable.

### Important distinction

Anchoring overlaps with confirmation bias but is not identical. Anchoring concerns excessive pull from an initial reference point; confirmation bias concerns selective treatment of evidence in favour of a hypothesis. A scenario can contain both.

---

## 4.3 Availability

### Operational definition

The availability heuristic occurs when perceived frequency, probability or plausibility is influenced by how easily examples come to mind.

Tversky and Kahneman showed that ease of retrieval can be informative but can also be distorted by vividness, recency and repeated exposure.

### Field failure mode

After hearing about several highly publicized transit incidents, a learner begins treating ordinary ambiguous conduct on public transit as more likely to be violent. After a recent workplace theft, the learner gives theft explanations excessive weight in unrelated access-control situations.

### Teachable control

Ask:

- Am I estimating likelihood from evidence in this case or from how memorable a similar event is?
- Has a recent incident made one explanation unusually salient?
- What is the ordinary baseline for this environment?

Argus should not teach that availability is irrational. Retrieval fluency often tracks real frequency. The error occurs when salience substitutes for case-specific evidence or reasonable base-rate context.

---

## 4.4 Stereotyping

### Operational definition

Stereotyping is the use of generalized beliefs about a social group to interpret or predict an individual’s behaviour, especially where those group beliefs substitute for relevant person-specific and situational evidence.

Classic social-cognition research distinguishes automatic stereotype activation from controlled judgement. Devine’s work helped establish that awareness of cultural stereotypes does not require endorsing them and that controlled processing can matter in preventing stereotype-consistent interpretation of ambiguous behaviour.

### Ontario relevance

Ontario’s security-guard and private-investigator training materials explicitly require learners to recognize prejudice, discrimination and stereotyping and to prevent their own biases from affecting professional interactions. This is directly applicable to Argus.

### Field failure mode

The same ambiguous conduct is judged as more threatening, dishonest or non-compliant depending on the person’s race, age, disability, clothing, gender expression, housing status or other identity-linked cue.

### Teachable control

The primary question is:

> **What behaviour would concern me if a different person did exactly the same thing?**

Other prompts:

- Which conclusion depends on demographic or identity information rather than behaviour?
- What person-specific evidence is actually relevant?
- Would changing only the person’s identity change my judgement? If yes, why?

### Safety boundary

Argus should not create simplistic “spot your implicit bias” morality tests. Scenarios should test whether identity characteristics are being used as unsupported evidence. They should also preserve legitimate context: observable behaviour, role, access authorization, known history and situational constraints may be relevant when properly sourced.

---

## 4.5 Fundamental attribution error / correspondence bias

### Operational definition

The fundamental attribution error, often discussed more precisely as correspondence bias, is the tendency to infer stable traits or dispositions from behaviour while giving insufficient weight to situational explanations.

Gilbert and Malone describe correspondence bias as inferring enduring personal dispositions from behaviour that could be explained by the situation in which it occurs.

### Field failure mode

A person answers abruptly and avoids eye contact. The observer concludes “hostile,” “deceptive” or “uncooperative.” Missing context later reveals that the person is hard of hearing, distressed, rushed, unfamiliar with the setting or responding to another immediate demand.

### Teachable control

Ask:

- What situational constraint could produce the same behaviour?
- Is the evidence about a stable trait, or only about conduct in one moment?
- Do I know the person’s baseline?

### Why it matters for threat judgement

Threat assessment guidance consistently emphasizes context. The FBI’s behavioural threat-assessment material warns against “analysis at a glance,” demographic profiling and single-factor conclusions. A person’s behaviour must be interpreted with circumstances, change over time, mitigating information and the totality of available evidence.

---

## 4.6 Premature closure

### Operational definition

Premature closure is stopping the search for reasonable alternatives after reaching an initial satisfactory explanation.

The term is especially well developed in medical decision-making research, where diagnostic error studies have identified premature closure as a recurrent cognitive failure. The underlying pattern transfers cleanly to behavioural judgement: an observer finds one plausible story and stops asking whether another explanation better fits the evidence.

### Field failure mode

A learner sees someone repeatedly trying a secured door, decides “unauthorized entry attempt,” and stops gathering context. Additional information that the person is an employee whose access card has failed is available but never sought.

### Teachable control

Before closing a judgement, require:

- at least one plausible competing explanation;
- one item of missing information that could materially change the conclusion;
- a statement of what would trigger reassessment.

### Relationship to anchoring and confirmation bias

Premature closure is the **stopping error**. Anchoring is excessive dependence on the first frame. Confirmation bias is selective evidence handling after a belief forms. Scenarios should sometimes combine them, but the learner should be able to distinguish the mechanism.

---

## 4.7 Confidence/accuracy confusion

### Operational definition

Confidence is a metacognitive judgement about how likely one believes one’s answer is to be correct. It is not identical to accuracy.

The relationship is real but limited. A 2022 meta-analysis drawing on 213 datasets found a positive across-person confidence-accuracy correlation of about `R = .22`, with meaningful variation across domains. A 2015 meta-analysis of clinical judgement found only a small confidence-accuracy association (`r = .15`) across the included studies.

The practical lesson is neither “confidence means nothing” nor “trust confidence.” It is:

> **confidence should be earned by evidence quality, and confident delivery must not be treated as independent corroboration.**

### Field failure mode

A witness says with certainty that a person intended to steal. The certainty of the witness’s interpretation is treated as if it were additional evidence of intent. Or the learner becomes more confident merely because they have repeated the same interpretation several times.

### Teachable control

Require confidence to be justified by:

- directness of the observations;
- source reliability;
- corroboration;
- specificity of evidence;
- presence or absence of contradictory information;
- amount of missing context.

Scenarios should sometimes reward **lowering confidence without changing the current best hypothesis**. That distinction is important: a hypothesis can remain the leading explanation while becoming less certain.

---

## 4.8 Body-language folklore

### Operational definition

Body-language folklore is the use of simple, purportedly diagnostic nonverbal cues — such as gaze aversion, fidgeting, posture, touching the face or nervousness — as if they reliably reveal deception, intent, dangerousness or hidden personality.

The strongest relevant research comes from deception science. Vrij, Hartwig and Granhag’s Annual Review of Psychology article concludes that nonverbal cues to deceit identified by research are generally faint and unreliable and that people are mediocre lie detectors when relying on behaviour. Hartwig and Bond’s meta-analysis similarly shows serious limits in human lie judgement and cautions against simplistic cue-based inference.

### Field failure mode

A person appears nervous, avoids eye contact and shifts position. The observer concludes that the person is lying or planning wrongdoing.

Those observations may be accurate. The conclusion is not established by them. Nervousness can reflect fear, social discomfort, neurodivergence, language difficulty, authority pressure, pain, fatigue or many other causes.

### Teachable control

Argus should teach:

- describe the behaviour literally;
- do not convert a single nonverbal cue into a diagnostic conclusion;
- seek verbal content, chronology, corroboration and context;
- distinguish “this changed from the person’s baseline” from “this cue means deception.”

### Important nuance

The research does not imply that nonverbal behaviour is useless. Behaviour can matter as one part of a broader pattern, particularly when there is a known baseline or meaningful change. The folklore error is assigning fixed meanings to weak or ambiguous cues.

# 5. A teachable bias-control procedure

Argus should teach one reusable procedure across all eight biases. It should not depend on a catchy acronym.

## Step 1 — Record observations before interpretation

Capture observable facts:

- words spoken;
- actions taken;
- sequence and timing;
- location;
- objects and environmental conditions;
- changes from a known baseline.

Avoid motives, labels and emotional adjectives unless clearly attributed to a speaker.

Weak:

> “The man was acting guilty and casing the entrance.”

Stronger:

> “The man stood across from the entrance for approximately six minutes, looked toward the door several times, used his phone, then walked away.”

The second description preserves evidence for later interpretation.

## Step 2 — Make the interpretation explicit

The learner should state the current hypothesis rather than letting it remain implicit.

Example:

> “One possible interpretation is that he was monitoring access to the entrance.”

This wording matters. A hypothesis can be tested; a hidden assumption tends to be defended.

## Step 3 — Generate at least one competing explanation

Do not demand a long list. One plausible alternative is enough to break the illusion that the first explanation is the only explanation.

Example:

> “He may have been waiting for a pickup or checking whether someone had arrived.”

## Step 4 — Seek evidence that discriminates between explanations

Good information is not merely more information. It should change the relative plausibility of competing hypotheses.

Examples:

- Did he attempt to enter?
- Was he waiting for a known person?
- Did his behaviour change when access conditions changed?
- Is this common in that location and time period?

## Step 5 — Identify missing context

Missing information should be specific enough to matter.

Weak:

> “Need more information.”

Stronger:

> “We do not know whether he had authorized access, whether he was waiting for someone, or whether this pattern is unusual at that entrance.”

## Step 6 — Calibrate confidence

Confidence should track evidence quality rather than emotional vividness or fluency.

Argus can use four labels:

- low confidence;
- moderate confidence;
- high confidence;
- insufficient information to select a leading explanation.

The app should not force false precision with percentages unless a later course explicitly teaches probabilistic calibration.

## Step 7 — Update rather than defend

When new evidence arrives, the learner must be allowed — and often required — to change the judgement.

Changing one’s mind after relevant evidence is not an error. Failing to update is.

# 6. Scenario design

The course should use short authored scenarios with controlled information release. The same base scene can test several distinct biases by changing one detail.

## Scenario type A — Detect biased reasoning

Present an observer’s conclusion and ask which part of the reasoning is weak.

Example:

> A store employee sees a customer repeatedly look toward the exit while browsing. The employee says, “People who keep checking exits are preparing to run with merchandise.”

Strong learner response:

- identifies that the observed behaviour is real but the intent inference is unsupported;
- names premature closure or confirmation risk;
- requests additional behaviour/context rather than treating exit glances as diagnostic.

The exercise should not be scored solely on choosing the bias label.

## Scenario type B — Separate evidence from interpretation

Provide statements such as:

- “She checked the rear door twice.”
- “She was testing security.”
- “She asked whether the building was closing.”
- “She looked nervous.”

Ask the learner to classify each as:

- direct observation;
- attributed statement;
- interpretation;
- subjective characterization requiring support.

This connects directly to `OBSERVATION_VS_INFERENCE.md` without duplicating that course.

## Scenario type C — Seek disconfirming evidence

Give a working hypothesis and multiple possible next questions or observations.

Example hypothesis:

> “The person is attempting unauthorized access.”

Choices might include:

- “Watch whether they try additional doors.”
- “Check whether their access card is malfunctioning or whether they are expected on site.”
- “Look for more signs that they seem nervous.”

The best answer is the one that can meaningfully weaken or strengthen the hypothesis, not merely accumulate confirmatory cues.

## Scenario type D — Identify missing information

Present an apparently vivid but underdetermined scene.

Example:

> A person is standing near a closed office entrance at 22:30 and looking through the glass. No other information is provided.

Possible missing information:

- whether staff commonly work late;
- whether the person has access authorization;
- whether they attempted entry;
- whether anyone inside is expecting them;
- whether the behaviour has persisted or changed.

“Suspicious” should not be accepted as an evidence statement.

## Scenario type E — Revise an initial judgement

Use staged disclosure.

Stage 1:

> A person repeatedly pulls on a locked side door.

Initial judgement may reasonably favour unauthorized access concern.

Stage 2:

> The person then uses an employee badge three times; the reader flashes red.

Stage 3:

> A colleague inside recognizes the person and lets them in.

The learner should reduce or reverse the initial concern as evidence changes. The course should explicitly reward revision.

## Scenario type F — Same behaviour, different person

Hold behaviour constant while varying irrelevant identity cues.

The goal is to test whether the learner’s judgement changes without evidence changing.

This must be handled carefully. The lesson is not “identity can never matter.” The lesson is that protected or demographic traits are not substitutes for behaviour, authorization, role, history or situational evidence.

## Scenario type G — Recent-event availability

Begin with a short contextual prompt:

> Earlier this week, a theft occurred in the same building.

Then present ambiguous behaviour that would ordinarily be common.

Ask:

- Does the recent theft justify changing vigilance?
- Which observations are actually new evidence about this person?
- Has the recent event increased salience more than probability?

The correct answer may be “raise attention while withholding a conclusion.” This is a useful distinction for situational awareness.

## Scenario type H — Body-language folklore

Present a witness or subject who is nervous, avoids eye contact or fidgets.

Ask what can defensibly be concluded.

Best response:

- the nonverbal behaviour can be recorded;
- it may justify further neutral information gathering if relevant;
- it does not establish lying, guilt or dangerous intent.

## Scenario type I — Threat-judgement handoff

Use a safe, non-operational behavioural scenario that contains ambiguous concern.

The learner should identify:

- observable behaviours;
- unsupported assumptions;
- meaningful missing information;
- whether there is enough concern to report or seek appropriate help;
- why reporting a concern does not require certainty that someone is dangerous.

This is the bridge into `THREAT_RECOGNITION_I.md`. It must not teach attack planning, targeting or tactical details.

# 7. Examples of weak and strong reasoning

## Example 1 — Confirmation and body-language folklore

**Scenario:** During an interview, a person avoids eye contact and pauses before answering a question about an incident.

Weak:

> “They are probably lying. Avoiding eye contact and hesitation are deception cues.”

Stronger:

> “They avoided eye contact and paused before answering. Those observations do not establish deception. I would focus on the content and chronology of the account, ask neutral follow-up questions and compare it with independent evidence.”

## Example 2 — Fundamental attribution error

**Scenario:** A commuter answers an employee sharply after being asked to move.

Weak:

> “He is aggressive.”

Stronger:

> “He raised his voice and answered sharply after being asked to move. That behaviour may be confrontational in the moment, but I do not have enough information to infer a stable trait or motive.”

## Example 3 — Anchoring

**Scenario:** An earlier report labels a person a “shoplifting suspect,” but the available video shows only browsing, replacing items and leaving empty-handed.

Weak:

> “The earlier report says shoplifting, so the browsing pattern is probably part of it.”

Stronger:

> “The prior label is an anchor, not independent evidence. On the available video, the observable behaviour does not establish theft. I would reconstruct the sequence from source evidence and identify what the earlier reporter actually observed.”

## Example 4 — Availability

**Scenario:** A recent local assault has received heavy news coverage. The learner sees a stranger pacing on a subway platform.

Weak:

> “Pacing could be pre-assault behaviour, so this is high risk.”

Stronger:

> “Pacing alone is common and ambiguous. The recent assault may make violence easier to imagine, but it does not add case-specific evidence. I would maintain ordinary awareness and reassess if behaviour changes.”

## Example 5 — Premature closure

**Scenario:** A person is trying several keys at a residential building entrance late at night.

Weak:

> “They are trying to break in.”

Stronger:

> “They are trying several keys at a locked entrance. Unauthorized entry is one explanation, but a resident with the wrong key or a malfunctioning lock is also plausible. I would avoid asserting intent from the current evidence.”

## Example 6 — Confidence calibration

**Scenario:** Two witnesses give similar accounts but both were standing together and discussing the event before being interviewed.

Weak:

> “Two confident witnesses independently confirm it.”

Stronger:

> “The accounts agree, but the witnesses may not be independent because they discussed the event together. Agreement raises interest but not as much as two independently obtained accounts would.”

# 8. Assessment model

A pure multiple-choice test would measure vocabulary better than judgement. The course should use a small finite recall component plus scenario application.

## Knowledge component

Recall should cover:

- the eight bias concepts;
- the difference between an observation and an interpretation;
- why a competing hypothesis matters;
- why confidence is not accuracy;
- why no single body-language cue proves deception;
- why bias control means management rather than elimination.

## Scenario rubric

Each scored scenario should evaluate five dimensions, each from `0` to `2`.

### 1. Evidence separation

- `0` — treats interpretation as observed fact.
- `1` — identifies some facts but mixes them with assumptions.
- `2` — clearly separates observations, reports and interpretations.

### 2. Bias recognition

- `0` — misses the relevant judgement failure.
- `1` — identifies a general concern but not how it affects the reasoning.
- `2` — identifies the mechanism and points to the exact reasoning step it distorts.

### 3. Alternative/disconfirmation

- `0` — only seeks confirming evidence.
- `1` — names an alternative but cannot test it.
- `2` — gives a plausible alternative and identifies information that could discriminate between explanations.

### 4. Missing information and context

- `0` — closes judgement despite material gaps.
- `1` — says more information is needed without specificity.
- `2` — identifies specific missing facts that would materially change confidence or action.

### 5. Updating and calibration

- `0` — defends the initial judgement despite contradictory evidence.
- `1` — changes the conclusion but not the confidence appropriately, or vice versa.
- `2` — revises conclusion and/or confidence in proportion to the new evidence.

Maximum: `10` points per scenario.

## Automatic safety penalties

A response should not receive a high score if it:

- treats race, religion, disability, gender, sexual orientation or other demographic characteristics as evidence of dangerousness;
- diagnoses mental illness from appearance or behaviour;
- claims deception from a single body-language cue;
- invents facts not in the scenario;
- treats uncertainty as proof of innocence or proof of threat;
- demands certainty before appropriate reporting of a genuine safety concern.

## Completion standard

Recommended initial standard:

- at least `80%` on finite recall;
- at least `8/10` average across the final scenario set;
- no final scenario with a score below `6/10`;
- successful revision on at least two staged-evidence scenarios where the correct response requires changing the initial judgement or confidence.

The exact thresholds can be tuned during implementation, but the final requirement should ensure that the learner can **update**, not merely name biases.

# 9. Recommended lesson progression

## Lesson 1 — Evidence before explanation

Teach:

- observation versus interpretation;
- confidence as separate from evidence;
- the seven-step judgement sequence.

Practice:

- short fact/inference classifications;
- confidence labels;
- “what is missing?” prompts.

## Lesson 2 — First impressions and selective evidence

Teach:

- anchoring;
- confirmation bias;
- premature closure.

Practice:

- staged scenarios;
- competing hypotheses;
- disconfirming questions.

## Lesson 3 — Salience and social interpretation

Teach:

- availability;
- stereotyping;
- fundamental attribution error.

Practice:

- same-behaviour/different-person scenarios;
- recent-event scenarios;
- situational alternative generation.

## Lesson 4 — Confidence and body-language limits

Teach:

- confidence/accuracy distinction;
- body-language folklore;
- deception-detection limits.

Practice:

- witness confidence;
- nervousness/eye-contact scenarios;
- evidence-versus-impression comparisons.

## Lesson 5 — Behavioural judgement under uncertainty

Integrate all eight biases in realistic street, workplace, transit, consulting and incident-report scenarios.

The learner should not be told which bias is present.

## Lesson 6 — Update drill

Use multi-stage scenarios where new evidence changes the best interpretation.

The learner must:

- record the initial judgement;
- assign confidence;
- receive new information;
- decide whether to hold, weaken, strengthen or replace the judgement;
- explain which evidence caused the update.

This lesson should carry the most weight in completion.

# 10. Relationship to adjacent Argus courses

## Observation versus inference

`OBSERVATION_VS_INFERENCE.md` should teach the foundational distinction in depth. This course should assume or briefly reinforce it, then show how biases distort the move from observation to interpretation.

## Situational Awareness I

`SITUATIONAL_AWARENESS_I.md` should teach baseline, anomaly and reassessment. Bias Control should prevent “anomaly” from becoming a synonym for “threat” and should teach why vivid recent events and stereotypes can distort anomaly interpretation.

## Threat Recognition I

`THREAT_RECOGNITION_I.md` should teach concern, context, escalation and appropriate reporting. Bias Control supplies the discipline needed to avoid demographic profiling, checklist thinking, single-cue inference and overconfidence.

Professional threat-assessment guidance from the FBI and U.S. Secret Service is consistent on the core point: there is no demographic profile of a targeted-violence offender, no single behaviour is predictive, and behaviour must be interpreted in context and in the totality of circumstances. Argus should import that epistemic discipline without pretending that a short course trains a threat assessor.

## Interviewing I

Bias Control should prepare the learner to avoid leading questions, confirmation-seeking and premature credibility judgements. Interviewing should then teach how to gather information without unnecessarily contaminating it.

# 11. Misconceptions the course should explicitly correct

- **“Knowing my biases removes them.”** No. Awareness may help, but educational effects are modest and transfer is uncertain.
- **“Bias means every intuitive judgement is wrong.”** No. Heuristics are efficient and often useful; bias control matters when stakes, ambiguity or uncertainty justify a more explicit check.
- **“Being objective means having no interpretation.”** No. Judgement requires interpretation. The goal is to distinguish interpretation from evidence and make it revisable.
- **“If I can imagine an innocent explanation, there is no concern.”** No. Competing explanations manage confidence; they do not erase risk or the need to report concerning behaviour.
- **“If I can imagine a dangerous explanation, the person is dangerous.”** No. Plausibility is not proof, and vividness is not probability.
- **“Body language reveals the truth if you know what to look for.”** Not reliably. Nonverbal behaviour is often ambiguous and weakly diagnostic.
- **“A confident witness is an accurate witness.”** Confidence can contain information, but it is not equivalent to accuracy and must be evaluated with acquisition conditions and corroboration.
- **“Changing my mind shows poor judgement.”** Updating after relevant evidence is a core mark of good judgement.
- **“Bias control means treating every possibility as equally likely.”** No. Evidence should change relative plausibility. The course teaches disciplined weighting, not false equivalence.
- **“Uncertainty means do nothing.”** No. One can be uncertain about intent while still taking proportionate safety, reporting or information-gathering action.

---

# 12. Safety and editorial boundaries

This course should be useful without becoming amateur profiling or amateur diagnosis.

Do:

- teach observable behaviour;
- teach contextual interpretation;
- teach uncertainty and revision;
- teach evidence-seeking and corroboration;
- teach anti-stereotyping safeguards;
- teach appropriate reporting when genuine concern exists;
- teach that urgent danger may require immediate emergency action.

Do not:

- teach demographic profiles of offenders;
- teach personality “tells” for violence;
- teach mental-health diagnosis from appearance;
- teach deterministic body-language claims;
- teach attack tactics, operational targeting or adversarial evasion;
- imply that the learner can formally assess dangerousness;
- score the learner for confidently guessing intent from thin evidence.

The course’s tone should be epistemic rather than moralizing: **what does the evidence support, what does it not support, and what would make the judgement better?**

# 14. Proposed Argus treatment

Implement later as a **six-lesson practical psychology course** with a small finite-recall layer and a larger authored-scenario layer.

The distinctive mechanic should be **forced revision**: several scenarios must reveal new information after the learner commits to an initial judgement and confidence level. This is the most direct way to test whether the learner can resist anchoring, confirmation and premature closure in practice.

The course should repeatedly reward:

- precise observations;
- explicit uncertainty;
- specific missing information;
- plausible alternative explanations;
- disconfirming evidence-seeking;
- confidence adjustment;
- justified changes of mind.

It should never reward certainty for its own sake.

## Exact completion claim

> **Completed Bias Control in Behavioural Judgement: can identify common bias patterns in authored scenarios, separate evidence from interpretation, seek disconfirming information, identify material unknowns, calibrate confidence and revise an initial judgement when new evidence warrants it.**
